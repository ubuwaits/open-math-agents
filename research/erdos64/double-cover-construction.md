# How short a cycle can one recover from a bipartite double cover?

Chad Mazzola ([@ubuwaits](https://github.com/ubuwaits)), using Codex.

In the bipartite double cover of a graph $G$, the vertices are pairs $(v,i)$ with $i=0,1$. An edge joins $(u,i)$ to $(v,1-i)$ whenever $uv$ is an edge of $G$.

## What an upstairs cycle guarantees

If the double cover contains a simple cycle of length $n$, its projection either is a simple cycle of length $n$ or contains a simple odd cycle of length at most $n/2$. To see this, if a projected vertex occurs twice, choose a shortest positive arc between two occurrences of the same vertex on the cyclic word. Its length is at most half the total, since the complementary arc was also a candidate. Minimality makes the arc a simple cycle downstairs. Its endpoints lie on different sheets upstairs, so its length is odd. For a power of two $n\ge 4$, this odd length is strictly less than $n/2$. Lean checks the statements `cycle_or_short_odd` and `power_cycle_or_short_odd`. [Lean proof](Erdos64DoubleCover.lean)

## The odd-length bound is sharp even for bridgeless cubic graphs

**Theorem.** For every power of two $n\ge 8$ and every positive integer $R$, there is a finite connected simple bridgeless cubic graph $G$ with all three properties below.

1. Its bipartite double cover has a simple $n$-cycle.
2. Its shortest odd cycle has length $n/2-1$.
3. Every simple cycle of $G$ whose length is a power of two has length greater than $R$.

**The core.** Put $m=n/2$. Take two vertices $a,b$ joined by three internally disjoint paths of lengths $1,m-2,m$. Call this graph $T$. Its three simple cycles have lengths $m-1,m+1,2m-2$. The first two are odd, and the last is twice an odd integer greater than one. The two endpoints have degree three. The $2m-4$ internal vertices have degree two and serve as attachment points.

In the double cover, start over $a$, use the single edge to $b$, return to $a$ along the path of length $m-2$, use the single edge again, then return along the path of length $m$. This closes after $2m=n$ steps. Each of $a,b$ is visited on opposite sheets on its two occurrences, because the first lap has odd length $m-1$. Every internal vertex occurs only once. The lift is a simple cycle.

**The finite completion.** Choose a finite connected simple $(2m-4)$-regular graph $K$ with girth greater than $\max(R,n)$. A finite construction is given below. Replace each vertex of $K$ by a copy of $T$, assign its incident edges bijectively to its attachment points, and add one edge between the assigned attachment points for each edge of $K$. The resulting graph $G$ is finite, simple, connected, and cubic.

Each edge inside a copy of $T$ belongs to one of its three cycles. Each edge of $K$ lies on a cycle because $K$ is connected and even regular. Indeed, the degree sum over either side of a bridge cut would be odd. Lift a simple cycle of $K$ by joining its two attachment points in each visited copy with a simple internal path. The copies are distinct, so the lift is simple. This puts every edge between copies on a cycle as well, proving bridgelessness.

Any simple cycle in $G$ that leaves a copy projects to a nonempty closed trail in $K$ after its internal steps are erased. Each base edge has just one connecting edge, so the trail has no repeated edge. A nonempty closed trail contains a simple cycle. It therefore uses at least $\operatorname{girth}(K)$ connecting edges. Each consecutive pair of connecting edges on the original cycle is separated by at least one internal edge, since every attachment point has just one edge to another copy. The original cycle has length at least $2\operatorname{girth}(K)$. All cycles of length at most $\max(R,n)$ thus occur inside a core, where the three lengths have already been determined. This proves the theorem.

**Existence of the regular base.** For degree $d\ge 2$ and integer $g\ge 2$, take the finite set of words of length at most $g$ on $d$ letters with no consecutive equal letters, including the empty word. Each letter $s$ acts by an involution. Delete the last letter if it is $s$, append $s$ if there is room and the word does not end in $s$, and fix the word otherwise. Take the Cayley graph of the finite permutation group these involutions generate. The generators are nonidentity and distinct by their action on the empty word, so this is a finite connected simple $d$-regular graph. Starting at the empty word, any nonempty word of length at most $g$ without consecutive equal letters reaches that very word when its letters act successively. Its product cannot be the identity. Hence the Cayley graph has girth greater than $g$.

## An explicit example on 7,920 vertices

For $n=8$, use the Cayley graph generated by the two matrices with diagonals $1,1$ and single off-diagonal entry $2$, one upper triangular and one lower triangular, and their inverses, over the field of eleven elements. The generated group has 1,320 elements and the graph has girth 9. The replacement graph contains a triangle and a 64-cycle, has no 4-, 8-, or 16-cycle, and its double has an 8-cycle. The standard-library program checks these claims, including a direct search for the three excluded lengths from all six representative starting vertices. [Reproduce the example](verify_double_cover_example.py)

## Exoo’s 78-vertex example

Geoffrey Exoo constructed a 78-vertex graph with no cycle of length 4, 8, or 16. Daniel Garcia reconstructed it and published the graph in graph6 format. In the archive’s zero-based numbering, the triangles on vertices $1,3,5$ and $2,4,6$ are joined by edge $3,4$. Consequently the alternating-sheet lift of the closed sequence $3,1,5,3,4,2,6,4$ is a simple octagon. The verifier checks those edges, verifies a 32-cycle downstairs, and exhaustively excludes lengths 4, 8, and 16. To reproduce this part, download the archive’s `supplement.zip` and run `python3 verify_double_cover_example.py --exoo-supplement supplement.zip`. [Exoo’s construction](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.5636) · [Garcia’s paper, Sections 3 and 6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04686) · [Published graph data](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22180583)

Vertex replacement to exclude short power-of-two cycles goes back to Exoo. Garcia describes the possible lengths after replacement on a cubic base. Our construction chooses a core with a prescribed lifted cycle and odd girth, and uses a regular base with as many incident edges as the core has attachment points. The projected closed trail may revisit base vertices. [Garcia’s window lemma and references](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04686)

The finite-completion theorem above has a written proof. The projection bound has a Lean proof. Applied to $n=8$ and increasing $R$, the construction rules out any fixed upper bound on a downstairs power-of-two length inferred from a single upstairs 8-cycle. The Erdős–Gyárfás conjecture allows arbitrarily long cycles and remains open. [Original conjecture](https://www.erdosproblems.com/64)
