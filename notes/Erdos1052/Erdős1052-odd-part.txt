What the fixed-core reduction bounds about the odd part of the exponent

Let n be unitary perfect. Write n=2^a c ∏(q∈S)q, where the odd powerful
core c contains the full odd prime powers with exponent at least two, and
S consists of the other odd primes. Write a=h b, h=2^v, b odd. Let Ω count
prime factors with multiplicity, with Ω(1)=0. All results below also apply
to a support passing the arithmetic test in R2.H2.

For a nonempty set P of prime divisors of c with Σ(p∈P)1/p<1/4096, the
earlier result gives a finite explicit set of v and h≥16. The empty
support P corresponds to c=1, already classified by Graham.

For each permitted v define

    C = oddpart(σ*(c)),   g = gcd(c,C(2^h+1)),   K = Ω(c/g).

The new conclusions are

    v₃(b) ≤ K,
    Ω(b) ≤ (p−1)(K+2) + m(K+1),                                (1)

where p is any prime divisor of h+1, q any prime divisor of hp+1, and
m is the multiplicative order of p modulo q. In particular p and q
are distinct odd primes. Since p≤h+1 and m≤q−1≤hp, an upper bound
using only h and K is h((h+2)(K+1)+1). These conclusions use h≥16 and the unitary
balance. Sparsity makes the set of h finite. Formula (1) gives exponent
finiteness if the primes allowed in b belong to any specified finite set.
When K=0, b=1 is the only possibility, without any restriction on its
prime factors. The proof of this last assertion is at the end of section 2.
The general size of the largest prime divisor of b remains unresolved.

Sections 9–13 of Erdős1052-large-prime-branches.txt sharpen these
bounds to Ω(b)≤K and 2^ω(b)≤K+2. They also rule out every sparse
prime-power core with base at most 10¹² apart from two specified
families. The bounds below give the earlier argument.

1. Splitting the odd-part balance

Put d(q)=oddpart(q+1). The exact identities for a positive exponent give

    C(2^a+1) ∏(q∈S)d(q) = c ∏(q∈S)q,
    ∏(q∈S)(1+1/q) < 2.                                         (2)

Take any chain s₀=h, sᵢ₊₁=sᵢ fᵢ, where every fᵢ≥3 is odd and the last
sᵢ divides a with odd quotient. Each integer

    Aᵢ=(2^(sᵢ fᵢ)+1)/(2^sᵢ+1)

can be split off from C(2^a+1). The product of the Aᵢ telescopes and the
remaining factor is divisible by C(2^h+1).

Process S in decreasing order, assigning each q to a running factor
that it divides, and replacing that factor H by (H/q)d(q). There is
always such a factor by (2) since q divides none of the remaining d(r)
with r≤q. Each initial factor A ends with an odd integer D and its own
disjoint set T of assigned primes, with

    A ∏(q∈T)d(q)=D ∏(q∈T)q.                                     (3)

The final integers D have product c. Processing divides only by primes
outside c, so the factor that began as a multiple of C(2^h+1) retains
at least g. Consequently at most K of the factors Aᵢ can have D>1.
This reasoning permits common divisors between initial factors.

2. When a factor must use a prime from the core

Consider A=(Y^f+1)/(Y+1), Y=2^s, with s even, s≥16 and f odd, f≥3.
Suppose D=1 in (3). Let B=∏(q∈T)(1+1/q), and E=Σ(q∈T)v₂(q+1).
Then AB=2^E and 1≤B<2. If L=Y^(f−1), then L/2<A<L. The only power
of two in [A,2A) is therefore L. Thus

    B=L/A=(1+1/Y)/(1+1/Y^f).                                    (4)

Every q∈T exceeds Y since B<1+1/Y. If d(q)>1 choose a prime r|d(q).
By (3), r∈T and r<q. A selected r can occur only once across all
q because ∏(q∈T)q is squarefree. The chosen edges split T into paths
starting at primes with d(q)=1, so each starting prime is 2^j−1.
Along an edge r→q, q≥2r−1. With w(r)=(r+1)/(r−1), one has
(1+1/r)w(2r−1)=w(r), so the product along each path is at most w(r).

If 2^(s+1)−1 is composite, no path starts at exponent s+1. Exponent
s+2 is even and exceeds 2, so it cannot give a prime either. Thus
j≥s+3 for every start. There is at most one start per exponent, and
Σ(j≥s+3)2^(1−j)=1/(2Y). The elementary bound ∏(1−u)≥1−Σu gives

    B ≤ ∏(starts j)(1−2^(1−j))⁻¹ ≤ 2Y/(2Y−1).

But (4) gives B≥Y²/(Y²−Y+1)>2Y/(2Y−1) for Y>2. This proves that
2^(s+1)−1 composite forces D>1, for every odd multiplier f≥3.
In particular this holds whenever s+1 is composite.

At the *first* step, where s=h=2^v≥16, the same conclusion D>1
holds for every odd f≥3 even when r=2Y−1 is prime. Here is the extra
estimate. If v is odd, then 3 properly divides h+1. If v is 2 mod 4,
then 5 properly divides h+1. Thus in the remaining case 4|v, so
h≡6 mod 10. Now 2r−1=2^(h+2)−3 is divisible by 11 because
2^8≡3 mod 11. The integer 4r−1 is divisible by 3 since r≡1 mod 3.
A successor equal to 6r−1 would put 3 into d(6r−1), impossible as
all primes of T exceed Y. Therefore every successor on the path
starting at r is at least 8r−1. Its entire path contributes at most
(1+1/r)w(8r−1). This also bounds its contribution if the path is absent.

All other starting exponents are odd and at least h+3. Their total
weight loss Σ 2^(1−j) is at most the geometric sum 1/(3Y), so (4)
would imply

    Y²/(Y²−Y+1) ≤ B ≤ (8Y/(8Y−5))(3Y/(3Y−1)).

The right endpoint is strictly below the left one for Y>19, as is seen
by multiplying out. This contradiction proves the first-step claim.
In particular, if c divides C(2^h+1), every available core prime has
already been used and b=1. The same conclusion holds if the forced
prime factors of C(2^h+1) and their successive demands exhaust c.

3. The stronger result for a multiplier of three

For f=3, D>1 for every s≥16 under consideration, including those with
2^(s+1)−1 prime. Indeed A=Y²−Y+1. The least prime of T must be a path
start, so it is at least 2Y−1. Every divisor of ∏(q∈T)q strictly between
1 and (2Y−1)² must itself be a prime in T. Apply this first to A, using
(3), then downward to Qᵢ=1+2^i(Y−1), starting at Q_s=A. For i≥2,
d(Qᵢ)=Qᵢ₋₁, which again divides ∏(q∈T)q by (3). Thus Q₁,...,Q_s
would all be prime.

Write s=u d with u a power of two and d odd. If d≥3, 2^u+1 properly
divides Q_(2u−1), since Y≡−1 and 2^(2u)≡1 modulo 2^u+1. The index
lies in [1,s]. If d=1 write s=2^j. For odd j, s≡2 mod 3 and 7 properly
divides Q₁=2^(s+1)−1. For even j, s≡4 mod 12 and 2^s≡3 mod 13,
so 13 properly divides Q₅. All the indicated divisors are proper
since s≥16. Hence (3) cannot hold with D=1. Taking the chain with
v₃(b) consecutive multipliers of three proves the first bound in (1).

4. Bounding the total number of odd prime factors

Remove the copies of p from the multiset of prime factors of b. Any
p−1 remaining entries contain a nonempty subproduct equal to 1 modulo
p. Indeed their p partial products, including the empty product, take
only p−1 nonzero residue values. Two coincide. Repeatedly extract such
blocks, each of size at most p−1.

If there were (p−1)(K+2) entries or more, we could take K+2 blocks.
Use their products as successive odd multipliers. After the first block
each prefix exponent s satisfies s≡h≡−1 mod p and s+1>p, so s+1 is
composite. The following K+1 blocks all require D>1 by section 2,
contradicting section 1. Therefore Ω(b/p^v_p(b))≤(p−1)(K+2)−1.

For the copies of p use the separate chain with prefix exponents hp^i,
0≤i<v_p(b), and next multiplier p. At i=1+jm for j≥1, hp^i+1
is divisible by q since p^m≡1 mod q. It exceeds q. Each such index
requires D>1. If v_p(b)≥2+(K+1)m then j=1,...,K+1 all occur.
Hence v_p(b)≤1+(K+1)m. Adding these two estimates proves (1).

5. Why the earlier residue restriction alone cannot bound b

Take c=65537², h=16 and b=3^t for any t≥0. The only prime equal to
2 mod 3 dividing 2^(16·3^t)+1 is 65537, with multiplicity one. Indeed
2^16+1=65537, and each successive quotient Y²−Y+1 has Y≡1 mod 3.
It is coprime to Y+1 since their gcd divides 3 and 3∤Y+1. For every
prime r dividing the quotient, Y has order exactly 6 modulo r, so
r≡1 mod 3. These exponents meet the earlier residue and core-parity
conditions for arbitrarily large t. They are not examples of unitary
perfect numbers. In fact, 65537²+1 is divisible by 5; using the
previous sparse-core bound with seed 5 excludes this particular core.
The stronger bound on v₃(b) uses the unitary balance, not just residues.
For comparison, c=65537^128 has the same seed and also satisfies the
earlier order and parity restrictions. At h=16 the formulas give K=127
and Ω(b)≤2320 using p=17, q=3, m=2. Thus among candidates a=16·3^t
only 0≤t≤127 survive this particular estimate. Section 6 of
Erdős1052-large-prime-branches.txt subsequently excludes this core for
every exponent, by using a divisor of 65537^128+1.

6. Another restriction on the core

At a nonempty sparse core none of 3,5,17,257 divides σ*(c). To see this,
if any such prime m divides σ*(c), it divides its odd part and supplies
the seed m in the previous sparse-core argument. All four primes are
below 4096 and survive the deletion in that argument. The exact core-1
gap at m gives retained abundancy <1024/513. Multiplying by the
correction E=1/((1−1/4096²)(1−3/4095)) and the factor 1+2^(−a)
still gives less than 2 because a≥16.

The same calculation also forces 3, 5 and 7 into the simple support.
Apply the deletion argument with seed 1. If one of these primes were
absent, it would be absent from the retained support as well. With the
one indicated prime forbidden, the exact gap test at 1024/513 succeeds
in 1, 25 and 3 nodes, respectively, and the largest cutoffs are 3, 398
and 14. In particular 5+1 supplies the sole available copy of 3.
Every other simple prime different from 3 is therefore 1 modulo 3.
This also forces every prime equal to 2 modulo 3 dividing σ*(c) into
the repeated core, since 5 itself cannot divide σ*(c).

In particular, if 65537∈P and e=v₆₅₅₃₇(c), then 16|e. Indeed 65537
is 2 modulo each of F₀=3, F₁=5, F₂=17, F₃=257. If v₂(e)=j<4 then
Fⱼ divides 2^e+1 and hence divides 65537^e+1, a factor of σ*(c).
Consequently the earlier exceptional case v₂(a)=4 requires 65537^16|c.

There are further exclusions at this base. The following prime divisors
are verified by trial division and modular exponentiation:

       j          r          65537^(2^j) modulo r
       4       4365089                −1
       5          1409                −1
       6           641                −1

If v₂(e)=j then r|65537^e+1. At j=4, if r were simple then
9|4365090 would require two copies of 3 in n, contrary to 3∉P.
Thus 4365089 must be a second repeated prime. At j=5, the forced
simple prime 1409 has 3·5|1410. The forced simple prime 5 contributes
another 3 from 5+1, which is impossible. At j=6, 641 forces the
simple prime 107 from 642, and 9|108 gives the same contradiction.
All these forcing steps follow directly from (2).

In particular the fixed core c=65537^e has no admissible exponent
whenever 128∤e. This includes c=65537^16, even though its reciprocal
condition and the original restriction on v₂(a) allow v₂(a)=4.
The first numerical divisor was located in FactorDB's factored entry
for (65537^16+1)/2 and independently verified.
https://factordb.com/index.php?id=1100000000212021432

7. Consequences and the missing bound

For fixed c take the maximum of the right side of (1) over its finite
list of permissible v. Call it H. If every prime factor of b is ≤L,
then b≤L^H. Thus in any hypothetical infinite sequence at a fixed
sparse core, the largest prime divisor of b must be unbounded. In
particular b restricted to powers of three gives at most K+1 exponents
for each v. If K=0 for every permitted v, the entire set of admissible
exponents is contained in the finite set of permitted values of 2^v.

For a prime multiplier ℓ, section 2 requires the quotient
(2^(hℓ)+1)/(2^h+1) to use at least one prime from c. The lower bound
of one is independent of ℓ. When a core factor is still available, the
argument supplies no upper bound for ℓ. Finiteness for every fixed
sparse core and a uniform bound on c both remain open in this argument.

The function in erdos1052_odd_part.py computes the bounds for a
specified factored core and checks the arithmetic in section 6. The
single-file Lean proof verifies both bounds in (1), the smooth-exponent
finiteness theorem, and the exclusion when e=16(2k+1). The final
first-step estimate in section 2 uses the written proof. All seven
audited Lean declarations use only propext, Classical.choice and
Quot.sound when checked together with the accepted reduction R2.
