Sparse odd cores in the unitary perfect number problem

Let n be unitary perfect and write n=2^a c ∏_(q∈S) q. The distinct odd
primes in S occur to exponent one. The core c contains the full powers
of the odd primes that occur at least twice. Write P for its prime
divisors, s=Σ_(p∈P)1/p and β(S)=∏_(q∈S)(1+1/q).

Theorem 1. Every nonempty core satisfies s>2^−38. If s<1/4096, the
Fermat-order restriction and interval certificates also force at least
two distinct core primes.
There is no unitary perfect number with core p^e for any prime p>4096
and any e≥2. Both e and a range over all exponents.

Theorem 2. For a sparse core (s<1/4096) with exactly two prime bases,
either v₂(a)=4 or v₂(a)≥20. The former requires 65537 and a second
base below 2^78. Several entire pairs of bases are excluded uniformly
in both core exponents and a.

Theorem 3. For every nonempty sparse core its least prime divisor is
less than exp(2^56), independently of the number of core bases. The
separate bound min(P)<2^38 |P| follows directly from
Theorem 1. Section 6 proves the absolute bound.

Theorem 4. Fix a permitted h and cancel the fixed odd seeds C and
F=2^h+1. Let D be the remaining divisor of c. Every odd multiplier b
satisfies D ≥ (2^24+1)^Ω(b). In particular D≤2^24 forces a=h.
Sections 8 and 9 give the compulsory small prime groups and the exact
finite certificate. They also bound a for many arbitrarily large D.

Theorem 5. At any fixed sparse core, exponent finiteness is equivalent
to a uniform bound on the number of simple primes congruent to 31
modulo 32. Given such a bound, the proof in section 10 computes an
upper bound for b. Whether sparsity alone bounds this count is open.

Theorem 6. If the remaining divisor D at h is a prime one unit from
a power of two, of any size, then a=h. A second sufficient condition
for arbitrary D is that |D/2^k−1|≤2^(−h)/9 for some integer k and
that the sum of 1/p over its distinct prime divisors is at most
2^(−h)/9. Sections 12, 13 and 22 give the proofs.

Theorem 7. For each fixed core, (7) bounds the whole odd multiplier in
terms of the number of odd shift valuations outside two freely chosen
values, one in each odd class modulo four. The same bound works even
when the two values vary with the proposed completion.

Theorem 8. No completion at h has residual D equal to a squared
Mersenne prime, or a nonempty product of Fermat primes each occurring
at most twice. Section 15 proves the exclusions for arbitrary sizes.
Section 16 gives a numerical bound using only D and h in further cases.

Theorem 9. The reciprocal sum over core bases other than 65537,
2^31−1 and 2^61−1 exceeds 2^−76. In particular no sparse core consists
entirely of Fermat primes, even with unrestricted multiplicities.

Theorem 10. Suppose ℓ|b is a sufficiently large prime and the quotient
(2^(hℓ)+1)/(2^h+1) has exactly two prime factors. A shift on one of
their descending paths must have multiple odd prime factors. The split
occurs within 48 binary positions at h=16 and within 2h+34 positions
at all larger permitted h. Section 18 gives the size threshold.

Theorem 11. For fixed h there are fourteen explicit intervals in one
octave, of combined base-two logarithmic length less than 1/100.
When the normalized value of (2^h+1)D misses them, that height admits
no completion. A test of logarithmic length less than 1/97 works at
every allowed h simultaneously using D alone. Section 22 proves
the exclusion using the full unitary balance.

Theorem 12. Suppose D consists of Mersenne primes and its weighted sum
Σ e_p/(p+1) is 2^(−m), m≥0. A nontrivial odd multiplier requires
m=14, h≥64 and at least four distinct bases. These include 131071,
524287 and at least one of 2^31−1 and 2^61−1. The remaining bases
beyond 131071 and 524287 contribute at least 4096 factors counted with
multiplicity. Sections 20 and 23 give the proof and interval certificates.

Theorem 13. At any fixed odd powerful core, sparse or dense, exponent
finiteness is equivalent to a bound on the number of simple primes
31 modulo 32. Section 21 gives an explicit bound from this count and
allows two jump sizes chosen separately at each completion.

Theorem 14. Under Theorem 12, the reciprocal sum of core primes that
are neither Mersenne nor in {65537,137089,274177,67280421310721}
exceeds 2^−16. In particular a core made entirely of Mersenne and
Fermat primes cannot have a positive dyadic Mersenne residual at a
realized height. Section 24 uses the complement of the variable group.

Theorem 15. Put P₀={8191,65537,131071,524287,2^31−1,2^61−1}.
In any sparse completion with Σ_(p|c,p∉P₀)1/p≤2^−76, the simple
prime 607 must occur. Either 8191 divides c, or both 65537 and 131071
divide c. In the latter case, if 8191 is absent, c cannot contain both
524287 and 2^31−1. This applies in particular when all core primes
are Mersenne or Fermat, for arbitrary core exponents. For instance
there is no completion at c=65537^u·524287^v for any u,v≥2.

Theorem 16. At a sparse core, no nonempty residual divisor made entirely
of Mersenne primes to exponents one or two can support a completion.
There is no restriction on the number or sizes of its prime factors.
Each distinct odd prime dividing a requires its own non-Mersenne core
factor or three copies of one Mersenne prime in the residual divisor.
Section 26 proves the resulting counting inequality.

Theorem 17. If the quotient (2^(hℓ)+1)/(2^h+1) for a sufficiently
large prime ℓ|b has three prime factors, a descending shift splits
at every permitted height h. For h≥256 a split occurs within 2h+40
binary positions. Section 27 gives the finite uniform certificate.

Theorem 18. Suppose a nonempty residual divisor is cubefree and all its
prime factors are one unit from a power of two. A completion requires
both a Fermat prime 2^(2^j)+1 with j≥33 and a Mersenne prime 2^k−1
with k≥2^32−1. Also h≥2^32. Each first-layer group separately needs
both types of prime. Section 28 gives the proof.

1. Input from the order and small-prime arguments

We use the following earlier sparse-core conclusions. All sparse
solutions have a≥48, and either 64 divides a or v₂(a)=4 and 65537∈P.
Writing h=2^v₂(a), the Fermat number 2^h+1 has a prime divisor p∈P
equal to 2 modulo 3, and the multiplicative order of 2 modulo this p
is exactly 2h. Every simple prime except 3,5 is 1 modulo 3. The set
S contains 3,5,7 and one of the supplier pairs

     (19,223), (19,433), (79,13), (79,223).

The first member supplies 5 in its shift, the second supplies 7.
For s≤2^−14 the only pair is (19,223). These conclusions and their
exact interval checks are in Erdős1052-core-625.txt §5,
Erdős1052-odd-part.txt §6 and Erdős1052-prime-square.txt §§5,8.

For q odd let d(q) be the odd part of q+1. The odd-part balance implies

     ∏_(q∈S) d(q) | c ∏_(q∈S) q.                                  (1)

For any finite set of allowed core bases P, remove all their factors
from each d(q), obtaining d_P(q). Then ∏d_P(q) divides ∏q. We allow
any multiplicity of those removed bases. For a prefix of S in
increasing order, record the unused ordinary primes in a set U.
Initially U is empty. The next prime q satisfies

     q+1 = 2^j b d,     j≥1, b supported on P, d | ∏_(r∈U)r.

Indeed any ordinary prime in d(q) is smaller than q and has only one
available copy in (1). On selecting q, consume the primes of d and
insert q in U. The exact partial product β is updated by (q+1)/q.

2. A finite interval certificate with no size cutoff on S

Draw an edge r→q when r is an ordinary predecessor of q in its shifted
factor. There is at most one outgoing edge per r by (1). Choose one
ordinary predecessor whenever any exists. This partitions the vertices
into disjoint increasing paths. When all remaining primes exceed y,
at most |U| paths starting in the remainder can use a predecessor in U.
All other paths start at primes q with q+1=2^j b and b supported on P.

An edge r→q has q≥2r−1. Put w(r)=(r+1)/(r−1). The identity
(1+1/r)w(2r−1)=w(r) shows that a whole finite path starting at r
contributes at most w(r). Since 1−1/w(r)=2/(r+1), subadditivity of
1−1/B under products B≥1 gives the following reciprocal loss bound for
the entire remaining product H.

    1−1/H ≤ 2|U|/(y+1) + L(P,y+1),
    L(P,x) = Σ_(j≥1, b supported on P, 2^j b>x) 2/(2^j b).            (2)

L is a rational number even though the sum has infinitely many terms.
For P empty it is 4/2^max(1,bit_length(x)). To add a base p, sum
p^−i L(P\{p},floor(x/p^i)) until p^i>x, then sum the remaining
geometric progression. The tail for powers starting at p^k>x is
2 p^−k ∏_(r∈P)(1−1/r)^−1.

Suppose the required interval is [l,u) and the current partial value
is N/D. If N/D≥u the branch closes. If a compulsory prime is still
missing, the next prime cannot exceed the least missing one.
Otherwise select a ceiling y such that the right side in (2) is
strictly less than 1−N/(Dl). Every continuation with all primes above
y, including the empty continuation, is thereby excluded. Branch only
on the finite list q=2^j b d−1 between the previous prime and y.
If a resource limit is reached or a qualifying partial value is found,
the verifier reports an inconclusive result. In particular a ceiling
never limits the primes permitted in a solution.

The script erdos1052_spectrum.py implements these rules with integers
and rational numbers. It rejects a candidate for compositeness only
by trial divisibility or a witness to Fermat's little theorem.
A pseudoprime surviving these tests adds branches. The powers of the
permitted bases are unbounded in (2). This is what permits a single
finite test to cover infinitely many core exponents.

An optional refinement chooses a predecessor in the suffix whenever
possible. Each path start then uses only ordinary primes of U, if any.
List all possible starts up to a farther ceiling. Charge each start
with an ordinary factor to its first used token. Distinct starts
must use distinct charged tokens. Use the largest individual charge for
each token, allowing 2/(farther ceiling+1) above the range, and sum
the separate charges for starts with no ordinary factor, with the smooth
tail in (2) beyond that ceiling. This bounds the possible loss for
each suffix of the candidate list. With sharpen_tail=True the neutral
and main 65537 gap checks take 112 and 1505 nodes, respectively.

3. A quantitative restriction on the entire repeated core

With P empty and 3,5,7,19,223 compulsory, the interval [2−2^−35,2)
is excluded in 362 nodes. Here the largest local ceiling is
1,139,725,879,647. For a contradiction suppose a nonempty core has
s≤2^−38. The sparse-core theorem gives 64|a since 65537 cannot be
a core divisor. The five compulsory primes all survive deleting the
vertices with p|q+1 for some p∈P and their successors. Such a deleted
vertex is at least 2 min(P)−1. The retained set G satisfies (1) with
core 1.

For clarity, the deleted product is at most 1/(1−3s/(1−s)).
A starting shift supported only on 2 and P contributes at most
2/(q+1) in reciprocal loss. Summing over all such starts with
nontrivial P-part gives at most 2s/(1−s). At any mixed start select
a simple predecessor r and a core divisor p. Its loss is at most
1/(pr). The r's are all distinct and Σ_(r∈S)1/r<1. This last sum
is bounded by β(S)−1. The mixed loss is at most s. Each successor
path is covered by the factor w(q) already charged at its start.

Also σ*(c)/c≤1/(1−s²) because the exponents are at least two. The
unitary-perfect equation gives

    2 > β(G) ≥ 2(1−s²)(1−3s/(1−s))/(1+2^−64) > 2−2^−35.

The final inequality at s=2^−38 is checked as a rational inequality.
This contradicts the neutral interval exclusion. Consequently s>2^−38
and min(P)<2^38 |P|.

4. No sparse prime-power core

If P={p}, the preceding section gives p<2^38. The Fermat-order sieve
in Erdős1052-large-prime-branches.txt §11 leaves just p=65537 and
p=167772161 in this range. For v=6,...,19 it uses divisibility
certificates for the Fermat numbers or a forced descending chain outside
the core. For v≥20 it enumerates all integers 1 mod 2^(v+1) in the
bounded range which are 2 mod 3 and satisfy 2^(2^v)≡−1 modulo that
integer. Only 167772161 at v=23 survives. The case v=4 gives 65537.
The script checks the certificates and the progression search directly.

For p=167772161, allowing arbitrary p powers in every shift, 412 nodes
exclude [2−2^−40,2). The exact simple product

    β(S) = 2 / ((1+2^−a)(1+p^−e))                                (3)

lies inside this interval for a≥48, e≥2.

For p=65537 the order gives v₂(a)=4. Moreover 16|e. To see the last
claim, if v₂(e)=j with 0≤j≤3, then respectively 3,5,17,257 divides
p^e+1, because p is 2 modulo each of these primes. The first two
copies are already supplied in 5+1 and 19+1. The last two are 2
modulo 3 and cannot be simple or repeated here. Hence e≥16.

For P={65537} the following intervals are all excluded. Here
T_a=2/(1+2^−a).

      interval                       nodes       largest local ceiling
      [2−2^−72,2)                    11766       2408433478466113845097697

5. Two repeated bases

There are also direct restrictions on the odd multiplier b when h=16,
valid without any ceiling on its prime factors. For every sparse core,
gcd(b,21)=1. If 3|b, then 193 divides 2^48+1 and therefore 2^a+1.
The prime 193 is simple and forces the simple prime 97 from its shift.
But 97+1=2·7² uses two copies of 7. If 7|b, 449 divides 2^112+1 and
its shift is divisible by 9, with the same obstruction at 3. In both
cases all primes used in the contradiction are below 4096.
In particular every sparse solution has a≥64.

With exactly two repeated primes one also has gcd(b,55)=1 at h=16.
If 5|b then both 414721 and 44479210368001 divide 2^80+1 and
therefore divide 2^a+1. Their shifts contain 11 and 71 respectively.
Neither 11 nor 71 can be simple, so both of the large primes would
have to be repeated alongside 65537. If 11|b write
(2^176+1)/65537 = 5304641·u. Both factors are 2 modulo 3, coprime
to one another and to 5·65537. Each forces a different repeated
prime. Consequently an exactly-two-base solution with v₂(a)=4
would require b≥13 and gcd(b,1155)=1. The case b=1 was excluded in
the earlier a=16 certificate.

At v₂(a)=6 the Fermat number is 274177·67280421310721. Both factors
are prime. The second, equal to 2 modulo 3, must be repeated. If
274177 is simple, its shift forces 137089. If that is also simple,
its shift 137090=10·13709 forces the prime 13709, which is 2 modulo
3 and therefore repeated. With only two bases the support is one of

    {274177,67280421310721}, {137089,67280421310721},
    {13709,67280421310721}.

At v₂(a)=12 the two primes 114689 and 26017793 both divide the Fermat
number, and both are 2 modulo 3. They fill the entire repeated support.
In fact the four named supports cannot occur for any a. Each omits
65537, so every possible exponent is at least 64.

Write (p,r) for a pair in the order printed, and e,f≥2 for the core
exponents. For a fixed e the simple product lies in

    [2/((1+p^−e)(1+r^−2)(1+2^−64)), 2/(1+p^−e)).

For all e≥E together use p^−E at the lower endpoint and 2 at the
upper endpoint. Taking E respectively 2,5,5,3 gives a certificate for
every e,f. At p=13709 all four supplier pairs must be checked. The other
three supports have s≤2^−14 and require only (19,223). The script
checks every listed interval and the primalities and divisibilities used
to force the two bases.

For an arbitrary *specified* sparse factorization the same program
provides exponent_bound(factorization,bits). Put R=2c/σ*(c). If for
each permissible supplier pair the simple products avoid
[R−2^−m,R), then a≤m. Indeed R−β(S)=R/(2^a+1), where 0<R≤2.
For instance, take c=114689²·26017793². Here R equals
8903985421153526735536129/4451992710915232720846850. With m=37
the gap test succeeds in 805 nodes, so a≤37. Both repeated primes
divide 2^4096+1 and have order 8192 for the base 2. The order theorem
would therefore force 4096|a, eliminating this fixed core. The test
can be reproduced with exponent_bound([(114689,2),(26017793,2)],bits=37).
A search that returns None leaves that input unresolved.

The same argument excludes all remaining v from 6 through 19. For
v=9,11,13,15,18,19 the two numbers in the corresponding SPLITS row
are in fact prime. The new Lucas certificates verify their primality
by recursive factorizations of p−1. They both equal 2 modulo 3, so they
are the entire core support. One interval from
2/((1+p^−2)(1+r^−2)(1+2^−64)) up to 2 excludes all exponents at each
pair. For v=7,8 the Fermat number factors as the first prime in PATHS
times respectively 59649589127497217 or 1238926361552897. The latter
factor in each case is a prime equal to 2 modulo 3 and exceeds 2^39.
The forced descending path in the former must meet a second core base.
All eligible vertices exceed 2^39 since vertices below 4096 cannot be
in a sparse core. Having both bases above 2^39 contradicts section 3.

For v=10,14,16,17 the PATHS row again requires a core base p outside
the required prime r≡2 mod 3 dividing the Fermat number. Each possible
last vertex in these rows is prime, which the program checks. Enumerate
all such r up to 2^38, 2^39, 2^40, 2^39 respectively. A sieve in the
progression r≡1 mod 2^(v+1), r≡2 mod 3 and an exact modular power test
leave only r=45592577 at v=10. The two supports there are excluded by
the same interval test as above. For a possible p>2^39 and an unlisted
partner beyond its bound, both exceed 2^39, contradicting section 3.
For each remaining p with r>R, delete the r-using shifts and their
successors. The retained set may use unlimited copies of p. Pure core
starts containing r cost at most 2p/((p−1)(r−1)) and mixed starts
less than 1/r. Hence the total reciprocal loss is below 4/(R−1).
The retained product lies between

    2(1−4/(R−1))/((1+p^−2)(1+R^−2)(1+2^−64)) and 2.

The verifier excludes this interval at each of the finitely many p,R.
The small Fermat-divisor enumeration was also run independently, testing
the same progressions without prime certification apart from division by
5,7,11,13,17,19. It returned the same four lists. Combining these cases
with the restrictions for v≤5 proves the stated alternative for exactly
two bases.

There is also an exclusion uniform in an arbitrary number of further
bases. When 65537∈P, their reciprocal sum t must exceed 2^−78. When
167772161∈P it must exceed 2^−44. To prove this, keep the indicated
base p and remove paths using any further core prime. The pure starts
with a further factor cost at most 2p t/((p−1)(1−t)) and the mixed
starts at most t. Thus their total reciprocal loss is below 4t/(1−t)
for positive t in the indicated range. The further part of the core
has abundancy at most 1/(1−t²). At t=0 section 4 already applies.
All five required small primes survive. The factor 16 divides the
exponent of 65537 even with the additional bases, since 3,5 are
already supplied and 17,257 are excluded from the entire support. The
intervals from section 4, with the new corrections at the lower
endpoints, again exclude all a≥80 and a=16,48. The other potential
small value a=64 would need a repeated prime equal to 2 modulo 3 in
2^64+1. This number is coprime to 65537 and smaller than 2^78, whereas
each additional base is at least 2^78. At 167772161 the single corrected
interval already covers every a≥48. For exactly two primes the
claimed bound on the other prime at v=4 follows immediately.

6. A uniform upper limit on the smallest repeated base

We prove a uniform form of the elementary upper-bound sieve. Let k be
even with Σ_(t|k, t odd prime)1/t≤1/4096. For any real L≥exp(16),

    Σ_(r>L, r and kr−1 prime) 1/r ≤ 50000/log L.                     (4)

Here are details accounting for uniformity in k. On X<r≤2X take
z=floor(X^(1/8)) and use the odd primes t≤z not dividing k. Each t
excludes two residue classes, since t|r(kr−1) precisely for r=0 or
k^−1 modulo t. For squarefree d on these primes the simultaneous
conditions have ν(d)=2^ω(d) classes, by the Chinese remainder theorem.
Their count in the interval differs from X g(d) by at most ν(d),
where g(d)=ν(d)/d. Put h(t)=g(t)/(1−g(t))=2/(t−2) and extend h
multiplicatively on squarefree d.

For the squarefree supported d≤z set G=Σ h(d). Choose weights λ_d
such that Σ_(d multiple of l) g(d)λ_d=μ(l)h(l)/G. Finite Möbius
inversion gives λ_1=1 and

    |λ_d| = (h(d)/g(d)) Σ_(m≤z/d, (m,d)=1) h(m)/G ≤1.

In this formula m is squarefree and supported on the sieving primes.
The inequality follows by counting all m times all divisors of d in
G and using h(d)/g(d)=∏_(t|d)(1+h(t)). Majorize the sifted count by
summing (Σ_(d|r(kr−1)) λ_d)^2. The main quadratic form is

    Σ_(d,e) λ_d λ_e g(lcm(d,e))
      = Σ_l (1/h(l))(Σ_(l|d)g(d)λ_d)^2 = 1/G,

because 1/g(gcd(d,e))=Σ_(l|gcd(d,e))1/h(l). The error is at most
Σ_(d,e≤z)ν(lcm(d,e))≤z^4. All r,kr−1 prime in the interval are
sifted because both exceed z.

To lower-bound G let Y=floor(sqrt(z)) and H=Σ_(n≤Y, n odd)1/n.
In the double sum H² remove pairs sharing an odd prime, pairs with a
nonsquarefree member, and pairs with a member divisible by an odd prime
of k. The fraction removed is at most

    3 Σ_(j≥1)1/(2j+1)^2 + 2/4096 < 3/4+2/4096.

Here 1/(2j+1)^2<1/((2j)(2j+2)) and the sum on the right telescopes
to 1/4. The retained pairs (m,n) have squarefree product d≤z and
are coprime. Their weights sum to at most G since h(d)≥2^ω(d)/d.
Thus G≥H²/5≥(log z)^2/80. For X≥exp(16) we have log z≥log X/16
and sqrt(X)≤X/(log X)^2. The sifted count is at most
21000 X/(log X)^2. Summation over X=2^j L, using the integral bound
for Σ_(j≥0)(log L+j log 2)^−2, proves (4).

Now suppose all p∈P exceed or equal L=exp(2^56). In a mixed
core-using shift choose a core prime p and an ordinary predecessor r.
The r's are all distinct, giving total reciprocal loss less than 1/L.
At a pure core-using shift choose a core prime r, and write
q+1=2^j d r. Summing over *every* such representation is an upper
bound. For fixed j and P-smooth d, formula (4) applies to k=2^j d.
The sum of 2/(q+1) over all these starts is therefore at most

    [2/(1−s)] · 50000/log L.

The harmless endpoint r=L can equivalently be avoided by taking
L=exp(2^56)/2 in (4), for which the same inequalities below hold.
In particular the total loss from these and the mixed starts is less
than 2^−38. Each successor path contributes at most its starting w.
The core itself contributes at most 1/(1−s/L), using e≥2.
Since 64|a, the retained ordinary product is at least
2(1−s/L)(1−2^−38)/(1+2^−64)>2−2^−35 and is strictly below 2.
The small supplier primes survive. For arbitrary s<1/4096, section 8
below proves that (19,223) is the mandatory supplier pair. This gives
the five primes required in section 3's neutral certificate and proves
Theorem 3. Alternatively the neutral interval is excluded for all four
original supplier pairs, in 112, 27, 13 and 44 nodes with the sharpened
tail. The size of each base alone does not bound their reciprocal sum.

The same sieve controls deletions when only the bases above L are
removed and smaller fixed bases are retained. For pure starts
the coefficient k may still use the smaller bases, with the same
uniform estimate. The results above give no upper limit on all the
other repeated bases or their exponents from a bound on the smallest.

7. The obstruction that remains at an arbitrary fixed sparse core

Fix one allowed h, let b=a/h, and cancel the fixed seeds consisting
of the odd part of σ*(c) and F=2^h+1. This fixes both their simple
primes and the residual divisor D of the core. With b unbounded, the
compactness argument in Erdős1052-prime-square.txt §10 gives an
infinite set T of simple primes of limiting product equal to the
unique dyadic normalization of DF in (1,2). The finite products tend
to this value because the path bound, after stripping the fixed core
bases, gives a uniformly negligible tail. A finite product has odd
denominator, whereas this target is a nonintegral dyadic rational.

More is true for the unstripped demands. For every X, with
T_X={q∈T:q≤X}, one has ∏_(q∈T_X)d(q) | D ∏_(q∈T_X) q. Indeed the
members of the prefix stabilize along the diagonal subsequence. Any
odd prime dividing a demand in this prefix is smaller than the prime
making that demand, so the supply needed on the right is entirely
contained in the prefix and the fixed D. Consequently at most Ω(D)
vertices anywhere in T can use a core factor. An ordinary vertex can
supply at most one other vertex.

If only finitely many Mersenne primes belong to T, it has finitely
many starting vertices because each start is a Mersenne prime or
spends a core factor. Choose one ordinary predecessor whenever possible.
The resulting paths partition T, since every backward sequence is
strictly decreasing. Every discarded ordinary edge consumes a source
which has no chosen successor. A path has at most one such terminal
vertex, and there are only finitely many paths. Thus only finitely
many vertices use multiple ordinary predecessors. Beyond all these
vertices and those spending a core factor, the paths split into finitely
many disjoint infinite chains plus a finite set. On each infinite chain

    q_(n+1)=2^j(n) q_n−1,    j(n)≥1.

After discarding 3 and 5 all q_n are 1 modulo 3, forcing each j(n)
odd. Write s_0=0, s_n=Σ_(i<n)j(i). Then

    α=lim q_n/2^s_n=q_0−Σ_(n≥1)2^−s_n > 0,
    ∏_(n≥0)(1+1/q_n)=(q_0+1)/α.

Each α is irrational. If α=A/B, the integers z_n=Bq_n−A2^s_n satisfy
0<z_n≤B and z_(n+1)+B=2^j(n) z_n. The upper bound uses the geometric
tail Σ_(m>n)2^(s_n−s_m)≤1. Given z_n, the exponent j(n) is unique
because B<2^j(n)z_n≤2B. The finite sequence of possible errors thus
enters a cycle. On any full period of its exponent sequence the
q-recurrence is an affine map with slope a power of two. Reduce this
map modulo the odd prime at the beginning of the period. It permutes
residues, so a positive number of whole periods brings the residue
back to zero. This would make that prime divide a strictly larger prime.

Hence failure of exponent finiteness at a fixed sparse core would
require either infinitely many Mersenne primes in T, or at least two
disjoint infinite chains as above whose irrational α's have rational
product. The latter conclusion uses the exact rational total product
and the finitely many discarded vertices. Excluding that product
for several disjoint chains is an additional open step.

A rational joint product also restricts how far all those chains can
jump simultaneously. Write ∏α_i=A/B, with k chains and B positive.
Truncate each chain at any index with cumulative exponent s_i, and
let t_i be the next cumulative exponent on that chain. Its truncated
value x_i=q_i/2^s_i exceeds α_i by at most 2^(1−t_i). Since ∏x_i
strictly exceeds A/B, its difference is at least 1/(B·2^(Σs_i)).
Telescoping the product on the upper side gives

    min_i t_i ≤ Σ_i s_i + log₂(2B Σ_i ∏_(j≠i) q_(j,0)).

This is a necessary restriction on any proposed set of multiple
infinite paths with the required product.

prime_path_product_irrational prove existence and irrationality of both
the normalized limit and the limiting abundancy of every such chain. Its elementary arithmetic content is no_bounded_errors
and no_rational_shadow. The theorem finite_test_of_gap converts a gap

8. Compulsory small primes at every sparse core

Here and below s denotes the sum of reciprocal core bases. In deleting
the paths that use a core base, the bound in section 3 can be sharpened.
At a pure start q+1=2^j m with m>1 supported on P, one has q≡1 mod 3.
For each m this prescribes the parity of j. Hence the sum of 2/2^j
over the eligible j≥1 is at most 4/3. Summing over m costs at most
4s/(3(1−s)). For mixed starts choose an ordinary predecessor r and a
core divisor p. The predecessors chosen are distinct. None is 3, 5 or 7,
since these three tokens are already spent in 5 and the two small suppliers.
The sum of 1/r over all remaining ordinary primes is at most 3/32,
since their product of 1+1/r is less than 2/((4/3)(6/5)(8/7))=35/32.
The mixed starts therefore cost at most 3s/32 in reciprocal loss.
At s₀=1/4096 the retained neutral product is at least

    L(a₀)=2(1−s₀²)(1−4s₀/(3(1−s₀))−3s₀/32)/(1+2^(−a₀))

whenever a≥a₀. The three alternative supplier pairs each exclude
[2−1/1024,2) in the neutral test, and L(16)>2−1/1024. Thus 19 and
223 are compulsory for every sparse core. In particular 13 cannot be
simple since 13 and 223 would both use 7. For instance, at v₂(a)=6
the mandatory repeated base 67280421310721 is 8 modulo 13 and 2
modulo 3. Its exponent is divisible by 4: an odd exponent would
supply an extra 3, and an exponent equal to 2 modulo 4 would force 13.

Using a≥64, the neutral interval [L(64),2) yields the following further
certificates. Each row excludes a support omitting all the primes in
the second column while requiring the extra primes in the first.
The five primes 3,5,7,19,223 are required in every row.

    extra required     all omitted       nodes (sharpened tail)
    none               31                  19

9. A lower bound on the amount of core used by every prime-power layer

Put F=2^h+1. For the entire variable group A=(2^a+1)/F, write D for
its terminal divisor after the fixed seeds C,F are cancelled. Set
k=floor(log₂(DF)) and R=DF/2^k. Since DF is odd and exceeds 1,
1<R<2. If a>k, the unique normalized product in [1,2) is

    β(A)=R/(1+2^(−a)).

For any candidate interval [l,u) of (5), this rules out a>k if R≤l.
If R>u, it gives 2^a<u/(R−u). All the upper endpoints in (5) are
dyadic with denominator dividing 2^35, so a≤max(k,35) whenever R
misses all the closed intervals. Known membership of the fixed groups
discards any incompatible allocation of the small blocks. For instance,
h=16 and D=13709 give a≤29, hence b=1. More generally the simpler
condition 5/4≤R≤8/5 already gives a≤k: a group containing 19 has
product at least 32/19 and one omitting its block has product below 19/16.

For an odd prime l dividing b and 0≤i<v_l(b), consider the factor

    A_i=(2^(u·l)+1)/(2^u+1),       u=h·l^i.

Every such factor can be split simultaneously from (2^a+1)/F, by the
cyclotomic factorization proved in the large-prime note, sections 9
and 13. Descending allocation assigns pairwise disjoint ordinary
supports, even if two numerical seed factors have a common prime.
Their terminal divisors d_i have product dividing D. Section 13 of
that note proves d_i>1 for each of these prime-power indices.

We prove d_i>2^24. Otherwise d_i=p is prime with 4096<p≤2^24, since
two prime factors counted with multiplicity already exceed 4096².
Call its group product B. The odd balance says that B is a power of
two times p(2^u+1)/(2^(u·l)+1). There are two ranges for u.

If u=16, the exclusion 3∤b at h=16 gives 16·l≥80. Write
m=p·65537 and R=m/2^floor(log₂m). Since floor(log₂m)<80, we get

    R/(1+2^(−80)) ≤ B < R.

Every other possible u is at least 64: h≥64 or h=16 and the prime
dividing b is at least 5. Write T=p/2^floor(log₂p). Then
T(1+2^(−64))<2 since p<2^24. The normalized value is

    B=T(1+2^(−u))/(1+2^(−u·l)),       T<B<T(1+2^(−64)).

Intersect the corresponding range with (5), requiring every member
of the chosen blocks and forbidding every member of the complementary
ones. For remaining intersections run the finite interval certificate
of section 2, with one copy of p available in the shifted factors.
A shift that uses p exhausts the copy. Subsequent starts and the tail
bound then use the empty base set. Reject p itself as an ordinary prime
throughout. These rules follow from ∏d(q) | p∏q within this group.

The inclusive sieve through 2^24 examines 1,077,307 primes above
4096. There are 34,750 nonempty interval tests and all close in 594,109
nodes altogether. The separate verification enumerated the prime ranges
by scaling the interval endpoints, used ordinary token subsets and the
coarser tail (2·number of tokens+4)/(y+1), and closed the same 34,750
tests in 1,081,584 nodes. Neither implementation caps the simple primes
in the theorem. The reproducible certificate is small_terminal_certificate
in erdos1052_chain_bounds.py.

There are Ω(b) of the prime-power indices. Taking the product of
d_i≥2^24+1 proves Theorem 4, including b=1 by the empty product.
In particular a fixed core has only the allowed powers of two as
exponents if D≤2^24 at every allowed h. Writing Ω_large(D) for the
number of prime factors of D above 2^24 counted with multiplicity also
gives 2Ω(b)≤Ω(D)+Ω_large(D), since a single-copy terminal must be
large. These estimates leave the size of a single prime in b open when
a sufficiently large terminal divisor is available.

10. Counting simple primes in one residue class

On an infinite pure path above 5 let q'=2^j q−1. Both primes are 1
modulo 3, so j is odd. Write x=q mod 5. A successor is never 4 modulo
5 since 5 does not divide 2q. For x=1 the two possibilities j≡1,3
modulo 4 lead to x'=1,2, respectively. At x=2 only j≡1 is possible
and it gives x'=3. At x=3 only j≡3 is possible and it gives x'=3.
Thus either the path eventually stays at 1 with j≡1 modulo 4, or it
eventually stays at 3 with j≡3 modulo 4.

An eventually periodic sequence of jumps is impossible. Over a whole
period the map on the current prime is affine with slope a power of two.
Modulo the odd prime at the start of the periodic tail this is a
permutation. Iterating the period returns to residue zero and produces
a larger prime divisible by the starting prime. In particular no pure
infinite path has all but finitely many jumps in {1,3}. The same holds
for any prescribed pair of positive odd jumps, one in each class mod 4.
Theorems eventual_class_mod_four and escapes_two_jumps in Spectrum.lean
verify the mod-5 argument and this escape from each pair in Lean.

Fix h,D and t. Consider finite ordinary supports compatible with D
and containing at most t vertices with odd v₂(q+1) outside {1,3}.
There is no infinite such support satisfying the balance at every
prefix. Indeed there are only finitely many Mersenne starts: apart
from the even exponent 2, a Mersenne prime exponent is odd, with all
but the two designated values counted against t. There are at most
Ω(D) other starts with core demand. As in section 7, there are then
finitely many merges and core uses, leaving at least one infinite pure
path if the support is infinite. Its eventual jumps would be in {1,3}.

Here is a terminating way to extract a numerical exponent bound. At
each sorted prefix W let U=D∏_(q∈W)q/∏_(q∈W)d(q) and B=β(W).
The reduced denominator of B is odd, so B≠R for the nonintegral
dyadic target R of section 9. If B>R, abandon that prefix. If B<R,
choose an integer X>(2Ω(U)+4)(R+B)/(R−B). When the next prime is
above X, the reciprocal loss of the whole suffix is at most
(2Ω(U)+4)/(X+1): at most Ω(U) starting paths use a prefix token and
every other start is a Mersenne prime. The resulting total product
is at most (R+B)/2. Since its exact value is R/(1+2^(−a)) for a>k,
this possibility gives 2^a+1≤2R/(R−B). Otherwise branch only on
primes q≤X with d(q) dividing U, updating U to Uq/d(q) and charging
each exceptional jump. This tree is finitely branching and has no
infinite branch by the preceding paragraph, so it is finite by
König's lemma. The maximum of k and its local exponent bounds works.
The construction also permits using a provable upper bound for Ω(U).

A bound on the number of q≡31 mod 32 bounds the count just used,
since every exceptional odd jump is at least 5. Conversely the exact
2-adic balance is v₂(σ*(c))+Σ_(q∈S)v₂(q+1)=a+1. Thus bounding a
bounds the number of q≡31 mod 32 by floor((a+1)/5). This proves
Theorem 5 after taking the maximum over the finitely many h at c.

The finite-state calculation can allow additional odd jumps. With
j in {3,7}, reduce modulo 7,11,13,17,19 in succession. For {1,9}
use 7,11,13,17,19,23,29,31,37. Above each sieving prime p, both
0 and −1 are forbidden residues on a pure path, the latter because
the preceding prime exceeds p. The transitions are x↦2^j x−1.
Every infinite walk eventually stays in one strongly connected
component. A component with at most one internal label per vertex
can only give an eventually periodic infinite word. Keep only the
other components before adjoining the next modulus, counting distinct
labels even when they have the same destination. The numbers of kept
vertices are respectively (4,18,112,221,0) and
(4,26,87,783,5445,576,576,9099,0). This verifies the same terminating
exponent bound when only odd jumps outside {1,3,7,9} are counted.

The exact balance also sharpens the several-path limit in section 7.
Take a prefix beyond all finite paths, core uses and mergers, including
a last vertex q_i on each surviving path. Its residual U has the form
V∏q_i with V an odd positive integer. Each further path step replaces
its corresponding prime token by its successor. Let E₀ be the total
shift valuation of this prefix. After arbitrary further path segments
with exponent sums s_i, the product is 2^(E₀+Σs_i)D/(V∏q_(i,n_i)).
Sending all indices to infinity and using R=DF/2^k gives

    F V ∏α_i = 2^(E₀+k).

Hence the reduced numerator of the joint product is a power of two
and its denominator is divisible by F. Individual path irrationality
and the mod-5 restriction do not yet exclude this equality.

11. Bounded witnesses among the repeated primes

Put L=exp(2^128). At any permitted v=v₂(a) there are at least two
distinct repeated primes at most max(L,2^(2^v)+1). For 6≤v≤19 at
least three distinct repeated primes are at most L. These bounds
allow arbitrarily many other core primes and arbitrary multiplicities.

Delete all paths using a core base larger than L. At mixed starts the
total loss is below 1/L. At pure starts pick one such base r and write
q+1=2^j m r. For each j,m the sieve (4) applies at L and summing
2/(2^j m) costs at most 2/(1−s). Their loss is bounded by
100000/((1−s)log L). The abundancy of the deleted part of the core is
at most 1/(1−s/L). Even together these corrections retain a factor
greater than 1−2^−100 at the lower endpoint of each interval. The
same estimates apply for any higher cutoff. If the core has at most
t bases the alternative cutoff 2^104 t suffices: the reciprocal sum
above it is at most 2^−104, and summing over a designated large factor
in each start gives loss at most 2·2^−104/(1−s)+2^−104. In the
three-prime assertion also take the maximum with 2^(2^v)+1 to contain
all witnesses below the Fermat seed.

If only one base p is at most max(L,2^(2^v)+1), the Fermat seed's
required prime equal to 2 modulo 3 is p. All forced descending paths
from this seed are also below the cutoff. If p≥2^38, remove p from
the retained set by section 3, with s there replaced by 2^−38.
Even with the additional 1−2^−100 factor this contradicts the neutral
gap. For smaller p the sole-base Fermat sieve from section 4 applies
to this fixed seed and its descendants, leaving 65537 or 167772161.
At 65537 the seed gives v=4 and a≥80, and the known small-prime
constraints still give 16|e. At 167772161 we can use a≥64. The single
base spectrum intervals in section 4 absorb the 1−2^−100 correction
in either case.

For 6≤v≤19 the entire Fermat seed is below L. It already forces the
two small witnesses described in section 5. If these are the only two
bases below L, repeat the two-base argument there on the retained set.
If both exceed 2^39, their combined reciprocal mass is below 2^−38
and the corrected neutral gap applies. For an enumerated pair or a
single known base with partner above the stated bound, multiply the
lower endpoint from section 5 by 1−2^−100. Every resulting interval
is still excluded. The calculations, including the small-prime
certificates in section 8, are in supplier_and_large_base_certificates.
An upper bound for the entire repeated core and a proof that uniformly
bounds the single large primes in b for every sparse core remain open.

12. Mersenne residual divisors of arbitrary size

Let D=p=2^k−1 be prime in the group A=(2^a+1)/(2^h+1), with
b=a/h>1 odd. Here h is the allowed power-of-two part of a. Put Y=2^h,
y=1/Y, z=2^(−a)≤y³ and write L(B)=1−1/B for reciprocal loss. The
same proof works for an individual first layer with a=h·l and l|b prime.

First suppose k>h. Since k is an odd prime, d=k−h is odd. Put
δ=2^(−d), so 1/p=δy/(1−δy). Normalizing the group identity gives

    B=(1+y)(1−δy)/(1+z),           1<B<1+y.

The inequalities follow from δ≤1/2 and y≤1/65536. Every prime in the
group is therefore greater than Y. A lower bound on its reciprocal loss is

    L(B) ≥ (1−δ)y−3y².                                               (6)

Indeed, for x=(1−δ)y−δy² one has 0≤x≤y and
1−(1+z)/(1+x)≥x−x²−z. The latter inequality follows on multiplication
by 1+x, since x³+xz≥0.

Remove the one possible vertex using p in its shifted factor and its
successors. Its path costs at most 1/p≤δy+2y². The rest is neutral.
A neutral path can start only at a Mersenne prime above Y. If
r=2Y−1 is such a prime, h+1 is prime, hence v₂(h) is a power of two
and is divisible by 4 since v₂(h)≥4. It follows that h≡6 mod 10
and 2r−1=2^(h+2)−3 is divisible by 11. The next possibilities 4r−1
and 6r−1 are excluded by divisibility by 3 in the prime or its shift.
Thus a successor of r is at least 8r−1. The entire path costs at most
1−1/((1+1/r)w(8r−1))=5y/8. This also bounds its cost if r is absent.
The remaining neutral starts have exponents h+3,h+5,... and total
loss at most y/3.

If d≥3, the Mersenne start at exponent h+d is p itself. It cannot occur
in the ordinary group, so subtract 2δy from that last upper estimate.
The resulting bound is

    L(B) ≤ (23/24−δ)y+2y².

This contradicts (6) since 5y<1/24. If d=1, the start 2Y−1 is p
and is absent. The same check of 2p−1,4p−1,6p−1 shows that any
vertex using p is at least 8p−1. Its cost is at most 1/(4p).
Together with y/3 for the remaining starts this gives at most
(11/24)y+2y². This is precisely the same contradiction at δ=1/2.

Now let k≤h. Here B=2(1−2^(−k))(1+y)/(1+z), which is greater
than 2(1−2^(−k)) and less than 2. For k≥37 this forces the five
small mandatory primes through 223 into this group: if one of them
were absent its contribution in the complement would put the total
above 2. They survive removing the one possible p-using path. The
remaining neutral product is at least (1−1/p)B>2−2^(2−k)≥2−2^−35,
which contradicts the neutral gap in section 3. The case k≤24
is covered by Theorem 4. Between 24 and 37 the only prime exponents
are 29 and 31, and 233 divides 2^29−1. For k=31, h≥64. Set
T=(2^31−1)/2^30. Three intervals from (5) intersect
[T,T(1+2^−64)). With one copy of p available the finite certificates
close in 90, 83 and 18 nodes. This completes the proof for all p.

There is a neighborhood of the dyadic powers with a related exclusion
that allows arbitrary residual factorizations. Suppose D=2^k(1+ε)
with |ε|≤y/128, and put t=Σ_(p|D)1/p≤y/128. Assuming b>1, the
normalized group has B=(1+ε)(1+y)/(1+z) in (1,1+2y). Every ordinary
prime in it exceeds Y. If its least prime were at most Y, its odd
shift could not use any core prime since every p|D is at least 128Y.
It would be a Mersenne prime. The value Y−1 is composite since h is
even, and a smaller Mersenne prime is at most Y/2−1, already giving
product greater than 1+2y.

Remove the core-using paths. Section 3 bounds their loss by 3t/(1−t).
The neutral remainder has loss at most (5/8+1/3)y=23y/24 as above.
By monotonicity, (6) with δ=1/128 bounds L(B) from below. This gives

    (127/128)y−3y² ≤ L(B) ≤ (23/24+3/127)y,

impossible for y≤1/65536. Thus every allowed h passing either
criterion has only the candidate a=h. To conclude exponent finiteness
at a specified c, evaluate the criteria at each of its finitely many h.

Spectrum.lean proves (6), the strict contradiction in the Mersenne
case, and the prime-path escape theorem. LayerMass.lean derives the
multiplicative bound from the subgroup bounds and the original odd
balance, splitting the supports of all prime-power cyclotomic factors
in Lean. The finite interval computations are verified with rational
arithmetic in Python. The full fixed-core finiteness statement is open.

13. Fermat residual divisors of arbitrary size

Suppose D=p=2^k+1 is prime. Here k is a power of two and k≥16 by
sparsity. If k>h then k≥2h, so 2^(−k) and 1/p are at most 2^(−h−7).
The neighborhood criterion of section 12 gives b=1. Suppose instead
b>1 and put X=2^k. If k=h, the group's product is
(1+1/X)²/(1+2^(−a)). Every prime in the group exceeds X/2−1.
The Mersenne starts begin at exponent k+1 since X−1 is composite,
with total reciprocal loss at most 4/(3X). Any p-using vertex is
at least 4p−1, since 2p−1 is divisible by 3. It adds at most 1/(2p).
But X times the required reciprocal loss is at least (2X−1)/(X+1),
which exceeds 11/6, a contradiction.

It remains to handle k<h. Here h≥2k, and

    1+1/X < B=(1+1/X)(1+2^(−h))/(1+2^(−a)) < 1+2/X.

Every ordinary prime in this group exceeds X. Otherwise its smallest
prime would have to be a Mersenne prime at most X/2−1, contradicting
the upper bound. If 2X−1 were absent, the remaining Mersenne starts
would cost at most 1/(3X) and removal of the possible p-using vertex
at most 1/(2p). Their total is below 1/(X+1), whereas B>1+1/X.
Consequently 2X−1 is prime. Writing k=2^w, primality of 2X−1
forces k+1 prime and hence w a power of two. Thus 4|w. One obtains

    X ≡ 1, 1, 2, 9, 3     modulo 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 respectively.

For 7,11,13 use k≡1 mod 3, k≡6 mod 10, and k≡4 mod 12 respectively.
These five congruences suffice for the following finite bound. As in
section 2, partition the group into increasing paths by choosing an
ordinary predecessor whenever possible. The starts are Mersenne primes
and at most one prime having shift of odd part p. For the latter start
the shift valuation is even, since p≡2 mod 3. All pure ordinary edges
above the starts have odd shift valuations.

Represent a vertex by Q=2^t X+c, initially c=−1 with t odd for a
Mersenne start, or c=2^t−1 with t even for the p-using start. Across
a pure edge with jump j the new pair is (t+j,2^j c−1). In all cases
|c|≤2^t−1. Each such vertex costs at most 2^(−t)/(X−1) in reciprocal
loss. Use a horizon t=10 and measure losses in units 1/(1024(X−1)).
If the next pure edge crosses the horizon the entire suffix from its
next prime costs at most 2 units by the path bound. The same bound
holds at the first impure edge: beside the chosen predecessor the
shift has a factor at least X, and so its size is at least 2X².
This includes any p used at a later vertex.

For t≤10 set H(t,c)=0 if one of the five residues proves Q composite.
All vertices being tested exceed the sieving primes. Otherwise set

    H(t,c)=2^(10−t)+max({2} ∪ {H(t+j,2^j c−1): j odd, 1≤j≤10−t}).

Induction down from the horizon bounds each path, including paths that
end early. At the Mersenne starts t=1,3,5,7,9 the values are
(533,194,0,14,5). At the five possible p-using starts t=2,4,6,8,10
they are (266,117,27,6,0). Use the largest among these latter values
and the two-unit fallback for such a start beyond the horizon. All
Mersenne starts with t>10 together cost at most two units by the
geometric sum, since there is at most one start per exponent.
The complete group's loss is therefore at most

    (533+194+14+5+266+2)/(1024(X−1))=507/(512(X−1)) < 1/(X+1).

The lower bound on B gives the opposite strict inequality. This proves
b=1 in the last case. Spectrum.fermat_path_table verifies both finite
tables by kernel computation.

14. A bound for the odd multiplier from changes of shift size

Write a=hb as above and use the entire quotient A=(2^a+1)/(2^h+1).
Let D be the residual divisor used by this group and T its simple primes.
Choose any positive u≡1 mod 4 and w≡3 mod 4. Let t count the primes in
T whose shift valuation is odd and belongs to neither {u,w}. Then

    b < 4D · 2^[17(3Ω(D)+3t+10)].                              (7)

The constants are independent of the two chosen jump sizes. Thus u and w
can be chosen afresh at each exponent, for example as the most frequent
shift sizes in their respective classes. If this exceptional count has a
fixed upper bound at c, (7) bounds b there explicitly. The argument also
applies with a=hℓ to the individual group for any odd prime multiplier ℓ
and its own terminal divisor d|D. In particular an unbounded sequence
of prime multipliers would make t grow at least logarithmically.

Here is the binary estimate used in (7). Put F=2^h+1. Let j₁,...,j_s be
any positive odd integers, H=∏(2^jᵢ−1), and suppose HA is the sum of
at most W signed powers of two, with repetitions allowed. Then

    a < 2^s (W+1)(h+1).                                          (8)

Equal powers of opposite sign cancel. Equal powers of the same sign
combine into a single higher power. Repetition of these operations gives
distinct exponents without increasing W. Choose a prime p|F. It cannot
divide 2^j−1 for odd j, since 2^(hj) would then be both 1 and −1 mod p.
Set L=(W+1)(h+1). Start with u₀=0, and inspect the jᵢ in ascending order.
While the next j is less than u₀+L, absorb it by adding j to u₀. Each
absorption at most doubles u₀+L. On stopping, u₀+L≤2^s L. Modulo
2^(u₀+L), H agrees with a signed integer K equal up to sign to the
product of the absorbed factors. Thus |K|≤2^u₀ and p∤K.

Suppose a≥2^s L. Partition the L places starting at u₀ into W+1 blocks
of length h+1. A block [x,x+h+1) omitting all signed terms gives, with
P the sum of the terms below x,

    2^(x+h+1) | FP−K,   |P|<2^x,   0<|FP−K|<(F+1)2^x≤2^(x+h+1).

Indeed F(HA)=(2^a+1)H and p∤K. This contradiction forces one term in
every block and proves (8). Binary.quotient_bound checks the complete
estimate, including carrying repeated signed digits, in Lean.

To apply (8), orient the group as a forest whose roots divide A. The
children of q are the ordinary primes in d(q). Each has a unique parent
unless it is a root. There are integers m_q supported on the core with

    q=2^j m_q∏children r−1,   A=m₀∏roots q,   m₀∏m_q=D.

There are at most Ω(D)+t+3 leaves: a leaf spends a core factor or is a
Mersenne prime, and only the three Mersenne exponents 2,u,w can escape
the count t. The number of vertices with at least two children is at
most the number of leaves. Mark the leaves, the vertices with at least
two children, the vertices with m_q>1, the vertices 3,5 and their
parents, and the vertices counted by t. The number M of marked vertices
is at most 3Ω(D)+3t+10. Every other vertex has one child r>5 and is
2^j r−1 with j in {u,w}: both primes are 1 mod 3, so j is odd.

There are at most M maximal segments of unmarked vertices since each
ends downwards just above a different marked vertex. Along such a segment
read the steps in increasing-prime order. After the first step the
available residues mod 5 are 1,2,3. Their respective possible movements
are 1→1 by u, 1→2 by w, 2→3 by u, and 3→3 by w. Hence there are at most
five constant runs per segment, including its initial step. Let s≤5M be
the total number of these runs. For a run of length n and jump j,

    (2^j−1)q_out=2^(nj)(2^j−1)q_in−(2^(nj)−1).

For a computed subtree use the pair (Hq,H), where H is the product of
its run denominators. Bound its cost by the sum of the counts in signed
binary representations of both entries. A run multiplies this cost by
at most 4, by the displayed identity. A marked vertex needs at most
3m_q times the product of the costs of its children: its new numerator
is 2^j m_q times the product of their numerators, minus the product of
their denominators. The extra denominator adds that latter product once
more. This works for a leaf with the empty products as well. At the
roots multiply the numerators and m₀. Thus W≤D·3^M·4^s. Substitution
in (8), using h≥16 and 3·8^5<2^17, gives (7). No estimate for t solely
in terms of c follows from this calculation.

There is a simpler unconditional estimate before any runs are grouped.
Let ℱ be the forest of the assigned simple primes, and let U(ℱ) count
its antichains, including the empty one. Then b≤D U(ℱ)≤D·2^|T|.
Indeed A=1−2^h+2^(2h)−…+2^((b−1)h) has minimum signed binary weight
exactly b. To see the lower bound, first carry or cancel equal powers.
Term 0 is forced by parity. At each further center m=hi, the original
sum modulo 2^(m+1) differs from ±2^m by less than 2^(m−1), since all
lower terms have exponent at most m−2. A distinct signed expansion
omitting both m−1 and m would instead be within 2^(m−1) of a multiple
of 2^(m+1). These position pairs are disjoint. For a rooted tree at q,
its antichain count is one plus the product of the children's counts.
The defining formula for q therefore gives signed weight at most this
count times all core factors used in that tree. Multiplying over roots
proves the bound. In particular the group for a single prime multiplier
ℓ requires at least log₂(ℓ/d) simple primes, including every possible
branch, where d is the divisor actually spent on that multiplier.

If the forest has L>0 leaves, this also gives the stronger polynomial
bound b≤D(1+|T|/L)^L. Indeed, choose one child at each vertex with
children and keep only these chosen edges. The resulting disjoint
chains end at distinct leaves and cover every vertex. An antichain
uses at most one member of each chain. If their lengths are n_i, there
are at most ∏(1+n_i) choices, bounded by (1+|T|/L)^L by the arithmetic
and geometric mean inequality. In particular a fixed number of leaves
forces polynomial growth of the support in any unbounded multiplier.

There is a short additional restriction at the top of the quotient.
For any odd m≥3 write A_m=(2^(hm)+1)/F and Q_i=1+(A_m−1)/2^i for
0≤i<h. All Q_i are odd, Q_0=A_m, and their successive odd shifts are
Q_(i+1), followed at i=h−1 by A_(m−2). This last identity follows from
Q_(h−1)+1=2^(h+1) A_(m−2). Modulo 11 the residues of the first ten
terms are 1+(A_m−1)6^i. Unless A_m≡1 mod 11 one of these is zero,
since 6 has order 10. For Y=2^h, its order mod 11 is 5, so A_m≡1
exactly when m≡1 mod 5. Two consecutive odd multipliers m,m−2 cannot
both do this. At a sparse core 11 divides neither the core nor any
simple prime's shift. For a large prime ℓ, before reaching this forced
multiple of 11, some earlier prescribed number must therefore split
or be absorbed by the core. There are at most 9 candidate positions
before the forced multiple if ℓ≢1 mod 5, and at most h+9 in every case.
Taking ℓ large makes every number tested larger than c. A split can
still include a bounded prime. The modular contradiction for the two
successive blocks is ResidualSquares.terminal_halves.

15. Squared Mersenne residuals and products of Fermat primes

At every permitted h the following residual divisors allow only b=1.

  • D=p² for a Mersenne prime p=2^k−1, of any size.
  • D is a product of any finite set of Fermat primes, each with
    exponent one or two, of any sizes compatible with sparsity.

In fact any nontrivial D with this conclusion excludes h altogether.
If b=1 then A=1, and A·∏d(q)=D·∏q together with d(q)<q forces T empty
and D=1. Conversely at a sparse completion D=1 forces b=1 by section 9.

These conditions concern D after the fixed seeds are cancelled. They
place no such factorization condition on the original core c. A
normalized candidate for B=∏_(q∈T)(1+1/q) below refers to the unique
power-of-two multiple in [1,2) required by AB=D·2^E. In each case the
stated interval for B fixes this multiple. Throughout assume b≥3.

For the Fermat assertion let the smallest residual prime be p=X+1,
X=2^k, x=1/X, and e=1 or 2 its exponent. Sparsity gives X≥65536.
All larger Fermat bases have the form 1+X^(2^i), i≥1. Removing their
leading dyadic powers gives a factor 1≤G≤(1−x²)^−2 since
∏_(i≥1)(1+x^(2^i))=1/(1−x²). Independently, the entire reciprocal
loss from paths starting with a shifted core factor that contains any
larger base is less than 3x². Indeed, summing 2/(2^j m) for j≥1 and
all such m, even allowing unlimited powers of every base, costs at most

    2(1+x)((1−x²)^−1−1)=2x²/(1−x)<3x².

Here 1/(1−1/p') equals 1+1/(p'−1) for every Fermat base p'. Choose an
ordinary predecessor whenever possible. The roots of the resulting
increasing paths have either a Mersenne shift or a core-only shift.
Thus the bound just obtained applies irrespective of mixed vertices
farther along these paths.

If k>h then k≥2h. The normalized D differs from 1 by at most
(1−x)^−2−1<3x and the reciprocal sum of its bases is at most
x/(1−x). Section 12's neighborhood criterion applies since x≤2^(−2h).
For k≤h put y=2^(−h), z=2^(−a), so z≤y³. Now
B=(1+x)^e G(1+y)/(1+z).

First let k<h and e=1. Then y≤x² and 1+x<B<1+2x, by the bound on G.
As in section 13, every ordinary prime in the group exceeds X. Without
r=2X−1 the Mersenne and p-only roots cost at most x/3+1/(2p).
Even with 3x² more this is below 1/(X+1), so r is forced. The finite
path table of section 13 still applies to the Mersenne roots and the
one p-only root. Any impure continuation has a second factor greater
than X. The entire loss is at most 507/(512(X−1))+3/X²<1/(X+1), a
contradiction.

Next take either k<h,e=2 or k=h,e=1. In both cases

    1+2x < B < 1/(1−2x).

For the first upper bound use (1+x²)/(1−x)^2<1/(1−2x). For the second
use (1−x)^−2<1/(1−2x). When k=h the lower bound follows from
(1+x)^2/(1+x³)>1+2x. There is no ordinary prime q≤X/2−1, since such
a prime already contributes reciprocal loss at least 2/X. Exponent k
is even, so there is no Mersenne start X−1. A possible first start
2X−1 costs at most 5/(8X), and the remaining Mersenne starts cost at
most 1/(3X), by the modulo-11 argument in section 12 with k in place
of h. Roots whose odd part is p have even jump size since p≡2 mod 3,
and cost at most 2/(3p) in total. Roots with odd part p² cost at most
4/(3p²). Including the larger bases gives loss at most
13/(8X)+5/X²<2/(X+2), contradicting B>1+2x.

Finally let k=h,e=2. One has 1+3x<B<1/(1−3x), using
(1+x)/(1−x)^2<1/(1−3x). Every group prime exceeds X/3−1. The start
r=X/2−1 is forced, since in its absence the Mersenne roots cost at most
4/(3X), the p-only roots at most 4/(3X) even with unlimited powers,
and the larger-base roots less than 3/X². Their sum is below 3/(X+3).

Write k=2^v. Primality of r implies k−1 and v are prime. Since v≥4,
v is odd, k≡2 mod 3, k+1 is composite and X≡4 mod 7. The pair r,2r−1
has product X/(X−3)>B, and 8r−1=4X−9 is divisible by 7. An impure
edge uses a further factor exceeding X/3−1. The only remaining pure
multipliers are powers of two of odd exponent, so a successor of r is
at least 32r−1. Its path costs at most 17/(8X). All other Mersenne
roots cost at most 1/(3X) since k+1 is composite.

For the two p copies take q=4p−1, if present as a p-only root. Since
q≡2 mod 5, the vertex 4q−3 is composite. Either the first successor
is at least 8q−1, or the first is 2q−1 and its successor is at least
8(2q−1)−1. In either case the path costs at most 13/(8(q+1))=13/(32p).
There is at most one further p-only root and it costs at most 1/(8p).
Without q the two smallest possible jumps are 4 and 6, with still
smaller total cost. A p² root alone costs at most 1/p². Thus the total
loss is at most 287/(96X)+3/X²<3/(X+3). This proves the Fermat claim.
The tight constant uses 17/8+1/3+17/32=287/96.

For the Mersenne square put Y=2^h, y=1/Y, z=2^(−a), p=2^k−1. First
take k≤h or k=h+1. Then 2(1−2^(−k))²<B<2. For k≥40 all five mandatory
primes through 223 must lie in the group, since otherwise its complement
would bring the total to at least two. Delete the paths using p. There
are at most two, each of loss at most 1/p, and each starts above 223.
The resulting neutral product exceeds 2−9·2^(−k)>2−2^(−35), contrary
to section 3. For k<40 the prime possibilities compatible with sparsity
are 13,17,19,31: the exponents 23,29,37 give factors 47,233,223 in their
Mersenne numbers. In this branch the (k,h) cases with h=16 are (13,16),
(17,16); for h≥64 all four k occur. With p² providing two tokens,
verify_mersenne_squares in erdos1052_verify_terminal_range.py closes
all 18 nonempty intersections with the block intervals. It uses both
independent exact searches. The h≥64 envelope has endpoints T and
T(1+2^−64), T=2(1−2^(−k))². At h=16 use endpoints T(1+2^−16)/(1+2^−48)
and T(1+2^−16).

Otherwise d=k−h≥3 is odd. Set δ=2^(−d). The group product is
B=(1+y)(1−δy)^2/(1+z), in (1,1+y). Its reciprocal loss is at least
(1−2δ)y−3y², by expanding the numerator and using z≤y³. Every group
prime exceeds Y. At d≥5, the missing Mersenne start p saves 2δy from
the neutral bound of 23y/24. A root with odd part p has odd jump. Two
such roots cost at most 5/(4p), and a p² root alone costs at most 1/p².
The resulting upper bound is

    (23/24−2δ)y + 5δy/(4(1−δy)) < (1−2δ)y−3y²

for δ≤1/32 and y≤1/65536. At d=3, if 2Y−1 is absent, the neutral roots
cost at most y/12 after removing p, already enough with 5/(4p).
If 2Y−1 is present, h+1 is prime, h≡1 mod 3 and h+5 is composite.
The start h+3 would be p. The neutral cost is at most 5y/8+y/48.
Now 8p−1=64Y−9 is divisible by 7 because Y≡2 mod 7. Also q=2p−1 is
3 mod 5, so 2q−1 cannot be its successor. Its path costs at most 5/(8p).
A second p-only root has jump at least 5. Together they cost at most
11/(16p), a bound valid also if q is absent or there is just a p² root.
The full cost is at most 31y/48+11y/(16(8−y))<3y/4−3y². This finishes
the square case for all k. ResidualSquares.lean checks the path loss
identities, the three tight Fermat inequalities, and the two Mersenne
inequalities. The decomposition of the prime trees in this section is
proved above on paper.

16. An exponent test using only the residual divisor

Let F=2^h+1, k=⌊log₂(FD)⌋, R=FD/2^k and η=1−1/R. Suppose η≤2^(−s)
with s≥3. For a>k the normalization gives B=R/(1+2^(−a)) and its
reciprocal loss is η−2^(−a)/R. In particular every ordinary prime in
the group exceeds 2^s−1. A Mersenne starting exponent must then exceed
s. Let W_s be any upper bound for the sum of 2^(1−j) over these possible
exponents. Using every odd exponent gives W_s=2/(3·2^s) when s is odd.
When s is even and s+1 is composite one can use W_s=1/(3·2^s).

Every other starting prime has q+1=2^j m for m|D, m>1. All group primes
are 1 modulo 3 since none can be 3 or 5. Denote the nontrivial character
modulo 3 by χ. Summing 2/(2^j m) over its permitted jump parity gives
(1+χ(m)/3)/m. Thus if

    g = η−W_s−Σ_(m|D, m>1) (1+χ(m)/3)/m > 0,

then a≤max(k,⌊log₂(1/(Rg))⌋). This test allows arbitrary prime bases
and multiplicities in D. The function dyadic_root_bound uses the first
12 possible odd exponents explicitly before bounding the tail. For
example h=16, D=2147639671 prime gives a≤47, so b=1. The 32 block
intervals alone leave this residual open. This is an example at the
level of the residual condition. No existence of a core with this
particular residual is asserted.

17. A further bound on the repeated bases

Let P₀={65537,2^31−1,2^61−1}. At every sparse core one has

    Σ_(p|c, p∉P₀) 1/p > 2^−76.                                    (9)

To prove this, write t for that sum and assume t≤2^−76. In particular
all primes outside P₀ exceed 8191. Discard every ordinary vertex whose
shift uses one of those primes, and its successors. The reciprocal loss
of the discarded part is at most 4t. At a first core-only shift pick
one of its discarded factors p. Summing 2/(2^j p m) over j and the
remaining core factors costs at most 2t/(1−s), where s<1/4096 is the
full reciprocal sum. At a mixed start, choose an ordinary predecessor
r. Its cost is at most 1/(pr), and the chosen predecessors are distinct
with Σ1/r<1. The total is at most 2t/(1−s)+t<4t. The discarded core
powers have reciprocal abundancy at least 1−t². All the compulsory
blocks in section 8 survive, since every discarded node exceeds 8191.

Here a≥80: the only previously permitted a between 64 and 79 is 64,
and it forces the repeated factor 67280421310721 outside P₀. Its
reciprocal by itself exceeds 2^−76. If 65537 is present, its exponent
is at least 16. Put r=2^31−1 and set T=2/(1+r^−2) if r occurs to
exponent two, and T=2 in every other case. In the retained support
its simple product lies in [T−2^−72,T), by the rational inequality

  2(1−4t)(1−t²)/[(1+2^−80)(1+65537^−16)(1+r^−3)(1+(2^61−1)^−2)]
    > 2−2^−72.

For each of the eight subsets of P₀, allow all powers of the retained
bases in a shift. Use each possible T and each of the three mandatory
block arrangements. All 36 interval certificates close. Their exact
node counts and the divisibility checks below can be reproduced with
three_base_gap_certificate in erdos1052_chain_bounds.py. This proves (9).

Consequently no sparse core can consist entirely of Fermat primes,
whatever their exponents. More generally, if every repeated base is
one unit from a power of two, at least one of 8191,131071,524287 must
be repeated. Indeed F₅=2^32+1 and F₆=2^64+1 have proper divisors 641
and 274177, respectively. Apart from 65537, all possible Fermat bases
above 4096 therefore have exponent at least 128. For Mersenne bases
above 4096 and exponent below 79, the only possibilities have exponents
13,17,19,31,61. For the other prime exponents below 79 the following
proper divisors of 2^k−1 suffice:

    k   23   29   37    41   43    47    53     59         67      71   73
    d   47  233  223 13367  431  2351  6361 179951  193707721  228479  439

The sum of reciprocals of every Mersenne candidate at exponents ≥79
is at most 2^−77 by a geometric series. All Fermat candidates at
exponents ≥128 together cost less than 2^−126. Without any of the
three indicated bases these upper bounds contradict (9). This leaves
arbitrary sparse cores with other repeated primes unresolved.

18. A second split when the quotient has two prime factors

Put N=hℓ, F=2^h+1 and A=(2^N+1)/F for a prime ℓ|b. Fix B≥max(61,c),
and put C_B=c∏_(odd primes s≤B)s. Set H=48 if h=16, and H=2h+34
if h≥64. Suppose ℓ>max(F,2^H C_B) and A has exactly two prime factors.
Then in the descent from one of those factors there is a prime Q for
which d(Q) has two distinct prime divisors, one larger than B. The sum
of the shift valuations along the path, including v₂(Q+1), is at most
H. In particular the size of Q is at least (2hℓ+1)/2^H. The second
divisor can be bounded even when ℓ grows.

Every p|A has order 2hℓ for the base 2. Indeed gcd(A,F) divides ℓ,
as follows by evaluating the alternating quotient polynomial at −1.
Here ℓ>F, and 2^N≡−1 mod p rules out any smaller order whose 2-part
is below 2h. Thus p≥2hℓ+1 and all prime factors of A are simple and
distinct by the odd balance. Write A=pq.

Assume the stated split is absent. On a path from p, after consuming
i binary positions the current prime Q is at least p/2^i. Write
j=v₂(Q+1). If i+j≤H, the odd part of Q+1 exceeds C_B. It has a prime
factor above B, since all its factors together divide c times a
squarefree product. Absence of the split forces d(Q) to equal that
single prime R. Hence Q=2^j R−1 and R is again a simple prime above B.
If i+j>H the unexamined part of this edge need not be followed.

The low binary digits of p−1 now record these edges. Each edge has a
zero followed by j−1 ones, by Q−1=2^j(R−1)+(2^j−2). An unfinished
last edge has the corresponding prefix. At any zero at place i the
current prime is 1+floor((p−1)/2^i). The same applies to q.

Initially p≡1 mod 2h, so p−1 starts with at least five zero bits.
The four primes 1+(p−1)/2^i for 0≤i≤3 would include a multiple of 5
unless p≡1 mod 5. Thus p≡1 mod 5. For each completed edge Q,R≡1 mod 3,
so j is odd. If Q≡1 mod 5 then j≡3 mod 4 would give R≡4 mod 5.
Only 19 can be 4 mod 5 among the ordinary primes, since it consumes
the sole 5 in its shift. Therefore R≡1 mod 5 and j≡1 mod 4. Every
completed run of ones has length divisible by four. An unfinished run
places no constraint on its length.

Write k₁=v₂(p−1), k₂=v₂(q−1). Since Fpq=2^N+1, v₂(pq−1)=h.
If k₁≠k₂ their minimum is h. If both equal k<h−1, the first two bits
starting at k on both words are ones, since a run of length one is
impossible. Both (p−1)/2^k and (q−1)/2^k are thus 3 mod 4, giving
v₂(pq−1)=k+1<h. Consequently both p and q equal 1 modulo 2^m with
m=h−1. The first m zero positions give m successive large primes
1+(p−1)/2^i. For any odd prime s with 2 a primitive root and
s−1≤m, one of these would be 0 mod s unless p≡1 mod s. This gives
p,q≡1 mod 11 at h=16, and modulo each of 11,13,29 at h≥64.

The following finite digit test is necessary. At position i let z be
floor((p−1)/2^i) modulo the product of the sieving primes, and let l
be the length of the unfinished run of ones modulo four. To read a
zero require l=0 and z≠−1,−2 modulo each sieving prime. The current
prime and its shift have residues z+1 and z+2. None of 11,13,29
occurs in the core or the simple support, so each residue is nonzero.
After a bit x, replace z by (z−x)/2 modulo the product, and replace
l by zero at x=0 or l+1 mod 4 at x=1. At i=m both words start in
state (0,0).

For h=16 use only 11. If the retained low parts are P,Q and the carry
is (FPQ−1)/2^i, choose a bit x for P. The bit y for Q must equal
carry+x mod 2. The new carry is

    [carry+F(xQ+yP+2^i xy)]/2.

Initially P=Q=1, i=15, carry=2. Keep all choices passing both digit
tests, exchanging the words to omit the symmetric choice when P=Q.
There are no surviving states after bit 47. The peak list length is
8, with 145 noninitial states over all levels. PairChains.obstruction_sixteen
checks the empty final level in the Lean kernel.

For h≥64 use all three sieving primes and write p=1+2^m u, q=1+2^m v.
The equation modulo 2^N, after subtracting one and dividing by 2^m,
requires zero low bits for

    2+u+v+2^m(uv+2u+2v)+2^(2m+1)uv.

Read the first m bits of u,v using the addition u+v+2. Start the carry
at two. At each step choose x, set y=(carry+x) mod 2, and replace the
carry by (carry+x+y)/2. Preserve the lowest 36 bits a,b of u,v, along
with the two digit states. After position 36 the transition is the
same at every step. The complete set of possible states at position
431 equals the set at 851. Equality of the sets makes the subsequent
evolution periodic with period 420.

For the next 36 bits of the required zero expansion, start the same
addition at carry+a b+2a+2b modulo 2^36, retaining the two digit
states. Higher bits in the correction do not affect these 36 positions,
and m≥63 makes the last term irrelevant. The permitted m=2^v−1 start
at 63 and evolve by m↦2m+1. Reducing positions ≥431 into [431,850]
gives the following phase and the number of further bits to exhaustion.

    phase  63 127 255 511 603 787 735 631 843 847 435 451 483 547 675
    bits   15  24  20  16  15  29  20  36  12  24  20  36  15  24  20

The next reduced phase is 511 and the subsequent orbit repeats.
Thus the first 2m+36=2h+34 bits cannot all pass. The exact state sets,
their recurrence, and every entry in both calculations are checked by
erdos1052_pair_chains.py. PairChains.lean also checks the identities
for the binary edge, product lift, two additions and initial residues.
The uniform period calculation and its application to the prime descent
use the Python certificate and the argument above.

For quotients with at most four factors there is another consequence.
Use H=h+4, the same size threshold, and suppose no split occurs in
this shorter interval. If A has r≤4 prime factors and t is the least
valuation v₂(p−1) among them, then t≥h−r+1. Indeed suppose t+r≤h.
For every k=v₂(p−1)<t+r, the four bits starting at k are ones.
Hence p−1≡−2^k modulo 2^(t+r). All products of two such differences
vanish modulo 2^(t+r) because t≥5≥r. Therefore

    Σ_(p|A) 2^(v₂(p−1)−t) ≡ 0 mod 2^r.

One summand is one. The other r−1 powers would have residue 2^r−1,
which needs at least r ones in binary even after carrying. This is
impossible. Thus every top prime is 1 modulo each primitive-root
prime s with s−1≤h−r+1, by its initial zero positions. Reducing
F A=2^(hℓ)+1 modulo s forces ord_s(2^h) to divide ℓ−1. In particular
ℓ≡1 mod 15 at h=16, and ℓ≡1 mod 118755 at h≥64. The second modulus
is the least common multiple of the odd parts of s−1 for
s=3,5,11,13,19,29,37,53,59,61. Thus outside the indicated residue
class, either A has at least five distinct prime factors or an early
shift splits. Neither alternative bounds the size of ℓ at a fixed core.

When A has exactly three prime factors the direct digit test also closes
at h=16,64,128, without a congruence condition on ℓ. The corresponding
horizons are 85,154,267. Start at m=h−2 by the four-factor argument.
Use the sieving lists (5,7,11,13), (5,7,11,13,19,29,37), and the latter
list with 53,59 appended, respectively. Apart from 7, the initial
residue z is zero at each modulus by the primitive-root argument.
Modulo 7 it is in {0,1,2,4}, since the initial halves of a large prime
avoid multiples of 7. The top primes have product 1 modulo 7, since
ord₇(2^h)=3 and the preceding argument forces ℓ≡1 mod 3. Test all
unordered triples of compatible initial residues.

At each bit the parity of the three proposed bits must equal
(F P₁P₂P₃−1)/2^i modulo 2, with P₁,P₂,P₃ the current low parts.
For 19 and 37 require only z≠−1 at a zero. For the other sieving
primes both z≠−1 and z≠−2 hold, since 5 and 7 are already spent
in 19 and 223, and the others are absent from the support. Keeping
unordered triples gives respectively 3727,16648,27139 noninitial
states across all levels, and empty final levels. The three complete
calculations are in three_prime_check.

19. An exponent bound outside fourteen short intervals

For fixed h put F=2^h+1, k=⌊log₂(FD)⌋ and R=FD/2^k. Merge the 32
closed intervals of (5), including all overlaps. The resulting union
I has 14 intervals. If R is outside I, then

    h(b−1) ≤ ⌈log₂ D⌉.                                             (10)

Indeed section 9 gives a≤max(k,35). For b>1 one has a≥48, hence
a≤k. Since FD=2^h D(1+2^−h) and 1+2^−h<2, integrality gives
k≤h+⌈log₂ D⌉. The case b=1 also satisfies (10).

For a test using D alone, normalize D by its own leading power of two.
For each interval [l,u] in (5) include [l/(1+2^−16),u], folding the
part below 1 back into [1,2] by multiplication by 2. Merge overlaps
to obtain J, again with 14 intervals. Indeed every normalized FD is
the normalized D shifted by a factor 1+2^−h, followed by normalization,
and 1≤1+2^−h≤1+2^−16. Thus (10) holds at every permitted h whenever
the normalized D misses J.

If K is either merged list, its total logarithmic length is
log₂(∏_([l,u]∈K)u/l). Raising this rational product to the 100th
power for I, and to the 97th power for J, gives a rational strictly
less than 2 in each case. The construction and rational comparisons
are in normalized_residual_windows in erdos1052_chain_bounds.py.
The two logarithmic lengths are approximately 0.00997149 and
0.01025766. WindowBounds.lean verifies in the Lean kernel that the
fourteen intervals cover all 32 allocations, that both strict width
comparisons hold, and that the dyadic separation gives the exponent
bound. The passage from a unitary perfect number to those allocations
uses the earlier prime-support arguments.

For instance, on any specified sequence D=D₀ d^e with D₀ odd and
d>1 odd, the lower natural density of e satisfying the test at a
fixed h exceeds 99 percent. The lower density satisfying the test
simultaneously at all permitted h exceeds 96/97. Indeed log₂ d is
irrational, since no positive power of d is a power of two. The
fractional parts of e log₂ d plus a fixed constant are equidistributed.
For each nonzero integer k the corresponding mean exponential sum
is a geometric progression divided by its length and tends to zero,
which gives equidistribution by Weyl's criterion. The relevant
exceptional proportions equal the above lengths since interval
endpoints have measure zero. These are statements about residual
values. For an individual core its actual D_h is computed by the
fixed-seed cancellation and can fall in one of the exceptional intervals.

20. Mersenne residuals with a dyadic weighted sum

Let D>1 consist of Mersenne primes p=2^k−1 with multiplicities e_p.
Put x_p=1/(p+1), S=Σ e_p x_p, T=Σ x_p and U=∏(1−x_p)^e_p. Here
the letter S denotes this scalar only in section 20. Suppose S=2^(−m)
for an integer m≥0. A completion with b>1 requires both 131071 and
524287 in D and one of the following two parameter pairs:

    m=14 and h≥64,                 m=15 and h=16.                  (11)

In particular there is no such completion for m≥19, or for any single
Mersenne prime power with multiplicity 2^i, 0≤i≤k. The bounds have no
cutoff on k, on the multiplicities in a composite D, or on b under the
stated weighted-sum condition.

Write Y=2^h, y=1/Y, z=2^(−a)≤y³. The group product B is the unique
dyadic multiple of U(1+y)/(1+z) in [1,2). The elementary bounds
1−S≤U≤1/(1+S) follow by multiplying 1−x_p and by expanding the
product of their reciprocals, respectively.

First suppose y≤S≤2^−38. Then 2(1−S)<B<2. Each of 3,5,7,19,223 is
in the group, since a missing one's factor in the complement would
take the total above 2. Delete all vertices spending a core prime and
their successors. Every deleted path can be started at a vertex using
some p|D: at a merger continue along a deleted incoming path if one
exists. Its loss is at most 2/(q+1)≤1/p. At most e_p such starts may
use p. Thus the deleted loss is at most Σ e_p/p≤2S. The five required
vertices survive, since p>4096. The neutral remainder has product
greater than 2(1−S)(1−2S)>2−2^−35, contradicting section 3. At the
opposite extreme S≤y/256, use the last criterion of section 12 with
|1−U|≤S and Σ1/p≤2S.

For y/256<S<y put δ=S/y. Then δ is a power of 1/2 between 1/128 and
1/2, and every exponent k is greater than h. Now

    B=U(1+y)/(1+z),       1<B<1+(1−δ)y,
    1−1/B ≥ (1−δ)y−3y².                                           (12)

The upper bound follows from U≤1/(1+δy), and the lower bounds from
U≥1−δy as in (6). Thus every ordinary group prime exceeds Y.

Partition the group into paths as in section 2. Begin with the possible
Mersenne starts. If r=2Y−1 is prime then h+1 is prime; since h=2^v and
v≥4, v must be a power of two divisible by four. Thus h is a positive
power of 16. One has Y≡9,3,2,1 modulo 11,13,7,5, respectively, by
raising these residues to the sixteenth power. Every pure edge a→q has
q=2^j a−1 with j odd, by reduction modulo 3. An edge with another odd
factor has q+1≥2·4097a, since all ordinary vertices exceed Y and all
core factors exceed 4096.

The candidates 2r−1 and 8r−1 are divisible by 11 and 13. The next
vertex is therefore at least 32r−1. For t=8Y−1, the candidate 8t−1
is divisible by 5. Unless the next vertex is z₁=2t−1, it is at least
32t−1. After z₁, the exponents 1,5 give multiples of 5, since z₁≡3
mod 5. The exponent 3 gives 128Y−25, divisible by 7. Hence an edge
after z₁ goes at least to 128z₁−1. If a vertex q has a suffix of loss
at most C/(Kq) after it, its whole path has loss at most
(1+C/K)/(q+1). Starting with the universal C=2, this bounds the r
path by 17/[16(r+1)] and the t path by 193/[128(t+1)]. The exponent
h+5 is divisible by 3. The remaining odd exponents h+7,h+9,... cost
at most y/48. In total the neutral-start budgets are

    (17/32+193/1024+1/48)y = (2275/3072)y.                       (13)

Without an ordinary r one can instead allot the entire sequence
h+3,h+5,... its unrestricted budgets 2·2^(−k), of sum y/3. This
also applies if 2Y−1 is composite.

Pay for all starts using D from the allowances for Mersenne primes
that occur in D and are consequently absent as ordinary vertices.
Each start has q+1=2^j d with d|D and d>1. All p|D equal 1 modulo 3,
so j is odd. For d=p the total cost is at most 4/(3p). For p=r the
divisors 11 and 13 again rule out j=1,3, leaving at most 1/(12p).
For d involving at least two factors with multiplicity, put s=Σ1/p.
Ordered products overcount each such d, giving total cost at most
(4/3)s²/(1−s). Since s≤T/(1−S) and T≤S≤1/65536, this is at most
T/100. Charge x_p/100 to each p. The entire charge per p≠r is then
at most (3/2)x_p, and the charge at p=r at most x_p/8. The neutral
budget lost at a missing t is (193/128)x_t>(3/2)x_t; at any later
candidate it is 2x_p. Thus when r is ordinary, (13) covers every
path. Without an ordinary r, the y/3 budget covers all p≠r and the
extra charge for p=r is at most y/16. This gives (19/48)y. For
δ≤1/4 both bounds contradict (12), since (3/4)y−3y²>(2275/3072)y.
For δ=1/2 the prime r cannot be ordinary, by the strict upper bound
in (12) and 1/r>y/2. Now y/2−3y²>(19/48)y. This treats all S<y.

It remains to examine 0≤m≤37 and y≤S. The check uses only rational
arithmetic. Enumerate the possible subsets of the exponents 13,17,19,
31,61 from section 17 subject to k≥m. Drop vertices using any prime
with k≥79 and their successors. By section 17 this costs at most
4·2^−77 in loss, independently of all multiplicities. The compulsory
blocks survive. If known small multiplicities have already contributed
the factor V to U and removed part of the mass, let s₁ be the mass
left, and x an upper bound for the remaining individual x_p. Then

    V exp(−s₁/(1−x)) ≤ U ≤ V exp(−s₁).

This follows by integrating 1/(1−t) from 0 to x_p. For a lower
bound use the degree-13 Taylor polynomial of exp(−t), and for an upper
bound its degree-14 polynomial, by the sign of the remainder. Initially
V=1, x≤1/8192 and S≤1, so U>1/4. Also U(1+y)/(1+z)<1. Only scale
factors 2 and 4 need be tested. For h=16 the correction lies in
[(1+y)/(1+y³),1+y); the entire range h≥64 uses (1,1+2^−64).
Multiply the lower bound by 1−2^−75 for the deletion and intersect
with the appropriate block window (5). Its lower bound and all required
primes still hold after deletion. The upper bound is strict.

For each nonempty interval apply the section 2 verifier. An inconclusive
test is retried with at most floor(2^k s₁) copies of any remaining
small base. When needed, enumerate the exact positive multiplicity of
the smallest remaining base through that bound, reserving at least one
copy of the others. The process either proves exclusion or reports the

already covered by exception (11), erdos1052_mersenne_mass.py closes

residues, the path costs and the inequalities used in (12)–(13), in
conjunction with (6) in Spectrum.lean.

If there are only the two compulsory bases, write their exponents as
e and f. Then 4e+f=2^(19−m). In the two regimes of (11), e ranges
from 1 to 7 or from 1 to 3, respectively. Evaluate U exactly for each
pair. Here U>1−2^−14, so the dyadic multiplier is 2. There is no
large-base deletion. Separate h=64 from h≥128 to narrow the correction.
The remaining 51 nonempty intervals close in 39,257 nodes in
exact_pair_certificate. This proves the requirement of a third base.

21. The residue count for an arbitrary fixed core

Let c be any odd powerful core, with full ordinary support S and
d(q) the odd part of q+1. Write Ω for prime factors counted with
multiplicity. For an individual completion choose any positive jumps
u≡1 and w≡3 modulo 4, and let t count q in S for which v₂(q+1) is
odd and outside {u,w}. We give a uniform bound on a from c and t
alone; the choice of u and w may vary with the completion.

Divisibility (1) holds for every core. For each odd prime p the number
of q∈S with p|q+1 is at most v_p(c)+1. The number n₀ of q with
d(q)=1 is at most t+3: the exponent of a Mersenne prime is prime,
and at most 2,u,w escape the count. By (1),

    Σ_(q∈S) Ω(d(q)) ≤ Ω(c)+|S|.

Consequently at most Ω(c)+n₀ vertices have Ω(d(q))≥2. At most Ω(c)
others have d(q) prime outside S. If d(q)=r∈S, then r<q, and each r
can belong to at most one such q since r does not divide c.

Mark all vertices for which d(q) is not a single r∈S, and the t
exceptional vertices. Mark q if q≠1 modulo 3 or q≡0,4 modulo 5.
For d(q)=r∈S also mark q if r≠1 modulo 3. Each mod-3 screen costs at
most v₃(c)+2 marks, counting 3 itself. The mod-5 screen costs at most
v₅(c)+2. Finally mark the smallest q witnessing each of the jump
sizes u,w if such a q exists. In total there are at most

    K=2Ω(c)+3t+14+2v₃(c)+v₅(c)

marks. Each unmarked q has exactly one smaller predecessor r∈S,
and q=2^j r−1 with j=u or w: reducing modulo 3 forces j odd.
Keep exactly the edges entering unmarked q. They partition S into at
most K disjoint increasing paths, one starting at each marked vertex.

Fix M≥1 and put g_M(x)=2^[M(x+1)](x+1). Let G(M) be the result of
applying g_M five times starting from M. Suppose no marked vertex
lies in (M,L] with L≥G(M). Every prime of S at most L lies below
or at G(M). Indeed, its path prefix starts at a marked prime ≤M.
For every jump occurring in this prefix the smallest witness is a mark
≤M, so its jump size j is ≤M. After the first edge, residues mod 5 are
in {1,2,3}; the only transitions are 1→1 by u, 1→2 by w, 2→3 by u,
and 3→3 by w. A path prefix has at most five constant runs including
its first edge. Start a run of jump j at a prime p. The map
x↦2^j x−1 permutes the residues modulo p. Within p steps it returns
0 to 0, which would give a larger prime divisible by p. Thus the run
has fewer than p edges. From a starting prime ≤x it ends below
g_M(x). Five iterations give the assertion.

Now put R=2c/σ*(c). The full product B=β(S) equals R/(1+2^−a)<R.
Put B₀=β({q∈S:q≤G(M)}) and N(M)=2c·G(M)!. Cross multiplication
of B₀≤B<R gives the integer numerator gap

    1−B₀/R ≥ 1/(2c ∏_(q∈S,q≤G(M)) q) ≥ 1/N(M).                  (14)

Choose X=4(K+1)N(M)+G(M)+1 and L=2X+1. In the absence of a mark
in (M,L], the rest of the product uses only primes above 2X+1.
Along each path q_next−1≥2(q−1); starting above 2X+1 its whole
tail has reciprocal sum at most 1/X by a geometric series. Applying
subadditivity of reciprocal loss, (14) therefore implies

    1/N(M) ≤ 2^−a + K/X.

This is impossible if a>N(M)+1. Starting at M₀=1 put M_(i+1)=L
from this construction at M=M_i. If a exceeds max_(0≤i≤K)(N(M_i)+1),
all K+1 disjoint intervals (M_i,M_(i+1)] contain a mark, contradicting
the bound K. This proves the asserted effective exponent bound. It is
deliberately coarse, but needs no list of possible values of v₂(a).

Taking u=1,w=3, every counted jump is at least 5. Conversely a bound
on a bounds the number of q≡31 mod 32 by (a+1)/5, by the exact
2-adic balance. The equivalence follows. One can count only two kinds
of exceptional vertices: let t₀ count exceptional Mersenne starts and
t₁ exceptional vertices with d(q) a single member of S. Other
exceptions were already marked. The same argument then uses
K=2Ω(c)+2t₀+t₁+14+2v₃(c)+v₅(c). The two counts concern disjoint
vertices. No bound for them depending solely on c has been proved here.

22. The fourteen intervals are necessary for every completion

In section 19 the numerical bound can be sharpened to exclusion. At a
fixed nonempty sparse core and permitted h, every completion has

     l < R = (2^h+1)D/2^floor(log₂((2^h+1)D)) ≤ u c/σ*(c) < u      (15)

for its actual allocation [l,u) in (5). Thus a residual outside the
fourteen intervals excludes that height altogether. If its own dyadic
normalization misses the enlarged intervals J, the same conclusion
holds at every height with that residual. In particular for the
sequences D=D₀d^e of section 19, the density estimates there now count
exclusions. Each candidate core still requires its actual D_h at each h.

Let B and C be the simple-prime products of the variable group and of
its complement. Set ρ=σ*(c)/c>1 and z=2^(−a). The *full* unitary
perfect equation gives B(1+z)Cρ=2. Consequently B(1+z) is in (1,2).
By the group equation, B(1+z) is an integer power of two times
(2^h+1)D; it is therefore exactly R, for every exponent. Since B≥l
and C≥2/u by the compulsory blocks, (15) follows. The calculation
also applies with the true product of any additional known factors
in the complement in place of 2/u. The rational inequalities and the
uniqueness of the dyadic normalization are corrected_allocation and
unique_octave in WindowBounds.lean.

Another consequence of the group equation is D≤A, where
A=(2^a+1)/(2^h+1). Directly, A∏d(q)=D∏q, and every odd prime q has
d(q)≤(q+1)/2≤q. Thus b>1 gives D≤A<2^(a−h), since 2^(a−h)>1.
WindowBounds.terminal_le_seed proves the first inequality directly
from the distinct prime support and the integer group balance.

There is also a larger exclusion around a dyadic power that allows
arbitrary prime divisors of D. If

   |D/2^k−1| ≤ y/9  for some k,       Σ_(p|D)1/p ≤ y/9,     y=2^−h,

then b>1 is impossible at this h. Indeed the normalized group product
is B=(D/2^k)(1+y)/(1+z) in (1,1+2y) with z≤y³. Its least ordinary
prime exceeds Y=1/y: a smaller least prime cannot use any p|D, since
each p≥9Y. It would be Mersenne, and its shift cannot be Y since h
is even. A smaller Mersenne prime is at most Y/2−1, contradicting the
upper bound on B.

Partition into paths by choosing an ordinary predecessor whenever
available. By (13) the neutral-start paths contribute at most
(2275/3072)y, including their mixed successors. If r=2Y−1 is absent
the simpler y/3 bound suffices. All other starts have q+1=2^j d with
d>1 supported on D. Since q is 1 modulo 3, d fixes the parity of j,
and the sum of the budgets 2/(2^j d) is at most 4/(3d) for each d.
Writing s=Σ_(p|D)1/p, summing all nonempty ordered products gives
at most 4s/[3(1−s)]. On the other hand (6) gives loss at least
(8/9)y−3y². For 0<y≤1/65536 one has strictly

    (2275/3072)y + (4/3)(y/9)/(1−y/9) < (8/9)y−3y².

The two y/9 tolerances improve the y/128 tolerances of section 12.

Retain the assumptions of section 20, with b>1. Write e and f for

    t = 2^(−m)−e/2^17−f/2^19

24. A lower bound from the complementary primes

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 12, suppose a completion exists.
D>1 and D≤A force b>1. Thus m=14 and h≥64 by section 23. Write
C for the product of 1+1/q over the simple primes outside the variable
group. Put ρ=σ*(c)/c, S=2^−14 and y=2^−h. The exact balance is

    CρU(1+y)=1.

Since U≥1−S and ρ(1+y)>1, we have C<16384/16383. Every simple
prime in this group is therefore above 16383. On the other hand
U≤1/(1+S) and ρ≤1/(1−s²) with s=Σ_(p|c)1/p<1/4096. The latter
bound follows from 1−1/ρ≤Σ_(p|c)p^(−v_p(c))≤s². Hence

    1−1/C ≥ L = 1−(1+2^−64)/[(1+2^−14)(1−2^−24)].

Choose one ordinary predecessor whenever one is available in this
complement, using its own odd group balance. The paths are disjoint
and a path starting at q costs at most 2/(q+1) in reciprocal loss.
Every start has q+1=2^j d, with d supported on the core. If d=1,
the Mersenne exponent is at least 31. Indeed q>16383 and the only
candidates with exponent between 15 and 30 are 17 and 19. Both are
already used as repeated bases in D. All such starts together cost
at most 8/(3·2^31), summing over every odd exponent at least 31.

For d>1, q≡1 modulo 3 fixes the parity of j. All d with at least two
prime factors counted with multiplicity cost at most
(4/3)s²/(1−s), by summing ordered products. When d=p is a single
core prime, odd j is required for p≡1 mod 3 and even j for p≡2 mod 3.
The respective bounds are 4/(3p) and 2/(3p).

For p=8191 the choice j=1 lies below the cutoff and j=3 gives
7 | 8p−1. For p=131071 the choices j=1 and 3 are excluded by 11 and
13, respectively. These two singleton budgets can each be replaced by
1/(12p), summing over odd j≥5. At p=524287 keep the full 4/(3p).
For all higher Mersenne primes together, summing 1/(2^k−1)<2/2^k
over the odd k≥31 gives a further budget 32/(9·2^31).

Let s₁ and s₂ denote the sums of 1/p over the non-Mersenne core primes
in the two nonzero residue classes modulo 3. Subtracting the budgets
just obtained from L gives the strict inequality

    s₁ + s₂/2 > 9/2^18.

The exact rational comparison is complement_charges in MersenneMass.lean.
The primes 65537 and 67280421310721 are 2 modulo 3. The primes 137089
and 274177 are 1 modulo 3. Together their contribution plus 2^−16
is strictly below 9/2^18. This proves Theorem 14 with the same
weighting, hence also for the ordinary unweighted reciprocal sum.
If every non-Mersenne core prime were Fermat, all those outside this
list would be of the form 2^k+1 with k≥32; their reciprocal sum is
less than ∑_(k≥32)2^−k=2^−31. This contradicts the lower bound.

At h=64 there are at least seven distinct repeated primes in this
situation. In addition to the four Mersenne primes from section 23,
67280421310721 divides F and is repeated. At least one of 274177
and 137089 must also be repeated: if both are simple, the fixed factor
274177 | F forces 137089 through 274177+1. Next 137089+1 forces 5
in the fixed complement, below its cutoff of 16383. Theorem 14 now
forces another non-Mersenne base outside all three of these primes.

26. Residual products of Mersenne primes with multiplicities at most two

Consider the group of A=(2^a+1)/(2^h+1), or the first-layer group
A=(2^(hℓ)+1)/(2^h+1) for an odd prime ℓ|b. Write a' for a or hℓ
respectively, D' for the group's terminal divisor and B for its
simple-prime abundancy. Suppose a'>h and every prime factor of D'
is Mersenne and has multiplicity at most two. The first-layer charge
excludes D'=1, also for the entire quotient with b>1.

For p=2^k−1 dividing D' put x_p=2^(−k) and e_p=ν_p(D'). Set
S=Σ e_p x_p, U=∏(1−x_p)^e_p, y=2^(−h) and z=2^(−a')≤y³.
All k are odd primes at least 13. The group identity makes B the
unique dyadic multiple of (1+y)U/(1+z) in [1,2). We have
1−S≤U≤1/(1+S) and S≤2Σ_(odd k≥13)2^(−k)=1/3072.

Suppose first S≥y. Then B=2U(1+y)/(1+z). By section 17 the only
possible k below 79 are 13,17,19,31,61. Call the corresponding product
of factors (1−x_p)^e_p V and their weighted sum S₀. For the remaining
bases, both Σ e_p x_p and Σ e_p/p are strictly below ε=2^−76. Summing
4·2^(−k) over all integers k≥79 already gives ε. Delete the vertices
using these larger bases and their successors as in section 20. The
reciprocal loss is at most Σ e_p/p, and all compulsory block members
survive. The retained product is at least 2V(1−ε)² times (1+y)/(1+y³)
at h=16, or times 1 for h≥64. It is strictly below 2V(1+2^−16) or
2V(1+2^−64) respectively. At h=16 omit S₀+ε<2^−16.

Enumerate the three possible multiplicities of each of the five
small bases. Intersect these intervals with each compatible allocation
of section 8, requiring its included primes and forbidding the others.
Two verifiers close all 1080 nonempty tests. cubefree_certificate uses
the smooth tail, with 43,302 search nodes. verify_cubefree_residuals
independently uses individual factor tokens and the geometric tail,
with 198,910 nodes. Both use exact integer and rational arithmetic.

It remains to treat S<y. Every k exceeds h, since k and h have
different parity. Put Y=2^h, r=2Y−1 and t=8Y−1. If r divides D', it
occurs only once. The cases below give S≤37y/48, so 1<B<1+y and all
ordinary primes in the group exceed Y. Indeed, with w=(1+y)U−1,
one has y−S−y²≤w≤y and w>z. The reciprocal loss L=1−1/B satisfies

    L ≥ w−w²−z ≥ y−S−3y².

The first difference after multiplication by 1+w is w³+wz≥0.
To upper-bound the loss use the increasing paths of section 20.
For starts with odd shift part equal to p, the jump is odd since p
and the starting vertex are 1 modulo 3. There are at most two such
starts per p and their jumps are distinct. Their combined loss is
therefore at most 1/p+1/(4p). When p=r occurs in D', it has a single
copy. Jumps one and three are impossible by the divisibilities by
11 and 13 in section 20, so its singleton start costs at most 1/(16p).
The roots spending at least two core factors, counted with repetition,
cost in total at most T/100 with T=Σ x_p, by section 20. Distribute
this allowance as x_p/100 to each p. Since x_p≤1/65536, the resulting
allowances per base are

    (5/4)/p+x_p/100 ≤ (13/10)x_p,
       1/(16p)+x_p/100 ≤ (3/40)x_p    when p=r is used once.

A base in D' cannot be an ordinary start. For candidate starting
exponents h+7,h+9,... its removed allowance 2x_p covers its whole
charge. With unrestricted starts from h+3 onwards the same accounting
applies there. When r is ordinary, or is a core base, h+1 is prime.
Then h is a positive power of 16, h+5 is a multiple of 3, and the
sharper path bounds in (13) apply. The sum of 2x_p over all odd
candidate exponents starting at h+7 is y/48.

The second column bounds S/y and the third bounds L/y from above.

    least exponent k and presence of r,t         S/y             L/y
    k≥h+5                                        1/12            2275/3072
    k=h+3, r is not ordinary                     1/3             1/3
    k=h+3, r is ordinary                         13/48           17/32+1/48+13/80
    k=h+1, t does not divide D'                  25/48           1/3+3/80
    k=h+1, t divides D'                          37/48           1/48+3/80+13/80

For the first row sum the geometric series for e_p≤2. If r is
ordinary, (13) covers all charges, and otherwise the neutral budget

For the entire variable group, b=1 would give A=1 and D'=1 by

For a general residual D, the first-layer groups for distinct odd

    ω(b) ≤ Σ_(p|D, p non-Mersenne) ν_p(D)
           + Σ_(p|D, p Mersenne) floor(ν_p(D)/3).

The certificate functions are in erdos1052_mersenne_mass.py and

27. An early split for three top prime factors at every permitted height

Use B≥max(181,c) and C_B=c∏_(odd primes s≤B)s, as in section 18.
Take H=85,154,267 for h=16,64,128, respectively, and H=2h+40 for
h≥256. Suppose ℓ|b is prime with ℓ>max(2^h+1,2^H C_B), and
(2^(hℓ)+1)/(2^h+1) has exactly three prime factors. They are
distinct. On at least one descending path there is a prime Q with
two distinct odd prime divisors of Q+1, at least one exceeding B.
The shift valuations up to and including Q add to at most H. In
particular Q≥(2hℓ+1)/2^H. Section 18 covers the three small heights.

For h≥256 suppose no such split occurs. Section 18 gives p_i=1+2^m u_i
at the three roots, m=h−2, with m initial zero bits on each word.
At every completed edge the odd shift part is a single further large
prime. The digit test of section 18 therefore applies. Use the primes
5,7,11,13,19,29,37,61,181. Except at 7, two is a primitive root and
s−1≤254≤m, so u_i is zero modulo that sieving prime. Modulo 7 it
is in {0,1,2,4}. Since ℓ≡1 mod 3 under the no-split assumption
and 2^h has order 3 modulo 7, ∏p_i≡1 modulo 7. Also 2^m mod 7 is
1 or 4. There are seven unordered residue triples satisfying at
least one of these two product tests. Allow all seven at every height.

Write Z=2^m. The first two coefficients of

    ((1+4Z)∏(1+Z u_i)−1)/Z

are 4+Σu_i and Z(Σ_(i<j)u_i u_j+4Σu_i). The rest is divisible
by Z². The displayed number must vanish modulo 2^(hℓ−m) by the
root product identity. Read the first m binary columns of 4+Σu_i.
The state carries the integer carry, three digit states, and the low
56 bits of each u_i. Start the carry at 4. A row of bits x_i is
eligible if carry+Σx_i is even and each word permits its bit. Divide
that sum by two for the next carry. Sort entire word states together
to remove permutations.

After 56 positions the same transition acts at every step. The full
sets of states at 3000 and 4260 are equal, so every subsequent set
has period 1260. The actual positions start at m=254 and advance by
m↦2m+2. Reduce positions at least 3000 into [3000,4259]. At each
of the resulting phases start the next addition with the previous
carry plus Σ_(i<j)a_i a_j+4Σa_i modulo 2^56, using the three retained
low parts a_i. This is valid since m≥254. The following are the
numbers of additional bits until the entire eligible set is empty.

    phase  254  510 1022 2046 3030 3150 3306 3542
    bits    21   17   40   44   20   25   44   40
    phase 3782 3786 3794 3810 3842 3906 4034 4094
    bits    25   22   25   27   30   28   30   29

Thus 2m+44=2h+40 original positions suffice in every case.
uniform_three_prime_check in erdos1052_pair_chains.py verifies the
set equality and the complete phase cycle with exact integer
operations. PairChains.three_additions checks the product expansion
in Lean. Direct multiplication independently gives empty levels at
529 and 1037 bits for h=256 and h=512. The split can still spend a
factor at most B, and the result allows quotients with four or more
prime factors.

28. Cubefree residuals containing both Mersenne and Fermat primes

Use the entire quotient or any first-layer group of section 26, with
nonempty terminal divisor D'. Suppose its prime bases are all one unit
from a power of two and each occurs at most twice. Sections 15 and 26
exclude the cases of Fermat bases alone and Mersenne bases alone. Thus
both occur. Write U for the normalized Mersenne product of section 26,
G=∏_(q=2^t+1|D')(1+2^(−t))^ν_q(D') for the normalized Fermat product,
and B for the group's abundancy. The possible normalizations of
UG(1+y)/(1+z) have scale 1 or 2. Indeed the Mersenne mass is at most
1/3072 and the Fermat mass Σν_q(D')2^(−t) is at most 2^−14.

First either h≥64 and none of 8191,65537,131071,524287,2^31−1,2^61−1
divides D', or the group is impossible. To prove this, retain exactly
the bases in that list and enumerate their multiplicities in {0,1,2}.
Call the product of their normalized factors V. Every other Mersenne
exponent is at least 79. Since F₅ and F₆ are composite, every other
Fermat exponent t is at least 128. The total of e/p over all omitted
bases and their corresponding total dyadic mass are each below
ε=2^−75. Thus the omitted normalized product lies between 1−ε and
1/(1−ε). Deleting paths that spend these bases costs at most ε in
reciprocal loss. All required primes in the small blocks survive.

At each scale λ=1,2 use the lower bound λV(1−ε)², multiplied at h=16
by (1+y)/(1+y³), and the strict upper bound λV(1+y₀)/(1−ε) with
y₀=2^−16 or 2^−64 for the two height ranges. Intersect with all
compatible block allocations. Include every subset at h=16 and every
nonempty subset for h≥64. All 3534 nonempty tests close. The two
independent verifiers take 92,507 and 451,985 nodes respectively.
These counts are reproduced by cubefree_certificate(mixed=True)
and verify_cubefree_residuals(mixed=True).

In the surviving range every base is large, so scale 2 is impossible.
It would give B>2(1−ε). Each of 3,5,7,19,223 would have to belong
to this group, since otherwise its contribution in the complement
would exceed the available factor 2/B. All five survive deleting
every core-using path. The retained neutral product would exceed
2(1−ε)²>2−2^−35, contrary to the neutral gap in section 3.

Let t be the smallest exponent among the Fermat bases. We show t≤2h.
Suppose t≥4h. Now the total Fermat mass and the sum of multiplicities
divided by their bases are at most 4y⁴. Hence G≤1/(1−4y⁴)≤1+5y⁴.
If the Mersenne mass S is at least y, then

    1/U ≥ 1+S+S²/2 ≥ 1+y+y²/2 > (1+y)(1+5y⁴).

For the first inequality expand each 1/(1−x) through x², with a
separate factor for each copy. The quadratic terms have sum at least
S²/2. This puts the unscaled product below one, forcing the excluded
scale 2.

If S<y, the five rows of section 26 still apply up to an additional
4y⁴ of loss. In detail the normalized group has scale 1, and its
lower loss bound y−S−3y² persists since G≥1. Its product is below
(1+y)(1+5y⁴)<1+2y. Every ordinary prime in the group exceeds Y=1/y:
otherwise the smallest one has no possible core factor in its shift,
since all Mersenne exponents exceed h and t≥4h. It must be a Mersenne
prime at most Y/2−1, since Y−1 is composite. That one prime would
already exceed the upper bound for the product. The path estimates for
Mersenne starts now apply unchanged, including their impure edges.
Charge any additional path starting with a Fermat factor q at most
1/q to one of its copies. This costs at most 4y⁴. The table entries
s,l have l+1/120≤1−s in every row, hence

    ly+4y⁴ < (1−s)y−3y²

for 0<y≤1/65536. This contradicts the required loss. Since t,h are
both powers of two, the exclusion t≥4h proves t≤2h.

There is also a lower bound for every Mersenne exponent k in D'.
Put H=min(h,t), x=2^(−H). The Fermat mass is at most 4x and G≤1/(1−4x).
If k≤H−3 then U≤1−8x. Since (1+x)(1−8x)<1−4x, this again forces
the excluded scale 2. As k is odd and H even, we have k≥H−1.

The finite table excludes 65537. The published compositeness results
for every Fermat number F₅ through F₃₂ now give t≥2^33. Together
with t≤2h and k≥min(h,t)−1 this proves Theorem 18. The reported
results for F₂₀ and F₂₄ use compositeness tests; the local interval
checks do not rerun those tests.
https://www.fermatsearch.org/factors/composite.php

Because the first-layer divisors for distinct primes ℓ|b use disjoint
supplies, for any such entire cubefree residual D one also has

    D ≥ [(2^(2^33)+1)(2^(2^32−1)−1)]^ω(b).

Thus below this size a nonempty cubefree residual of these prime
types cannot occur in a sparse completion. The theorem permits arbitrary
prime bases in the original core outside that residual. The Lean lemmas
mixed_tail_gaps and mixed_first_negative check the two separation
inequalities above. Exponents at general sparse cores remain unresolved.
