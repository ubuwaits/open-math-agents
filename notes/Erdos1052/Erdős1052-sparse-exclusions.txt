Sparse odd cores in the unitary perfect number problem

Let n be unitary perfect and write n=2^a c ∏_(q∈S) q. The distinct odd
primes in S occur to exponent one. The core c contains the full powers
of the odd primes that occur at least twice. Write P for its prime
divisors, s=Σ_(p∈P)1/p and β(S)=∏_(q∈S)(1+1/q).

Theorem 1. Every nonempty core satisfies s>2^−38. If s<1/4096, the
Fermat-order restriction and interval certificates also force at least
two distinct core primes.
There is no unitary perfect number with core p^e for any prime p>4096
and any e≥2. Both e and a range over all exponents.

Theorem 2. For a sparse core (s<1/4096) with exactly two prime bases,
either v₂(a)=4 or v₂(a)≥20. The former requires 65537 and a second
base below 2^78. Several entire pairs of bases are excluded uniformly
in both core exponents and a.

Theorem 3. For every nonempty sparse core its least prime divisor is
less than exp(2^56), independently of the number of core bases. The
separate bound min(P)<2^38 |P| follows directly from
Theorem 1. Section 6 proves the absolute bound.

Theorem 4. Fix a permitted h and cancel the fixed odd seeds C and
F=2^h+1. Let D be the remaining divisor of c. Every odd multiplier b
satisfies D ≥ (2^24+1)^Ω(b). In particular D≤2^24 forces a=h.
Sections 8 and 9 give the compulsory small prime groups and the exact
finite certificate. They also bound a for many arbitrarily large D.

Theorem 5. At any fixed sparse core, exponent finiteness is equivalent
to a uniform bound on the number of simple primes congruent to 31
modulo 32. Given such a bound, the proof in section 10 computes an
upper bound for b. Whether sparsity alone bounds this count is open.

Theorem 6. If the remaining divisor D at h is a prime one unit from
a power of two, of any size, then a=h. A second sufficient condition on an arbitrary D is
that |D/2^k−1|≤2^(−h−7) for some integer k and that the sum of 1/p
over its distinct prime divisors is at most 2^(−h−7). Section 12
proves the Mersenne case and the second criterion. Section 13 proves
the Fermat case.

1. Input from the order and small-prime arguments

We use the following earlier sparse-core conclusions. All sparse
solutions have a≥48, and either 64 divides a or v₂(a)=4 and 65537∈P.
Writing h=2^v₂(a), the Fermat number 2^h+1 has a prime divisor p∈P
equal to 2 modulo 3, and the multiplicative order of 2 modulo this p
is exactly 2h. Every simple prime except 3,5 is 1 modulo 3. The set
S contains 3,5,7 and one of the supplier pairs

     (19,223), (19,433), (79,13), (79,223).

The first member supplies 5 in its shift, the second supplies 7.
For s≤2^−14 the only pair is (19,223). These conclusions and their
exact interval checks are in Erdős1052-core-625.txt §5,
Erdős1052-odd-part.txt §6 and Erdős1052-prime-square.txt §§5,8.

For q odd let d(q) be the odd part of q+1. The odd-part balance implies

     ∏_(q∈S) d(q) | c ∏_(q∈S) q.                                  (1)

For any finite set of allowed core bases P, remove all their factors
from each d(q), obtaining d_P(q). Then ∏d_P(q) divides ∏q. We allow
any multiplicity of those removed bases. For a prefix of S in
increasing order, record the unused ordinary primes in a set U.
Initially U is empty. The next prime q satisfies

     q+1 = 2^j b d,     j≥1, b supported on P, d | ∏_(r∈U)r.

Indeed any ordinary prime in d(q) is smaller than q and has only one
available copy in (1). On selecting q, consume the primes of d and
insert q in U. The exact partial product β is updated by (q+1)/q.

2. A finite interval certificate with no size cutoff on S

Draw an edge r→q when r is an ordinary predecessor of q in its shifted
factor. There is at most one outgoing edge per r by (1). Choose one
ordinary predecessor whenever any exists. This partitions the vertices
into disjoint increasing paths. When all remaining primes exceed y,
at most |U| paths starting in the remainder can use a predecessor in U.
All other paths start at primes q with q+1=2^j b and b supported on P.

An edge r→q has q≥2r−1. Put w(r)=(r+1)/(r−1). The identity
(1+1/r)w(2r−1)=w(r) shows that a whole finite path starting at r
contributes at most w(r). Since 1−1/w(r)=2/(r+1), subadditivity of
1−1/B under products B≥1 gives the following reciprocal loss bound for
the entire remaining product H.

    1−1/H ≤ 2|U|/(y+1) + L(P,y+1),
    L(P,x) = Σ_(j≥1, b supported on P, 2^j b>x) 2/(2^j b).            (2)

L is a rational number even though the sum has infinitely many terms.
For P empty it is 4/2^max(1,bit_length(x)). To add a base p, sum
p^−i L(P\{p},floor(x/p^i)) until p^i>x, then sum the remaining
geometric progression. The tail for powers starting at p^k>x is
2 p^−k ∏_(r∈P)(1−1/r)^−1.

Suppose the required interval is [l,u) and the current partial value
is N/D. If N/D≥u the branch closes. If a compulsory prime is still
missing, the next prime cannot exceed the least missing one.
Otherwise select a ceiling y such that the right side in (2) is
strictly less than 1−N/(Dl). Every continuation with all primes above
y, including the empty continuation, is thereby excluded. Branch only
on the finite list q=2^j b d−1 between the previous prime and y.
If a resource limit is reached or a qualifying partial value is found,
the verifier reports an inconclusive result. In particular a ceiling
never limits the primes permitted in a solution.

The script erdos1052_spectrum.py implements these rules with integers
and rational numbers. It rejects a candidate for compositeness only
by trial divisibility or a witness to Fermat's little theorem.
A pseudoprime surviving these tests adds branches. The powers of the
permitted bases are unbounded in (2). This is what permits a single
finite test to cover infinitely many core exponents.

An optional refinement chooses a predecessor in the suffix whenever
possible. Each path start then uses only ordinary primes of U, if any.
List all possible starts up to a farther ceiling. Charge each start
with an ordinary factor to its first used token. Distinct starts
must use distinct charged tokens. Use the largest individual charge for
each token, allowing 2/(farther ceiling+1) above the range, and sum
the separate charges for starts with no ordinary factor, with the smooth
tail in (2) beyond that ceiling. This bounds the possible loss for
each suffix of the candidate list. With sharpen_tail=True the neutral
and main 65537 gap checks take 112 and 1505 nodes, respectively.

3. A quantitative restriction on the entire repeated core

With P empty and 3,5,7,19,223 compulsory, the interval [2−2^−35,2)
is excluded in 362 nodes. Here the largest local ceiling is
1,139,725,879,647. For a contradiction suppose a nonempty core has
s≤2^−38. The sparse-core theorem gives 64|a since 65537 cannot be
a core divisor. The five compulsory primes all survive deleting the
vertices with p|q+1 for some p∈P and their successors. Such a deleted
vertex is at least 2 min(P)−1. The retained set G satisfies (1) with
core 1.

For clarity, the deleted product is at most 1/(1−3s/(1−s)).
A starting shift supported only on 2 and P contributes at most
2/(q+1) in reciprocal loss. Summing over all such starts with
nontrivial P-part gives at most 2s/(1−s). At any mixed start select
a simple predecessor r and a core divisor p. Its loss is at most
1/(pr). The r's are all distinct and Σ_(r∈S)1/r<1. This last sum
is bounded by β(S)−1. The mixed loss is at most s. Each successor
path is covered by the factor w(q) already charged at its start.

Also σ*(c)/c≤1/(1−s²) because the exponents are at least two. The
unitary-perfect equation gives

    2 > β(G) ≥ 2(1−s²)(1−3s/(1−s))/(1+2^−64) > 2−2^−35.

The final inequality at s=2^−38 is checked as a rational inequality.
This contradicts the neutral interval exclusion. Consequently s>2^−38
and min(P)<2^38 |P|.

5. Two repeated bases

There are also direct restrictions on the odd multiplier b when h=16,
valid without any ceiling on its prime factors. For every sparse core,
gcd(b,21)=1. If 3|b, then 193 divides 2^48+1 and therefore 2^a+1.
The prime 193 is simple and forces the simple prime 97 from its shift.
But 97+1=2·7² uses two copies of 7. If 7|b, 449 divides 2^112+1 and
its shift is divisible by 9, with the same obstruction at 3. In both
cases all primes used in the contradiction are below 4096.
In particular every sparse solution has a≥64.

With exactly two repeated primes one also has gcd(b,55)=1 at h=16.
If 5|b then both 414721 and 44479210368001 divide 2^80+1 and
therefore divide 2^a+1. Their shifts contain 11 and 71 respectively.
Neither 11 nor 71 can be simple, so both of the large primes would
have to be repeated alongside 65537. If 11|b write
(2^176+1)/65537 = 5304641·u. Both factors are 2 modulo 3, coprime
to one another and to 5·65537. Each forces a different repeated
prime. Consequently an exactly-two-base solution with v₂(a)=4
would require b≥13 and gcd(b,1155)=1. The case b=1 was excluded in
the earlier a=16 certificate.

At v₂(a)=6 the Fermat number is 274177·67280421310721. Both factors
are prime. The second, equal to 2 modulo 3, must be repeated. If
274177 is simple, its shift forces 137089. If that is also simple,
its shift 137090=10·13709 forces the prime 13709, which is 2 modulo
3 and therefore repeated. With only two bases the support is one of

    {274177,67280421310721}, {137089,67280421310721},
    {13709,67280421310721}.

At v₂(a)=12 the two primes 114689 and 26017793 both divide the Fermat
number, and both are 2 modulo 3. They fill the entire repeated support.
In fact the four named supports cannot occur for any a. Each omits
65537, so every possible exponent is at least 64.

Write (p,r) for a pair in the order printed, and e,f≥2 for the core
exponents. For a fixed e the simple product lies in

    [2/((1+p^−e)(1+r^−2)(1+2^−64)), 2/(1+p^−e)).

For all e≥E together use p^−E at the lower endpoint and 2 at the
upper endpoint. Taking E respectively 2,5,5,3 gives a certificate for
every e,f. At p=13709 all four supplier pairs must be checked. The other
three supports have s≤2^−14 and require only (19,223). The script
checks every listed interval and the primalities and divisibilities used
to force the two bases.

For an arbitrary *specified* sparse factorization the same program
provides exponent_bound(factorization,bits). Put R=2c/σ*(c). If for
each permissible supplier pair the simple products avoid
[R−2^−m,R), then a≤m. Indeed R−β(S)=R/(2^a+1), where 0<R≤2.
For instance, take c=114689²·26017793². Here R equals
8903985421153526735536129/4451992710915232720846850. With m=37
the gap test succeeds in 805 nodes, so a≤37. Both repeated primes
divide 2^4096+1 and have order 8192 for the base 2. The order theorem
would therefore force 4096|a, eliminating this fixed core. The test
can be reproduced with exponent_bound([(114689,2),(26017793,2)],bits=37).
A search that returns None leaves that input unresolved.

The same argument excludes all remaining v from 6 through 19. For
v=9,11,13,15,18,19 the two numbers in the corresponding SPLITS row
are in fact prime. The new Lucas certificates verify their primality
by recursive factorizations of p−1. They both equal 2 modulo 3, so they
are the entire core support. One interval from
2/((1+p^−2)(1+r^−2)(1+2^−64)) up to 2 excludes all exponents at each
pair. For v=7,8 the Fermat number factors as the first prime in PATHS
times respectively 59649589127497217 or 1238926361552897. The latter
factor in each case is a prime equal to 2 modulo 3 and exceeds 2^39.
The forced descending path in the former must meet a second core base.
All eligible vertices exceed 2^39 since vertices below 4096 cannot be
in a sparse core. Having both bases above 2^39 contradicts section 3.

For v=10,14,16,17 the PATHS row again requires a core base p outside
the required prime r≡2 mod 3 dividing the Fermat number. Each possible
last vertex in these rows is prime, which the program checks. Enumerate
all such r up to 2^38, 2^39, 2^40, 2^39 respectively. A sieve in the
progression r≡1 mod 2^(v+1), r≡2 mod 3 and an exact modular power test
leave only r=45592577 at v=10. The two supports there are excluded by
the same interval test as above. For a possible p>2^39 and an unlisted
partner beyond its bound, both exceed 2^39, contradicting section 3.
For each remaining p with r>R, delete the r-using shifts and their
successors. The retained set may use unlimited copies of p. Pure core
starts containing r cost at most 2p/((p−1)(r−1)) and mixed starts
less than 1/r. Hence the total reciprocal loss is below 4/(R−1).
The retained product lies between

    2(1−4/(R−1))/((1+p^−2)(1+R^−2)(1+2^−64)) and 2.

The verifier excludes this interval at each of the finitely many p,R.
The small Fermat-divisor enumeration was also run independently, testing
the same progressions without prime certification apart from division by
5,7,11,13,17,19. It returned the same four lists. Combining these cases
with the restrictions for v≤5 proves the stated alternative for exactly
two bases.

There is also an exclusion uniform in an arbitrary number of further
bases. When 65537∈P, their reciprocal sum t must exceed 2^−78. When
167772161∈P it must exceed 2^−44. To prove this, keep the indicated
base p and remove paths using any further core prime. The pure starts
with a further factor cost at most 2p t/((p−1)(1−t)) and the mixed
starts at most t. Thus their total reciprocal loss is below 4t/(1−t)
for positive t in the indicated range. The further part of the core
has abundancy at most 1/(1−t²). At t=0 section 4 already applies.
All five required small primes survive. The factor 16 divides the
exponent of 65537 even with the additional bases, since 3,5 are
already supplied and 17,257 are excluded from the entire support. The
intervals from section 4, with the new corrections at the lower
endpoints, again exclude all a≥80 and a=16,48. The other potential
small value a=64 would need a repeated prime equal to 2 modulo 3 in
2^64+1. This number is coprime to 65537 and smaller than 2^78, whereas
each additional base is at least 2^78. At 167772161 the single corrected
interval already covers every a≥48. For exactly two primes the
claimed bound on the other prime at v=4 follows immediately.

6. A uniform upper limit on the smallest repeated base

We prove a uniform form of the elementary upper-bound sieve. Let k be
even with Σ_(t|k, t odd prime)1/t≤1/4096. For any real L≥exp(16),

    Σ_(r>L, r and kr−1 prime) 1/r ≤ 50000/log L.                     (4)

Here are details accounting for uniformity in k. On X<r≤2X take
z=floor(X^(1/8)) and use the odd primes t≤z not dividing k. Each t
excludes two residue classes, since t|r(kr−1) precisely for r=0 or
k^−1 modulo t. For squarefree d on these primes the simultaneous
conditions have ν(d)=2^ω(d) classes, by the Chinese remainder theorem.
Their count in the interval differs from X g(d) by at most ν(d),
where g(d)=ν(d)/d. Put h(t)=g(t)/(1−g(t))=2/(t−2) and extend h
multiplicatively on squarefree d.

For the squarefree supported d≤z set G=Σ h(d). Choose weights λ_d
such that Σ_(d multiple of l) g(d)λ_d=μ(l)h(l)/G. Finite Möbius
inversion gives λ_1=1 and

    |λ_d| = (h(d)/g(d)) Σ_(m≤z/d, (m,d)=1) h(m)/G ≤1.

In this formula m is squarefree and supported on the sieving primes.
The inequality follows by counting all m times all divisors of d in
G and using h(d)/g(d)=∏_(t|d)(1+h(t)). Majorize the sifted count by
summing (Σ_(d|r(kr−1)) λ_d)^2. The main quadratic form is

    Σ_(d,e) λ_d λ_e g(lcm(d,e))
      = Σ_l (1/h(l))(Σ_(l|d)g(d)λ_d)^2 = 1/G,

because 1/g(gcd(d,e))=Σ_(l|gcd(d,e))1/h(l). The error is at most
Σ_(d,e≤z)ν(lcm(d,e))≤z^4. All r,kr−1 prime in the interval are
sifted because both exceed z.

To lower-bound G let Y=floor(sqrt(z)) and H=Σ_(n≤Y, n odd)1/n.
In the double sum H² remove pairs sharing an odd prime, pairs with a
nonsquarefree member, and pairs with a member divisible by an odd prime
of k. The fraction removed is at most

    3 Σ_(j≥1)1/(2j+1)^2 + 2/4096 < 3/4+2/4096.

Here 1/(2j+1)^2<1/((2j)(2j+2)) and the sum on the right telescopes
to 1/4. The retained pairs (m,n) have squarefree product d≤z and
are coprime. Their weights sum to at most G since h(d)≥2^ω(d)/d.
Thus G≥H²/5≥(log z)^2/80. For X≥exp(16) we have log z≥log X/16
and sqrt(X)≤X/(log X)^2. The sifted count is at most
21000 X/(log X)^2. Summation over X=2^j L, using the integral bound
for Σ_(j≥0)(log L+j log 2)^−2, proves (4).

Now suppose all p∈P exceed or equal L=exp(2^56). In a mixed
core-using shift choose a core prime p and an ordinary predecessor r.
The r's are all distinct, giving total reciprocal loss less than 1/L.
At a pure core-using shift choose a core prime r, and write
q+1=2^j d r. Summing over *every* such representation is an upper
bound. For fixed j and P-smooth d, formula (4) applies to k=2^j d.
The sum of 2/(q+1) over all these starts is therefore at most

    [2/(1−s)] · 50000/log L.

The harmless endpoint r=L can equivalently be avoided by taking
L=exp(2^56)/2 in (4), for which the same inequalities below hold.
In particular the total loss from these and the mixed starts is less
than 2^−38. Each successor path contributes at most its starting w.
The core itself contributes at most 1/(1−s/L), using e≥2.
Since 64|a, the retained ordinary product is at least
2(1−s/L)(1−2^−38)/(1+2^−64)>2−2^−35 and is strictly below 2.
The small supplier primes survive. For arbitrary s<1/4096, section 8
below proves that (19,223) is the mandatory supplier pair. This gives
the five primes required in section 3's neutral certificate and proves
Theorem 3. Alternatively the neutral interval is excluded for all four
original supplier pairs, in 112, 27, 13 and 44 nodes with the sharpened
tail. The size of each base alone does not bound their reciprocal sum.

The same sieve controls deletions when only the bases above L are
removed and smaller fixed bases are retained. For pure starts
the coefficient k may still use the smaller bases, with the same
uniform estimate. The results above give no upper limit on all the
other repeated bases or their exponents from a bound on the smallest.

7. The obstruction that remains at an arbitrary fixed sparse core

Fix one allowed h, let b=a/h, and cancel the fixed seeds consisting
of the odd part of σ*(c) and F=2^h+1. This fixes both their simple
primes and the residual divisor D of the core. With b unbounded, the
compactness argument in Erdős1052-prime-square.txt §10 gives an
infinite set T of simple primes of limiting product equal to the
unique dyadic normalization of DF in (1,2). The finite products tend
to this value because the path bound, after stripping the fixed core
bases, gives a uniformly negligible tail. A finite product has odd
denominator, whereas this target is a nonintegral dyadic rational.

More is true for the unstripped demands. For every X, with
T_X={q∈T:q≤X}, one has ∏_(q∈T_X)d(q) | D ∏_(q∈T_X) q. Indeed the
members of the prefix stabilize along the diagonal subsequence. Any
odd prime dividing a demand in this prefix is smaller than the prime
making that demand, so the supply needed on the right is entirely
contained in the prefix and the fixed D. Consequently at most Ω(D)
vertices anywhere in T can use a core factor. An ordinary vertex can
supply at most one other vertex.

If only finitely many Mersenne primes belong to T, it has finitely
many starting vertices because each start is a Mersenne prime or
spends a core factor. Choose one ordinary predecessor whenever possible.
The resulting paths partition T, since every backward sequence is
strictly decreasing. Every discarded ordinary edge consumes a source
which has no chosen successor. A path has at most one such terminal
vertex, and there are only finitely many paths. Thus only finitely
many vertices use multiple ordinary predecessors. Beyond all these
vertices and those spending a core factor, the paths split into finitely
many disjoint infinite chains plus a finite set. On each infinite chain

    q_(n+1)=2^j(n) q_n−1,    j(n)≥1.

After discarding 3 and 5 all q_n are 1 modulo 3, forcing each j(n)
odd. Write s_0=0, s_n=Σ_(i<n)j(i). Then

    α=lim q_n/2^s_n=q_0−Σ_(n≥1)2^−s_n > 0,
    ∏_(n≥0)(1+1/q_n)=(q_0+1)/α.

Each α is irrational. If α=A/B, the integers z_n=Bq_n−A2^s_n satisfy
0<z_n≤B and z_(n+1)+B=2^j(n) z_n. The upper bound uses the geometric
tail Σ_(m>n)2^(s_n−s_m)≤1. Given z_n, the exponent j(n) is unique
because B<2^j(n)z_n≤2B. The finite sequence of possible errors thus
enters a cycle. On any full period of its exponent sequence the
q-recurrence is an affine map with slope a power of two. Reduce this
map modulo the odd prime at the beginning of the period. It permutes
residues, so a positive number of whole periods brings the residue
back to zero. This would make that prime divide a strictly larger prime.

Hence failure of exponent finiteness at a fixed sparse core would
require either infinitely many Mersenne primes in T, or at least two
disjoint infinite chains as above whose irrational α's have rational
product. The latter conclusion uses the exact rational total product
and the finitely many discarded vertices. Excluding that product
for several disjoint chains is an additional open step.

A rational joint product also restricts how far all those chains can
jump simultaneously. Write ∏α_i=A/B, with k chains and B positive.
Truncate each chain at any index with cumulative exponent s_i, and
let t_i be the next cumulative exponent on that chain. Its truncated
value x_i=q_i/2^s_i exceeds α_i by at most 2^(1−t_i). Since ∏x_i
strictly exceeds A/B, its difference is at least 1/(B·2^(Σs_i)).
Telescoping the product on the upper side gives

    min_i t_i ≤ Σ_i s_i + log₂(2B Σ_i ∏_(j≠i) q_(j,0)).

This is a necessary restriction on any proposed set of multiple
infinite paths with the required product.

8. Compulsory small primes at every sparse core

Here and below s denotes the sum of reciprocal core bases. In deleting
the paths that use a core base, the bound in section 3 can be sharpened.
At a pure start q+1=2^j m with m>1 supported on P, one has q≡1 mod 3.
For each m this prescribes the parity of j. Hence the sum of 2/2^j
over the eligible j≥1 is at most 4/3. Summing over m costs at most
4s/(3(1−s)). For mixed starts choose an ordinary predecessor r and a
core divisor p. The predecessors chosen are distinct. None is 3, 5 or 7,
since these three tokens are already spent in 5 and the two small suppliers.
The sum of 1/r over all remaining ordinary primes is at most 3/32,
since their product of 1+1/r is less than 2/((4/3)(6/5)(8/7))=35/32.
The mixed starts therefore cost at most 3s/32 in reciprocal loss.
At s₀=1/4096 the retained neutral product is at least

    L(a₀)=2(1−s₀²)(1−4s₀/(3(1−s₀))−3s₀/32)/(1+2^(−a₀))

whenever a≥a₀. The three alternative supplier pairs each exclude
[2−1/1024,2) in the neutral test, and L(16)>2−1/1024. Thus 19 and
223 are compulsory for every sparse core. In particular 13 cannot be
simple since 13 and 223 would both use 7. For instance, at v₂(a)=6
the mandatory repeated base 67280421310721 is 8 modulo 13 and 2
modulo 3. Its exponent is divisible by 4: an odd exponent would
supply an extra 3, and an exponent equal to 2 modulo 4 would force 13.

Using a≥64, the neutral interval [L(64),2) yields the following further
certificates. Each row excludes a support omitting all the primes in

The five primes 3,5,7,19,223 are required in every row.

    extra required     all omitted       nodes (sharpened tail)
    none               31                  19

    31,991             1783                16
    31,607             3967,8191           20

    {3,5,19,607}, {7,223},      {31,3967}

9. A lower bound on the amount of core used by every prime-power layer

Put F=2^h+1. For the entire variable group A=(2^a+1)/F, write D for
its terminal divisor after the fixed seeds C,F are cancelled. Set
k=floor(log₂(DF)) and R=DF/2^k. Since DF is odd and exceeds 1,
1<R<2. If a>k, the unique normalized product in [1,2) is

    β(A)=R/(1+2^(−a)).

For any candidate interval [l,u) of (5), this rules out a>k if R≤l.
If R>u, it gives 2^a<u/(R−u). All the upper endpoints in (5) are
dyadic with denominator dividing 2^35, so a≤max(k,35) whenever R
misses all the closed intervals. Known membership of the fixed groups
discards any incompatible allocation of the small blocks. For instance,
h=16 and D=13709 give a≤29, hence b=1. More generally the simpler
condition 5/4≤R≤8/5 already gives a≤k: a group containing 19 has
product at least 32/19 and one omitting its block has product below 19/16.

For an odd prime l dividing b and 0≤i<v_l(b), consider the factor

    A_i=(2^(u·l)+1)/(2^u+1),       u=h·l^i.

Every such factor can be split simultaneously from (2^a+1)/F, by the
cyclotomic factorization proved in the large-prime note, sections 9
and 13. Descending allocation assigns pairwise disjoint ordinary
supports, even if two numerical seed factors have a common prime.
Their terminal divisors d_i have product dividing D. Section 13 of
that note proves d_i>1 for each of these prime-power indices.

We prove d_i>2^24. Otherwise d_i=p is prime with 4096<p≤2^24, since
two prime factors counted with multiplicity already exceed 4096².
Call its group product B. The odd balance says that B is a power of
two times p(2^u+1)/(2^(u·l)+1). There are two ranges for u.

If u=16, the exclusion 3∤b at h=16 gives 16·l≥80. Write
m=p·65537 and R=m/2^floor(log₂m). Since floor(log₂m)<80, we get

    R/(1+2^(−80)) ≤ B < R.

Every other possible u is at least 64: h≥64 or h=16 and the prime
dividing b is at least 5. Write T=p/2^floor(log₂p). Then
T(1+2^(−64))<2 since p<2^24. The normalized value is

    B=T(1+2^(−u))/(1+2^(−u·l)),       T<B<T(1+2^(−64)).

Intersect the corresponding range with (5), requiring every member
of the chosen blocks and forbidding every member of the complementary
ones. For remaining intersections run the finite interval certificate
of section 2, with one copy of p available in the shifted factors.
A shift that uses p exhausts the copy. Subsequent starts and the tail
bound then use the empty base set. Reject p itself as an ordinary prime
throughout. These rules follow from ∏d(q) | p∏q within this group.

The inclusive sieve through 2^24 examines 1,077,307 primes above
4096. There are 34,750 nonempty interval tests and all close in 594,109
nodes altogether. The separate verification enumerated the prime ranges
by scaling the interval endpoints, used ordinary token subsets and the
coarser tail (2·number of tokens+4)/(y+1), and closed the same 34,750
tests in 1,081,584 nodes. Neither implementation caps the simple primes
in the theorem. The reproducible certificate is small_terminal_certificate
in erdos1052_chain_bounds.py.

There are Ω(b) of the prime-power indices. Taking the product of
d_i≥2^24+1 proves Theorem 4, including b=1 by the empty product.
In particular a fixed core has only the allowed powers of two as
exponents if D≤2^24 at every allowed h. Writing Ω_large(D) for the
number of prime factors of D above 2^24 counted with multiplicity also
gives 2Ω(b)≤Ω(D)+Ω_large(D), since a single-copy terminal must be
large. These estimates leave the size of a single prime in b open when
a sufficiently large terminal divisor is available.

10. Counting simple primes in one residue class

On an infinite pure path above 5 let q'=2^j q−1. Both primes are 1
modulo 3, so j is odd. Write x=q mod 5. A successor is never 4 modulo
5 since 5 does not divide 2q. For x=1 the two possibilities j≡1,3
modulo 4 lead to x'=1,2, respectively. At x=2 only j≡1 is possible
and it gives x'=3. At x=3 only j≡3 is possible and it gives x'=3.
Thus either the path eventually stays at 1 with j≡1 modulo 4, or it
eventually stays at 3 with j≡3 modulo 4.

An eventually periodic sequence of jumps is impossible. Over a whole
period the map on the current prime is affine with slope a power of two.
Modulo the odd prime at the start of the periodic tail this is a
permutation. Iterating the period returns to residue zero and produces
a larger prime divisible by the starting prime. In particular no pure
infinite path has all but finitely many jumps in {1,3}. The same holds
for any prescribed pair of positive odd jumps, one in each class mod 4.
Theorems eventual_class_mod_four and escapes_two_jumps in Spectrum.lean
verify the mod-5 argument and this escape from each pair in Lean.

Fix h,D and t. Consider finite ordinary supports compatible with D
and containing at most t vertices with odd v₂(q+1) outside {1,3}.
There is no infinite such support satisfying the balance at every
prefix. Indeed there are only finitely many Mersenne starts: apart
from the even exponent 2, a Mersenne prime exponent is odd, with all
but the two designated values counted against t. There are at most
Ω(D) other starts with core demand. As in section 7, there are then
finitely many merges and core uses, leaving at least one infinite pure
path if the support is infinite. Its eventual jumps would be in {1,3}.

Here is a terminating way to extract a numerical exponent bound. At
each sorted prefix W let U=D∏_(q∈W)q/∏_(q∈W)d(q) and B=β(W).
The reduced denominator of B is odd, so B≠R for the nonintegral
dyadic target R of section 9. If B>R, abandon that prefix. If B<R,
choose an integer X>(2Ω(U)+4)(R+B)/(R−B). When the next prime is
above X, the reciprocal loss of the whole suffix is at most
(2Ω(U)+4)/(X+1): at most Ω(U) starting paths use a prefix token and
every other start is a Mersenne prime. The resulting total product
is at most (R+B)/2. Since its exact value is R/(1+2^(−a)) for a>k,
this possibility gives 2^a+1≤2R/(R−B). Otherwise branch only on
primes q≤X with d(q) dividing U, updating U to Uq/d(q) and charging
each exceptional jump. This tree is finitely branching and has no
infinite branch by the preceding paragraph, so it is finite by
König's lemma. The maximum of k and its local exponent bounds works.
The construction also permits using a provable upper bound for Ω(U).

A bound on the number of q≡31 mod 32 bounds the count just used,
since every exceptional odd jump is at least 5. Conversely the exact
2-adic balance is v₂(σ*(c))+Σ_(q∈S)v₂(q+1)=a+1. Thus bounding a
bounds the number of q≡31 mod 32 by floor((a+1)/5). This proves
Theorem 5 after taking the maximum over the finitely many h at c.

The finite-state calculation can allow additional odd jumps. With
j in {3,7}, reduce modulo 7,11,13,17,19 in succession. For {1,9}
use 7,11,13,17,19,23,29,31,37. Above each sieving prime p, both
0 and −1 are forbidden residues on a pure path, the latter because
the preceding prime exceeds p. The transitions are x↦2^j x−1.
Every infinite walk eventually stays in one strongly connected
component. A component with at most one internal label per vertex
can only give an eventually periodic infinite word. Keep only the
other components before adjoining the next modulus, counting distinct
labels even when they have the same destination. The numbers of kept
vertices are respectively (4,18,112,221,0) and
(4,26,87,783,5445,576,576,9099,0). This verifies the same terminating
exponent bound when only odd jumps outside {1,3,7,9} are counted.

The exact balance also sharpens the several-path limit in section 7.
Take a prefix beyond all finite paths, core uses and mergers, including
a last vertex q_i on each surviving path. Its residual U has the form
V∏q_i with V an odd positive integer. Each further path step replaces
its corresponding prime token by its successor. Let E₀ be the total
shift valuation of this prefix. After arbitrary further path segments
with exponent sums s_i, the product is 2^(E₀+Σs_i)D/(V∏q_(i,n_i)).
Sending all indices to infinity and using R=DF/2^k gives

    F V ∏α_i = 2^(E₀+k).

Hence the reduced numerator of the joint product is a power of two
and its denominator is divisible by F. Individual path irrationality
and the mod-5 restriction do not yet exclude this equality.

11. Bounded witnesses among the repeated primes

Put L=exp(2^128). At any permitted v=v₂(a) there are at least two
distinct repeated primes at most max(L,2^(2^v)+1). For 6≤v≤19 at
least three distinct repeated primes are at most L. These bounds
allow arbitrarily many other core primes and arbitrary multiplicities.

Delete all paths using a core base larger than L. At mixed starts the
total loss is below 1/L. At pure starts pick one such base r and write
q+1=2^j m r. For each j,m the sieve (4) applies at L and summing
2/(2^j m) costs at most 2/(1−s). Their loss is bounded by
100000/((1−s)log L). The abundancy of the deleted part of the core is
at most 1/(1−s/L). Even together these corrections retain a factor
greater than 1−2^−100 at the lower endpoint of each interval. The
same estimates apply for any higher cutoff. If the core has at most
t bases the alternative cutoff 2^104 t suffices: the reciprocal sum
above it is at most 2^−104, and summing over a designated large factor
in each start gives loss at most 2·2^−104/(1−s)+2^−104. In the
three-prime assertion also take the maximum with 2^(2^v)+1 to contain
all witnesses below the Fermat seed.

If only one base p is at most max(L,2^(2^v)+1), the Fermat seed's
required prime equal to 2 modulo 3 is p. All forced descending paths
from this seed are also below the cutoff. If p≥2^38, remove p from
the retained set by section 3, with s there replaced by 2^−38.
Even with the additional 1−2^−100 factor this contradicts the neutral
gap. For smaller p the sole-base Fermat sieve from section 4 applies
to this fixed seed and its descendants, leaving 65537 or 167772161.
At 65537 the seed gives v=4 and a≥80, and the known small-prime
constraints still give 16|e. At 167772161 we can use a≥64. The single
base spectrum intervals in section 4 absorb the 1−2^−100 correction
in either case.

For 6≤v≤19 the entire Fermat seed is below L. It already forces the
two small witnesses described in section 5. If these are the only two
bases below L, repeat the two-base argument there on the retained set.
If both exceed 2^39, their combined reciprocal mass is below 2^−38
and the corrected neutral gap applies. For an enumerated pair or a
single known base with partner above the stated bound, multiply the
lower endpoint from section 5 by 1−2^−100. Every resulting interval
is still excluded. The calculations, including the small-prime
certificates in section 8, are in supplier_and_large_base_certificates.
An upper bound for the entire repeated core and a proof that uniformly
bounds the single large primes in b for every sparse core remain open.

12. Mersenne residual divisors of arbitrary size

Let D=p=2^k−1 be prime in the group A=(2^a+1)/(2^h+1), with
b=a/h>1 odd. Here h is the allowed power-of-two part of a. Put Y=2^h,
y=1/Y, z=2^(−a)≤y³ and write L(B)=1−1/B for reciprocal loss. The
same proof works for an individual first layer with a=h·l and l|b prime.

First suppose k>h. Since k is an odd prime, d=k−h is odd. Put
δ=2^(−d), so 1/p=δy/(1−δy). Normalizing the group identity gives

    B=(1+y)(1−δy)/(1+z),           1<B<1+y.

The inequalities follow from δ≤1/2 and y≤1/65536. Every prime in the
group is therefore greater than Y. A lower bound on its reciprocal loss is

    L(B) ≥ (1−δ)y−3y².                                               (6)

Indeed, for x=(1−δ)y−δy² one has 0≤x≤y and
1−(1+z)/(1+x)≥x−x²−z. The latter inequality follows on multiplication
by 1+x, since x³+xz≥0.

Remove the one possible vertex using p in its shifted factor and its
successors. Its path costs at most 1/p≤δy+2y². The rest is neutral.
A neutral path can start only at a Mersenne prime above Y. If
r=2Y−1 is such a prime, h+1 is prime, hence v₂(h) is a power of two
and is divisible by 4 since v₂(h)≥4. It follows that h≡6 mod 10
and 2r−1=2^(h+2)−3 is divisible by 11. The next possibilities 4r−1
and 6r−1 are excluded by divisibility by 3 in the prime or its shift.
Thus a successor of r is at least 8r−1. The entire path costs at most
1−1/((1+1/r)w(8r−1))=5y/8. This also bounds its cost if r is absent.
The remaining neutral starts have exponents h+3,h+5,... and total
loss at most y/3.

If d≥3, the Mersenne start at exponent h+d is p itself. It cannot occur
in the ordinary group, so subtract 2δy from that last upper estimate.
The resulting bound is

    L(B) ≤ (23/24−δ)y+2y².

This contradicts (6) since 5y<1/24. If d=1, the start 2Y−1 is p
and is absent. The same check of 2p−1,4p−1,6p−1 shows that any
vertex using p is at least 8p−1. Its cost is at most 1/(4p).
Together with y/3 for the remaining starts this gives at most
(11/24)y+2y². This is precisely the same contradiction at δ=1/2.

Now let k≤h. Here B=2(1−2^(−k))(1+y)/(1+z), which is greater
than 2(1−2^(−k)) and less than 2. For k≥37 this forces the five
small mandatory primes through 223 into this group: if one of them
were absent its contribution in the complement would put the total
above 2. They survive removing the one possible p-using path. The
remaining neutral product is at least (1−1/p)B>2−2^(2−k)≥2−2^−35,
which contradicts the neutral gap in section 3. The case k≤24
is covered by Theorem 4. Between 24 and 37 the only prime exponents
are 29 and 31, and 233 divides 2^29−1. For k=31, h≥64. Set
T=(2^31−1)/2^30. Three intervals from (5) intersect
[T,T(1+2^−64)). With one copy of p available the finite certificates
close in 90, 83 and 18 nodes. This completes the proof for all p.

There is a neighborhood of the dyadic powers with a related exclusion
that allows arbitrary residual factorizations. Suppose D=2^k(1+ε)
with |ε|≤y/128, and put t=Σ_(p|D)1/p≤y/128. Assuming b>1, the
normalized group has B=(1+ε)(1+y)/(1+z) in (1,1+2y). Every ordinary
prime in it exceeds Y. If its least prime were at most Y, its odd
shift could not use any core prime since every p|D is at least 128Y.
It would be a Mersenne prime. The value Y−1 is composite since h is
even, and a smaller Mersenne prime is at most Y/2−1, already giving
product greater than 1+2y.

Remove the core-using paths. Section 3 bounds their loss by 3t/(1−t).
The neutral remainder has loss at most (5/8+1/3)y=23y/24 as above.
By monotonicity, (6) with δ=1/128 bounds L(B) from below. This gives

    (127/128)y−3y² ≤ L(B) ≤ (23/24+3/127)y,

impossible for y≤1/65536. Thus every allowed h passing either
criterion has only the candidate a=h. To conclude exponent finiteness
at a specified c, evaluate the criteria at each of its finitely many h.

Spectrum.lean proves (6), the strict contradiction in the Mersenne
case, and the prime-path escape theorem. LayerMass.lean derives the
multiplicative bound from the subgroup bounds and the original odd
balance, splitting the supports of all prime-power cyclotomic factors
in Lean. The finite interval computations are verified with rational
arithmetic in Python. The full fixed-core finiteness statement is open.

13. Fermat residual divisors of arbitrary size

Suppose D=p=2^k+1 is prime. Here k is a power of two and k≥16 by
sparsity. If k>h then k≥2h, so 2^(−k) and 1/p are at most 2^(−h−7).
The neighborhood criterion of section 12 gives b=1. Suppose instead
b>1 and put X=2^k. If k=h, the group's product is
(1+1/X)²/(1+2^(−a)). Every prime in the group exceeds X/2−1.
The Mersenne starts begin at exponent k+1 since X−1 is composite,
with total reciprocal loss at most 4/(3X). Any p-using vertex is
at least 4p−1, since 2p−1 is divisible by 3. It adds at most 1/(2p).
But X times the required reciprocal loss is at least (2X−1)/(X+1),
which exceeds 11/6, a contradiction.

It remains to handle k<h. Here h≥2k, and

    1+1/X < B=(1+1/X)(1+2^(−h))/(1+2^(−a)) < 1+2/X.

Every ordinary prime in this group exceeds X. Otherwise its smallest
prime would have to be a Mersenne prime at most X/2−1, contradicting
the upper bound. If 2X−1 were absent, the remaining Mersenne starts
would cost at most 1/(3X) and removal of the possible p-using vertex
at most 1/(2p). Their total is below 1/(X+1), whereas B>1+1/X.
Consequently 2X−1 is prime. Writing k=2^w, primality of 2X−1
forces k+1 prime and hence w a power of two. Thus 4|w. One obtains

    X ≡ 1, 1, 2, 9, 3     modulo 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 respectively.

For 7,11,13 use k≡1 mod 3, k≡6 mod 10, and k≡4 mod 12 respectively.
These five congruences suffice for the following finite bound. As in
section 2, partition the group into increasing paths by choosing an
ordinary predecessor whenever possible. The starts are Mersenne primes
and at most one prime having shift of odd part p. For the latter start
the shift valuation is even, since p≡2 mod 3. All pure ordinary edges
above the starts have odd shift valuations.

Represent a vertex by Q=2^t X+c, initially c=−1 with t odd for a
Mersenne start, or c=2^t−1 with t even for the p-using start. Across
a pure edge with jump j the new pair is (t+j,2^j c−1). In all cases
|c|≤2^t−1. Each such vertex costs at most 2^(−t)/(X−1) in reciprocal
loss. Use a horizon t=10 and measure losses in units 1/(1024(X−1)).
If the next pure edge crosses the horizon the entire suffix from its
next prime costs at most 2 units by the path bound. The same bound
holds at the first impure edge: beside the chosen predecessor the
shift has a factor at least X, and so its size is at least 2X².
This includes any p used at a later vertex.

For t≤10 set H(t,c)=0 if one of the five residues proves Q composite.
All vertices being tested exceed the sieving primes. Otherwise set

    H(t,c)=2^(10−t)+max({2} ∪ {H(t+j,2^j c−1): j odd, 1≤j≤10−t}).

Induction down from the horizon bounds each path, including paths that
end early. At the Mersenne starts t=1,3,5,7,9 the values are
(533,194,0,14,5). At the five possible p-using starts t=2,4,6,8,10
they are (266,117,27,6,0). Use the largest among these latter values
and the two-unit fallback for such a start beyond the horizon. All
Mersenne starts with t>10 together cost at most two units by the
geometric sum, since there is at most one start per exponent.
The complete group's loss is therefore at most

    (533+194+14+5+266+2)/(1024(X−1))=507/(512(X−1)) < 1/(X+1).

The lower bound on B gives the opposite strict inequality. This proves
b=1 in the last case. Spectrum.fermat_path_table verifies both finite
tables by kernel computation.
