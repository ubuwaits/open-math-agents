Erdős 1052 — partial results on the two hypotheses of reduction R2

The question asks whether the set of unitary perfect numbers is finite.
Write n = 2^a c ∏(q∈S) q. The odd core c contains the full powers of the
primes occurring at least twice. S consists of distinct odd primes outside c.
Let σ*(c) = ∏(p^e || c) (p^e+1), and let o(t) be the largest odd divisor
of t. For a > 0, put M = 2^a+1. Multiplicativity of the unitary divisor sum
gives the exact equation

    M σ*(c) ∏(q∈S)(q+1) = 2^(a+1) c ∏(q∈S)q.                       (1)

When a = 0, the factor M on the left is 1. R2 asks for a uniform upper
bound on c and a finiteness theorem for the exponents at each fixed c.
Neither bound in that generality follows from the results below.

1. A bound on primes of arbitrary size

Let m be an odd divisor of M. Taking odd parts in (1) gives

    m o(σ*(c)) ∏(q∈S)o(q+1)  |  c ∏(q∈S)q.                          (2)

Only divisibility is needed. Start by factoring m o(σ*(c)). A requested
prime p dividing c uses one of its available copies in c. A requested
prime p outside c must be in S and can be used exactly once. Each time
such a p is discovered, factor o(p+1) and include its demands. Process
the largest pending prime first. All odd factors of p+1 are strictly
smaller than p, so the procedure terminates. An excess demand proves
impossibility. Otherwise let F denote the forced primes in S and D the
unused part of c. Cancellation gives, for R = S minus F,

    ∏(q∈R)o(q+1) | D ∏(q∈R)q.                                        (3)

The current odd abundancy is A = (σ*(c)/c) ∏(p∈F)(1+1/p). If R is
nonempty and q is its least element, then d = o(q+1) divides D, since
all prime factors of d are smaller than q. Upon selecting q one replaces
D by (D/d)q, multiplies A by 1+1/q, and examines larger primes.

Suppose all the remaining primes exceed an integer x ≥ 1. Set
w(q)=(q+1)/(q−1). Then

    ∏(q∈R)(1+1/q) ≤ (1+2/x)^Ω(D) ∏(q∈R, o(q+1)=1)w(q),             (4)

where Ω counts prime factors with multiplicity. One proof uses the
completely multiplicative function G whose value at a prime p is 1+2/x
for p≤x, and p/(p−1) otherwise. For q>x we have
(1+1/q)G(q)=w(q). If d=o(q+1)>1, any prime p|d satisfies 2p≤q+1.
For p≤x we get G(p)≥w(q) from q−1≥x. For p>x this follows from
q≥2p−1. Thus G(d)≥w(q). Multiply over R, using w(q) as the extra factor
where d=1. Equation (3) implies ∏G(d)≤G(D)∏G(q), since G takes values
at least 1. Cancel ∏G(q), then use G(D)≤(1+2/x)^Ω(D).

The last product in (4) is over Mersenne primes q=2^k−1. For k<31 the
prime possibilities are 3, 7, 31, 127, 8191, 131071, and 524287. Composite
k gives composite 2^k−1. The few remaining exponents are checked by
trial division. Each factor w(q) equals 1/(1−2^(1−k)). For k≥K≥31,
the sum of 2^(1−k) is 2^(2−K). Therefore every finite product of these
factors is at most 1/(1−2^(2−K)), by ∏(1−t)≥1−Σt for 0≤t≤1.
Taking K=max(31, floor(log2(x+1))+1) skips roots already below x.
This gives a rational bound U(x,D) valid with arbitrarily large primes.

To exclude a target interval [L,H), first discard a branch with A≥H.
Find y≥x such that A U(y,D)<L. This handles every continuation whose
least remaining prime exceeds y, including the empty continuation.
The other branches are exactly the possible primes q in (x,y] of the
form 2^j d−1 with j≥1 and d|D, excluding primes of c or F. Recurse with
the updated A,D and lower endpoint q. The search has a finite ceiling
for y. If a suitable y cannot be certified, the checker returns an
inconclusive result. All bounds, decisions and primality tests use exact
arithmetic. The ceiling for y never bounds the primes in a solution.

2. Fixed cores up to one hundred million

There are 7,857 odd powerful c≤10^8, including 1. Enumerate them without
duplication as x²y³ with y odd and squarefree and x odd. For every a≥20,
the required odd abundancy is in [2/(1+2^(−20)),2). The counts, in the
order in which each core first receives a certificate, are

    7,391   incompatible demands already at m=1
       14   forced odd abundancy at least 2
      396   excluded by the interval certificate at m=1
       25   excluded after dividing the exponents into classes
       31   unresolved by this check.

For v=v₂(a)=0,...,5, take m=2^(2^v)+1, which divides M since a/2^v
is odd. For 64|a, M≡2 mod 3, so some prime p|M is 2 mod 3. Every such
p exceeds 1024. For each prime p≤1024 in this residue class one checks
2^(64k)≠−1 mod p for 0≤k<p−1, which covers all k by Fermat's theorem.
If such a p could divide c, the checker leaves this class unresolved.
Otherwise p∈S. Cancel p against its occurrence in M, drop p from S,
and use 3|o(p+1). This yields (2) with m=3 for the reduced S, even
though 3 does not divide M. Its odd abundancy is less than 2. A
certificate excluding [1024/513,2) now suffices because

    (1024/513)(1+1/1024)(1+2^(−64)) < 2.

For a≤19, exact descending cancellation for each pair (a,c) yields
only (1,1), (2,1), (6,1), (1,9), and (18,625). Specifically, the
largest odd prime with an outstanding valuation in (1) must belong to S
with multiplicity one, since any shifted factor q+1 supplying it would
come from a larger q. Remove the prime, add the demands of q+1 and
continue downwards, finally testing the valuation at 2. Thus H2 holds
for 7,826 specified cores in this range. The script prints the other 31.

3. The core 625

Since 625+1=2·313, 313+1=2·157, 157+1=2·79 and 79+1=16·5, the
primes 313,157,79 are forced, leaving D=125 and A=128/125 at m=1.
At m=3 the interval check proves A ∏(q∈R)(1+1/q)<1024/513. Thus
odd a≥9 are impossible, and the smaller exponents can be checked
directly. For v₂(a)=2,3,4,5 the Fermat divisors in section 2 give
excess demand. For 64|a the preceding artificial m=3 argument applies,
since 5 cannot divide M. Hence every solution has a=2k with k odd.

When t|k, 2^(2t)+1 divides M. For t=7,11,13,17,19,23,29,31 this
produces an excess demand. For t=5 the odd abundancy is strictly below
2/(1+2^(−10)), whereas a≥10 would require at least that much. Thus k
is coprime to the product of these nine primes.

Suppose 18|a. The only case left is a=18j for odd j. The divisor
m=2^18+1 forces F={3,7,11,13,19,37,79,109,157,313}, D=1 and
A=2^19/(2^18+1). At j=1 these primes give the known solution
n=146361946186458562560000. If j>1 then a≥54, and the additional
primes must supply

    (1+2^(−18))/(1+2^(−a)).

In particular every additional prime q exceeds 2^18. To sharpen (4),
choose a predecessor prime dividing o(q+1) for each q with o(q+1)>1.
By (3) with D=1 each predecessor can be used only once. This partitions
the primes into upward paths starting at Mersenne primes. Along an edge
r→s, s≥2r−1. Since (1+1/r)w(2r−1)=w(r), a whole path starting at r
contributes at most w(r).

The only possible starting exponent 19≤k<31 is k=19. Write p=2^19−1
and q=2p−1. The path from p either has no successor, has a successor
at least 4p−1, or has successor q. In the last case its third prime,
if present, is at least 4q−1, because 2q−1=2097145 is divisible by 5.
Consequently an upper bound for the product of all the additional primes'
abundancies is

    max((1+1/p)w(4p−1), (1+1/p)(1+1/q)w(4q−1)) / (1−2^(−29))
    = 1125899906842624/1125896146649095
    < 68719476736/68719214593 = (1+2^(−18))/(1+2^(−54)).

This proves uniqueness on all multiples of 18.

There is also a restriction on the simple primes at this core. For a≥20,
if 3 is absent then every simple prime is 1 mod 3, since a prime equal
to 2 mod 3 would force 3. The interval check at m=5 excludes this case.
Exactly one simple prime p≡2 mod 3 supplies the factor 3. Indeed, (1)
has exactly one 3 on its right and neither M nor σ*(625) supplies it.
Follow p upwards through the unique prime whose shifted factor supplies
it, until reaching a seed. This path cannot end at a prime supplied by
σ*(625), since those downward demands have already been exhausted at
313,157,79,5. Removing the path therefore cancels its terminal prime
against M/5. Retain the 3 from p+1. The remaining support satisfies
(2) with m=15 because the other odd factors removed from the path can be
discarded by divisibility. Its abundancy is less than
367001600/185598129 = 2/((1+2^(−20))w(176)), as verified by the
interval certificate. The removed path contributes at most w(p), so
p<176. Removing only p's proper ancestors gives (2) with m=5p even
if p itself was terminal. Testing divisibility at the primes p<176
leaves 11,23,29,41,47,59,83,113,149,167. Any proper ancestors of p
start at a prime at least 2p−1, so they contribute at most w(2p−1).
The interval check at m=5p rules out 41,83,113,149,167 even after
allowing this factor. Thus p belongs to {11,23,29,47,59}.

4. A restriction on prime-power cores with arbitrarily large base

If c=p^e for an odd prime p>65537 and e≥2, any unitary perfect
completion must satisfy all four conditions

    64 | a,    p | 2^a+1,    p ≡ 2 mod 3,    e even.                 (5)

Here is the estimate that removes dependence on e. Suppose a set S of
simple primes satisfies

    m ∏(q∈S)o(q+1) | p^e ∏(q∈S)q,

where every prime dividing m is less than p, and its simple abundancy
is less than 2. For a prime r∈S draw an edge to q∈S whenever r|q+1.
Each vertex has at most one outgoing edge by the displayed divisibility.
Delete all q with p|q+1 and all their successors. Every deleted prime
is at least 2p−1, so no prime of m is deleted. In the retained set G no
shifted factor uses p or a deleted vertex. Cancelling prime by prime gives

    m ∏(q∈G)o(q+1) | ∏(q∈G)q.

To bound what was deleted, start a path at each q with p|q+1 and use
the path estimate w(q) from section 3. Overlap only increases the
upper bound. For starts with q+1=2^j p^i, j,i≥1, summing 2/(q+1)
over all such pairs gives at most 2/(p−1). At any other start, choose
an odd prime r≠p dividing q+1. It belongs to S and is chosen at most
once. Then 2/(q+1)≤1/(pr). Since 1+Σ(r∈S)1/r≤∏(r∈S)(1+1/r)<2,
these mixed starts contribute at most 1/p. Using
w(q)=1/(1−2/(q+1)) shows the deleted abundancy is at most
1/(1−3/(p−1)). Including σ*(p^e)/p^e gives an overall correction
bounded by E=(1+1/65537²)/(1−3/65536).

For 1≤a<20 take m=2^(2^v)+1 at v=v₂(a), and put T=2/(1+2^(−a)).
The good set G would have abundancy in [T/E,T), where the strict upper
endpoint follows already from σ*(p^e)/p^e>1. The interval checker
excludes this interval in each case. R2's small-exponent classification
also excludes a=0. For a≥20 and v₂(a)≤5 use m=3,5,17,257,65537,641
respectively (641 divides 2^32+1). Each good set with abundancy below 2
has abundancy below 1024/513, by the same checker. Even after applying
E and the factor contributed by 2^a, the result is below 2.

For 64|a choose a prime r|M congruent to 2 mod 3. If r≠p, remove r
and reserve 3 as in section 2, then apply the estimate at m=3 to this
smaller support. This is impossible because

    (1024/513) E (1+1/1024)(1+2^(−20)) < 2.

Hence p itself divides M and is 2 mod 3. If e were odd, 3 would
divide p^e+1. Its factor 3 could be used directly as m=3, leading to
the same contradiction without removing r. This establishes (5).
More precisely, F_v=2^(2^v)+1 with v=v₂(a) divides M and is 2 mod 3.
It therefore has a prime factor equal to 2 mod 3. Since the argument
applied to every such prime factor of M, that prime must be p. Thus
each v≥6 allows at most one base p>65537 for any prime-power core.

There is a separate obstruction valid for general cores. If an odd
prime r outside c satisfies r²|1+t^v for some prime power t^v || c,
then (1) demands two copies of r while the right side supplies at most
one. In particular, if 3 does not divide c, then v₅(c) cannot be
6k+3. This follows from 9|5³+1 and (2k+1) being odd.

5. An additive height removes the entire simple support

The following results hold for every fixed odd core c. No sparseness
of its primes and no restriction on v₂(a) is needed. For the completely
additive integer function W on the positive integers set W(2)=1 and
W(p)=1+W((p+1)/2) for every odd prime p. These rules define W by
induction, since (p+1)/2<p. In particular W(p)=W(p+1). This is
Defant's function D associated with the modified Dedekind psi function.
Colin Defant, “An arithmetic function arising from the Dedekind ψ
function”, Mathematical Journal of Okayama University 59 (2017), 81–92.
https://doi.org/10.18926/mjou/54715

Define the integer K(c)=1+W(c)−W(σ*(c)) and f(t)=W(2^t+1)−t. Applying
W to (1) cancels every W(q+1) against W(q). Therefore every positive
exponent at the core satisfies the exact equation

    f(a)=K(c).                                                       (6)

For every positive n we have n≤2^W(n). At a composite n multiply the
bounds for its proper factors. For an odd prime p apply the bound to
(p+1)/2 to get p+1≤2^W(p); the cases 1 and 2 are immediate. Thus
f(t)≥1, and K(c)≤0 already excludes the core.

Write a=hb with b odd; here h can be any positive integer. If b has
prime factorization ∏ p_i^e_i, set

    E(b) = #{d|b : d>1 and ω(d) is even}
         = (∏(1+e_i)+∏(1−e_i))/2−1.

Then for every such pair, without any unitary-perfect hypothesis,

    f(hb) ≥ f(h)+E(b).                                              (7)

For a proof put Y=2^h, A_d=|Φ_d(−Y)|. Their product over d|b is
2^(hb)+1, with A₁=Y+1. Fix d>1. Set m=rad(d), u=d/m, y=Y^(−u).
The identity Φ_d(X)=Φ_m(X^u) follows by considering the orders of the
roots, since every prime dividing u divides m. Since u is odd, Möbius
inversion gives

    A_d/Y^φ(d) = (1+y)^μ(m) ∏_(e|m, e>1)(1+y^e)^μ(m/e).

Put t=Σ_(odd e≥3)y^e=y³/(1−y²)≤y/3 since y≤1/2. The product after
the first factor lies between 1−t and 1/(1−t), by multiplying out
∏(1−y^e)≥1−Σ y^e and using 1+y^e≤1/(1−y^e).
We have (1+y)(1−t)>1, (1−t)/(1+y)>1/2 and (1+y)/(1−t)<2.
Consequently A_d is strictly between Y^φ(d) and twice that number
when ω(d) is even, and strictly between half that number and Y^φ(d)
when ω(d) is odd. The size bound on W and its integrality give

    W(A_d) ≥ hφ(d) + 1_(ω(d) even).

Sum this over d>1 and use Σ_(d|b)φ(d)=b to obtain (7). The formula
for E(b) follows by adding the total divisor count to its signed
count by parity of the number of distinct prime factors.

For a≥1 at a fixed core take h=2^v₂(a), so b is its odd part. When
r=ω(b)≥1 the even-cardinality squarefree indices give

    2^(r−1) ≤ K(c)−f(h)+1 ≤ K(c).                                 (8)

If r≥2 the two-prime indices alone give Σ_(i<j)e_i e_j≤K(c)−f(h).
The left side is at least Σe_i−1: use e₁e₂≥e₁+e₂−1, then for each
additional i the terms involving e₁ already supply e_i. In particular

    Ω(b) ≤ K(c)−f(h)+1 ≤ K(c)       whenever ω(b)≥2.                (9)

This proves that admissible exponents have natural density zero at
every fixed core (also for the union of all cores having K(c) bounded
by a fixed constant). Here is an elementary density argument. For
finitely many odd primes count their divisibilities in a uniform
residue class modulo their product. The Chinese remainder theorem
makes the indicators independent with mean sum M=Σ1/p and variance
at most M. For any fixed R<M the fraction divisible by at most R of
these primes is at most M/(M−R)² by Chebyshev's inequality. These
are periodic conditions on the exponent, so their residue fractions
bound its upper natural density. The sum of reciprocal odd primes
diverges, and (8) supplies a fixed R.

There is an effective finiteness statement for prime powers even
without any restriction on the core. Fix h and an odd prime r.
Choose a positive integer C with 2^C≥4(r−1). Put

    L = lcm{φ(hr+i) : 1≤i≤C, r does not divide i},

using 1 for an empty lcm. For every e≥2,

    f(hr^e) ≥ f(h)+floor((e−2)/L).                                 (10)

For j=1+nL with n≥1 set s=hr^j. All s+1,...,s+C are composite.
If r|i then r properly divides s+i. Otherwise Euler's theorem gives
hr^j+i≡0 modulo hr+i, and s+i>hr+i>1. For the corresponding layer
A=(2^(sr)+1)/(2^s+1), (7)'s size estimate gives W(A)≥(r−1)s.
Suppose equality holds. Expand each prime factor of A, counting its
multiplicity, into a finite tree. At q write q+1=2^j_q∏children q'.
Use every odd prime of the odd shift part with its multiplicity as a
separate child. The children are smaller than q. The height recursion
gives Σ j_q=W(A) over all vertices. Multiplying the local equalities
also gives

    ∏vertices(1+1/q)=2^W(A)/A=(1+2^(−s))/(1+2^(−sr)).

Every vertex therefore exceeds 2^s. Each leaf is a Mersenne prime
2^k−1 with k>s and contributes k units of W(A), so there are at
most r−1 leaves. The composite-exponent interval implies k≥s+C+1.
Partition all occurrences into paths by keeping one child at each
non-leaf. Each path ends at a different leaf, even if the same prime
has several occurrences. A path starting at 2^k−1 in increasing
order contributes at most 1/(1−2^(1−k)), by the telescoping
identity of section 3. Thus the full reciprocal loss is at most
(r−1)2^(−s−C)≤2^(−s)/4. But with y=2^(−s) it equals
(y−y^r)/(1+y)≥y(1−y)≥y/2. This contradiction forces at least one
extra height unit at every selected j. All other r-power layers have
nonnegative excess, and counting 1+nL<e proves (10).

Thus for any fixed c,h and any specified finite set of allowed odd
prime divisors, the possible b form an effectively bounded finite set.
For example, if r^e|b then (7) gives f(hr^e)≤K(c), so (10) gives
e≤L(K(c)−f(h)+1)+1. At a fixed sparse core the earlier v₂ restriction
makes the list of h finite. An unbounded largest prime remains possible
in these inequalities, in particular when b=ℓ itself.

As checks, K(1)=1 and K(625)=3. For the 7857 odd powerful cores
through 10^8 the budgets range from −4 to 6; 2229 are at most zero.
The script erdos1052_additive_height.py computes these exact values.
AdditiveHeight.lean constructs W and verifies complete additivity,
cancellation in (6), the size bound, the two Möbius comparisons and
the summation of the cyclotomic height costs. The cyclotomic
identification, prime-tree argument of (10) and density argument
are given in the written proof.

6. A uniform bound on the total number of odd prime factors of a

The bound here holds at every fixed core, including dense cores, with
no restriction on v₂(a). Put P=max({1}∪{p:p|c, p prime}). Let J be
the least nonnegative integer with 3^J≥32 and 2^(3^J)≥P. For every
positive exponent a at c, write h=2^v₂(a), b=a/h. Then

    K(c) ≥ f(h)+E(b)+Σ_(r^e||b) floor(max(e−J,0)/2),              (11)
    Ω(b) ≤ J+2K(c)−1.                                             (12)

Together with the earlier sparse-core restriction on h, (12) reduces
exponent finiteness there to bounding the largest odd prime divisor.
For dense cores it controls the odd part's multiplicity even if h is
unbounded. Equation (9) is stronger whenever b has two distinct primes.

To see the extra term in (11), fix r^e||b. For 0≤i<e put s=hr^i,
Y=2^s and A=(Y^r+1)/(Y+1). This is the evaluated cyclotomic factor
at r^(i+1). Its baseline height in (7) is (r−1)s. Descending-prime
allocation, as in section 1 of Erdős1052-odd-part.txt, gives an
assigned residual d|c, a squarefree subset T of simple primes, and

    A·∏_(q∈T)(q+1) = d·2^B·∏_(q∈T)q,       B=Σ_(q∈T)v₂(q+1).

Apply W to obtain W(A)=W(d)+B. Hence

    2^W(A)/A = (2^W(d)/d) ∏_(q∈T)(1+1/q).

Suppose W(A)=(r−1)s. The left side is (1+1/Y)/(1+1/Y^r), strictly
below 1+1/Y. At a divisor p|d the ratio 2^W(d)/d is at least
(p+1)/p: use complete additivity, 2^W(n)≥n and 2^W(p)≥p+1 for
odd primes. Consequently if Y≥P we must have d=1. All primes in T
exceed Y, and T breaks into paths starting at distinct Mersenne
primes. Its required reciprocal loss is at least y(1−y) for y=1/Y.

For s≥32 the baseline is impossible whenever s+1 is composite.
Indeed every starting exponent k is an odd prime with k≥s+2, and
the sum of all potential path losses 2^(1−k) is at most 2y/3.
This already covers every odd s in the range.

It is also impossible when s is even and s≡1 modulo 5. The potential
start q₀=2Y−1 has successor at least 8q₀−1, if it has one. Indeed
s≡6 mod 10, so 2q₀−1=2^(s+2)−3 is divisible by 11. The candidate
4q₀−1 is divisible by 3. A successor 6q₀−1 would spend a factor
3 in its shift, impossible with d=1 and every group prime above Y.
By the telescoping path bound its loss is at most 5y/8, including
the case where the start is absent. All other start exponents are
odd and at least s+3, with total path loss at most y/3. But
(23/24)y<y(1−y) for s≥32.

For i≥J all required size inequalities hold. Every two successive
such indices contain one of these obstructions:

  • h=1 gives odd s at every index.
  • If v₂(a) is odd and r≠3, then s≡−1 mod 3 at every even i.
    At r=3 and h even the cubic argument below works at every i≥J.
  • If v₂(a) is positive and even and r≠5, then h≡±1 mod 5 and
    r^i≡±1 mod 5 at every even i. The negative residue makes s+1
    composite; the positive residue uses the second path obstruction.
    At r=5 each odd i gives s≡−1 mod 3.

For the cubic argument, with s=h3^i even and i≥J, the smallest
ordinary prime of the neutral group must be a Mersenne start at
least 2Y−1. The integer A=Y²−Y+1 divides the squarefree product of
the ordinary primes and is less than (2Y−1)², so it must itself be a
group prime. Put Q_j=1+2^j(Y−1). Starting at Q_s=A, the odd part of
Q_j+1 is Q_(j−1) for j≥2. This too divides the squarefree product
and is below (2Y−1)², forcing each Q_j down to Q₁ to be prime.
But 2^h+1 properly divides Q_(2h−1), since Y≡−1 modulo 2^h+1 and
2h−1≤s. This contradiction handles r=3.

Such a layer costs at least one extra integer unit of height. Its
prime-power index is distinct from every index counted in E(b).
Adding the charges proves (11). If b is a prime power r^e, then
e≤J+2(K(c)−f(h))+1≤J+2K(c)−1. Otherwise (9), or b=1, gives (12).

For every core through 10^8, J=4 and K(c)≤6. In particular every
admissible a in this range has at most 15 odd prime factors counted
with multiplicity, and at most six if two distinct odd primes occur.
These statements allow arbitrarily large 2-adic parts and primes.
The Lean lemmas divisor_cost, neutral_loss_gap and two_step_count
verify the individual cost comparison and integer estimate. The
allocation of the paths and the residue cases are the written proof.

At a sparse core the previous prime-power result improves (12).
Indeed 16 divides each permitted h. Section 13 of the large-prime
note excludes a neutral group for every prime-power layer at such
an h. Thus every index i≥J costs an extra height unit by the residual
size test. This gives Ω(b)≤J+K(c)−1 via the prime-power case or (9).

One can also test a fixed sparse core directly at a permitted h.
If K(c)<f(h), (7) excludes that height. Suppose every prime of the
remaining divisor D at h is at most 2^h. Every prime-power layer now
passes the size test even at i=0. All Ω(b) layers cost an extra unit,
giving

    K(c) ≥ f(h)+E(b)+Ω(b).

In particular, at equality K(c)=f(h) the only candidate is a=h.
Checking these inequalities at the finitely many permitted heights
proves exponent finiteness for each core passing the stated test.
For example c=65537² has sole permitted height 16 by the order of
2 modulo 65537. Here W(65537)=18 and W(65537²+1)=36, with
65537²+1=2·5·8681·49477. Hence K(c)=1<f(16)=2, excluding the core.

7. No odd prime divides an exponent at the repeated core 25

Suppose n is unitary perfect and 5² is its only repeated odd prime
power. Then a=v₂(n) is a power of two, a=2^v with v≥20. Moreover
2^a+1 must be squarefree and composite. Core 25 was the smallest
core remaining in the finite interval classification of section 2.
The assertion bounds its odd multiplier at every height. The possible
indices v in the last assertion have no known upper bound here.

The factors 25+1=2·13, 13+1=2·7 and 7+1=8 force the simple primes
13 and 7. Together with 25 they contribute 32/25 to the abundancy.
After setting these aside the residual divisor is 25. Direct odd-part
cancellation checks that no exponent from 0 through 49 works.

First suppose a is odd and a≥20. Its seed 2^a+1 supplies a 3, so
3 is simple and no shifted ordinary prime can supply another 3.
Every simple prime other than 3 is therefore 1 modulo 3. Also 3∤a,
since otherwise 2³+1=9 would divide 2^a+1. Choose any prime ℓ|a.
Then ℓ≥5 and A=(2^ℓ+1)/3 divides the seed. Each prime q|A has
ord_q(2)=2ℓ. Indeed 3∤A by the binomial expansion of (−1+3)^ℓ+1
modulo 9, and all smaller possible orders dividing 2ℓ are excluded.
Also −2 is a square modulo q, since the square of 2^((ℓ+1)/2) is
−2 modulo q. Hence q is 1 or 3 modulo 8 by the supplementary laws
of quadratic reciprocity. But A≡3 modulo 8, so at least one such q
is 3 modulo 8. It is simple because 5 does not divide 2^a+1.

For this q one has q≡1 mod 3 and v₂(q+1)=2. Therefore (q+1)/4
is 2 modulo 3 and has a prime divisor in this residue class. Such a
divisor in the odd balance can only be the core prime 5. Remove q
from the simple support. It cancels against A in the seed; the seed's
separate 3 and the factor 5 of q+1 give the remaining support the
artificial seed 15 of section 1. Its odd abundancy, including 25,
is below 2 and consequently below 199/100 by the interval certificate
below. If q≥200 the full abundancy of n is strictly below

    (199/100)(1+1/200)(1+2^(−20)) < 2,

contradicting unitary perfection. The congruences q≡3 mod 8,
q≡1 mod 3 and q≡4 mod 5 leave only 19 and 139 below 200. Their base-2
orders are 18 and 138, whose halves 9 and 69 are both composite.
Neither order can be 2ℓ. This excludes every odd a.

For the interval certificate begin with 3,7,13 forced at seed 15.
The residual divisor is 5, the current odd abundancy is 128/75,
and 3,5,7,13 are unavailable for further simple primes. Section 1's
exact tail and recursion exclude [199/100,2) in 13 nodes up to cutoff
1555. The Lean theorem Core25.seed_fifteen_gap proves the interval
using the independently implemented tail of the core-625 certificate.
Its 13 cutoff rows use a largest cutoff of 1651. Both bounds cover
arbitrarily large omitted primes through the respective tail lemmas.

Next suppose a=2b with b odd, b≥25. Set X=2^((b−1)/2), L=2^b and
A±=L±2X+1. These coprime odd integers have product 2^a+1. Assign the
remaining simple primes to these two factors by decreasing cancellation.
Their terminal divisors d₊d₋=25 and their abundancies are

    B± = 2^E± d±/A±,         1≤B±<25/16.

Here L/A± lies between 1−1/2048 and 1+1/2048, as in section 1 of
Erdős1052-core-625.txt. One cannot have d₊=1, since A₊>L would
require 2^E₊≥2L and B₊>25/16. If d₋=1, B₋=L/A₋. This is exactly
the neutral negative group excluded in section 3 of the same note;
its proof uses only that group and b≥25. Hence d₊=d₋=5, whence
B±=(5/4)L/A± are both strictly between 6/5 and 4/3. Neither group
contains 3. Exactly one A± contains 5 and uses up its terminal 5
immediately. All upward paths of that group therefore start at
Mersenne primes. Both 3 and the previously fixed 7 are unavailable.
The remaining starts 31,127 and all exponents ≥13 give total abundancy
at most (16/15)(64/63)(2048/2047)<6/5 by section 1. This contradiction
excludes every exponent with v₂(a)=1.

Finally put a=hb, h=2^v≥4, b odd and F=2^h+1. Assign separate groups
to F and (2^a+1)/F after cancelling 25's fixed simple primes. Since
F≡2 mod 3 and 5∤F, a simple root r≡2 mod 3 occurs in F. The factor
3 of r+1 lies in the same group. Hence the abundancy B of the F group
lies in [4/3,25/16). If d|25 is that group's terminal divisor, then

    (1+2^(−h))B = d·2^(E−h) ∈ (5/4,2).

No power of two, or five times a power of two with integer exponent,
lies in that interval. Thus d=25 and the quotient group has terminal
divisor 1. At h=4,8,16,32 direct forced cancellation from F already
fails. For h≥64, the first-step result of section 2 of Erdős1052-odd-part.txt
excludes a terminal divisor 1 for (2^(hb)+1)/F whenever b>1. Its
hypotheses are h a power of two ≥16 and the group balance with total
abundancy less than 2. This proves b=1.

For 6≤v≤19 use the existing integer divisors in erdos1052_fermat_bases.py.
The SPLITS rows give two coprime divisors of F each 2 modulo 3, hence
two distinct simple roots using the sole available 3. In a PATHS row
the simple primes starting from a 1 modulo 3 divisor of F force a
divisor 2 modulo 3 coprime to 5F, through their shifted factors.
Besides the original root r≡2 mod 3 this gives a second simple prime
in the same class, again impossible. The script verifies the divisors,
primality certificates for the intermediate primes and coprimalities.
These two lists cover every v from 6 through 19. Thus v≥20.

All prime factors of F are simple, so F is squarefree. If F itself
were prime it would occur once. Removing it and decreasing a by one
preserves unitary perfection because

    (1+2^(−a))(1+1/(2^a+1))=1+2^(−(a−1)).

The resulting number would still have repeated core 25 and its exponent
a−1 would be odd, which has just been excluded. Hence F is composite.

Run erdos1052_core25.py for the interval, congruence and Fermat divisor
checks. Core25.lean imports the existing PrimeDouble625.lean when used
on the fork. It additionally kernel-checks the witness orders and
numerical comparisons. The group allocation and congruence argument
connecting them to the full stated result are the written proof.

8. A finite test using the Mersenne primes in the descent of the core

Fix a permitted sparse height h and put Y=2^h. Start with the residual
D after the fixed seeds have been processed. The following test for
b=1 can apply even when D has prime divisors much larger than Y.

For any positive integer x expand all its odd prime factors, with
multiplicity, into the full recursion trees of section 5. Each vertex
q has the odd prime factors of q+1, with multiplicity, as children.
For each Mersenne prime m=2^k−1 let λ_m(x) count its leaf occurrences.
Form D' by keeping, with full exponents, just the prime factors p of
D for which every leaf of p's tree exceeds Y. Compute the rational sum

    U = Σ_(m=2^k−1) λ_m(D')·2^(1−k).

The sum is finite and its evaluation uses only D and h. If

    K(c)=f(h)     and     25Y U ≤ 1,                               (13)

then the only possible exponent at this height is a=h. As before,
K(c)<f(h) excludes the height. This proves exponent finiteness for any
fixed sparse core passing one of these tests at each permitted height.
For example U=0 whenever the recursion of each prime dividing D reaches
at least one prime at most Y, regardless of how large those prime
divisors themselves are.

For a proof consider a first layer A=(Y^r+1)/(Y+1), r any odd prime
divisor of b, with its assigned terminal divisor d|D and simple group T.
The completely additive leaf counts obey λ_m(q)=λ_m(q+1)+1_(q=m)
for odd primes q. Applying them to the group balance gives exactly

    λ_m(A) = λ_m(d) + 1_(m∈T).                                     (14)

The same identity holds for the counts of every occurrence of any
marked odd prime, recording internal vertices as well as leaves.
AdditiveHeight.marker_group proves this latter identity in Lean.

Suppose the layer has baseline height W(A)=h(r−1). The product of
1+1/q over the entire recursion forest of A, with repeated vertices
counted separately, is (1+1/Y)/(1+1/Y^r). Every vertex exceeds Y,
so (14) implies d|D'. Its reciprocal loss, meaning one minus the
reciprocal of this product, is at least y(1−y) where y=1/Y.
Partition the forest into paths by keeping one child at each internal
vertex. Keep one path per distinct starting Mersenne prime and consider
the other paths separately. If h+1 is composite, all possible starting
exponents are odd and at least h+3, so the first paths have summed
reciprocal loss at most y/3. If h+1 is prime, write h=2^v. An odd v
makes h+1 divisible by 3 and v≡2 mod 4 makes it divisible by 5.
Since h≥16 we therefore have 4|v, h≡1 mod 5. The argument of section 6
bounds the loss of the one path from 2Y−1 by 5y/8. It applies with
repeated vertices too, since every child anywhere in this forest is
greater than Y. The other distinct starting exponents again contribute
at most y/3. In either case the summed loss is at most 23y/24.

Each remaining path from m costs at most 2^(1−k). By (14) there
are at most λ_m(d) such additional paths. Reciprocal loss of a product
is bounded by the sum of the individual losses. Thus a baseline layer
necessarily satisfies

    Σ_m λ_m(d)·2^(1−k) ≥ y(1−y)−23y/24 > y/25.

The strict comparison uses h≥16. Hence 25YU≤1 forces each first
layer to exceed its baseline height. In (7) the prime indices of
these layers are distinct and are outside E(b). It follows in fact
that K(c)≥f(h)+E(b)+ω(b) under this mass condition, proving (13).
More generally, if t≥1 first layers attain baseline then t<25YU,
since their separate terminal divisors have product dividing D'.
A value of U exceeding the bound still leaves a potentially unbounded
prime multiplier. No universal estimate of U at every sparse core
is proved here.

9. A core-wide test for the three squarefree exponents

There is also a test on c alone. For each Mersenne prime m put

    δ_m = λ_m(c) − λ_m(σ*(c)).

If δ_m ≤ 1_(m|c) for every m, every positive admissible exponent lies
in {1,2,6}. Hence a nonempty sparse core passing this test has no
completion. It suffices to check the finite list of leaves in c.
The test holds at 1236 of the 7857 odd powerful cores through 10^8.

To prove this, apply the marked-vertex count to the full unitary
balance. It gives λ_m(2^a+1)=δ_m+1_(m∈S). Since S avoids all core
primes, every leaf occurs at most once under the test. Repetition of
any internal prime would repeat a leaf below it, since both copies
expand according to the same rule. Therefore the complete recursion
forest of M=2^a+1 consists of distinct odd primes. Write T for their
set. Its abundancy is 2^W(M)/M by multiplying the local identities
through the forest. The number n₀=2^a∏_(q∈T)q consequently has
unitary abundancy 2^f(a), a power of two at least 2.

Choose one child at each internal vertex. The resulting paths start
at distinct Mersenne primes, so for a≥2 the abundancy of n₀ is at most

    (5/4)·2·(4/3)·(16/15)·(64/63)·(2048/2047) < 4.

The factors come from 3,7,31,127 and the tail of Mersenne exponents
at least 13. Thus n₀ is unitary perfect with squarefree odd part.
Graham's classification gives a∈{1,2,6}. At a=1 the conclusion holds
directly. Conversely, for each of 1,2,6 the tree of 2^a+1 does have
distinct leaves. This characterizes all positive exponents with that
property independently of the core. AdditiveHeight.marker_core checks
the marked identity; the classification uses Graham's theorem.
