Exponent finiteness for every sparse prime-square core

Let n be unitary perfect. Suppose its only repeated odd prime is p>4096
and its exponent is exactly two. Write a=v₂(n). Then

    a=2^v,     ord_p(2)=2^(v+1).

Thus a fixed prime-square core admits at most one exponent. This theorem
has no upper bound on p. It also excludes every odd multiplier greater
than one, including a single arbitrarily large prime. The case of a
pure power of two can still occur in the necessary conditions at some
bases; for any fixed p the order fixes that one candidate.

1. Two groups forced by a square core

Write a=hb, h=2^v, b odd, Y=2^h. The sparse-core results in section 5
of Erdős1052-core-625.txt and section 6 of Erdős1052-odd-part.txt give
h≥16, p|Y+1, 5∤p²+1, and both 3 and 5 in the simple support S. The
prime p divides Y+1 since the latter has a factor equal to 2 modulo 3
which must belong to the core. Its order is therefore 2h.

Suppose b>1 and put Z=Y^b. Cancel one copy of p from Y+1 in the
odd-part balance. Split the remaining seed into three integers

    C=oddpart(p²+1),     V=(Y+1)/p,     A=(Z+1)/(Y+1).

Descending prime cancellation assigns disjoint subsets of S to these
integers. Each subset T satisfies m∏oddpart(q+1)=D∏q for its seed m,
and the three terminal integers D have product p. The group for A must
use p. Indeed, the first-step theorem in Erdős1052-odd-part.txt
applies at h with the odd multiplier b≥3. Equivalently, choose a prime
ℓ|b and split the first cyclotomic factor at ℓ from A. The Lean
theorem first_layer_charges requires its group to use a core factor.
The groups of C and V thus have terminal integer 1. Denote the groups
for V and A by T_V and T_A and their abundancies by B_V and B_A.
All group abundancies are at least 1, and their product is less than 2.

Let 2^k<p<2^(k+1) and x=p/2^k. The product identity in each group,
including its shift valuations at 2, forces

    B_V=x/(1+1/Y),       B_A=x(1+1/Y)/(1+1/Z).                    (1)

Indeed B_V is a power of two times p/(Y+1), and B_A a power of two
times p(Y+1)/(Z+1). There is exactly one value in [1,2) in each
dyadic progression. The displayed B_V is at least 1 because
2^k≤p−1≤Y. The displayed B_A exceeds x since Z>Y, and is less than
2 since (2^(k+1)−p)Y>p. For p≤Y this last inequality follows from
p<Y (Y is even), and for p=Y+1 it follows from k=h and Y≥16.

By (1), x²=B_A B_V(1+1/Z)<2(1+1/Z). Hence both B_V and B_A are
less than 8/5, using Y≥65536. Since oddpart(5+1)=3 and no terminal
integer is a multiple of 3, the group containing 5 must also contain 3.
It contributes at least (6/5)(4/3)=8/5. Thus neither T_A nor T_V
contains 3.

2. Disjoint Mersenne starts separate the two abundancies

For any B≥1 put d(B)=1−1/B. In a group with terminal 1 the path
bound gives B≤∏w(r), where the product is over its Mersenne starts
r=2^j−1 and w(r)=(r+1)/(r−1). For terminal p the same bound has an
extra factor of at most p/(p−1). One way to see the latter is to
remove the simple prime whose shift contains p, if there is one, and
all its successors. There is at most one such starting prime, and
its path contributes at most w(2p−1)=p/(p−1). The retained shifts
only use retained simple primes. Alternatively, apply the completely
multiplicative weight with value q/(q−1) at every odd prime q to
the full divisibility, keeping its factor p/(p−1) exactly.

If neither group has a Mersenne start, then B_V=1, so (1) and
2^k≤Y give p−2^k=2^k/Y≤1. Otherwise let r=2^t−1 be the smallest
Mersenne start in either group, and put α=2^(−t). Since 3 is absent,
all these Mersenne exponents are odd primes. Disjointness makes
every Mersenne exponent in the other group at least t+2. Its Mersenne
factors have total reciprocal loss at most

    Σ_(j≥t+2, j odd) 2^(1−j) = (2/3)α.

Here 1−1/∏w(r)≤Σ(1−1/w(r)). The group containing r has loss at
least 1−1/(1+1/r)=α. The other group consequently satisfies one of
the following two bounds, depending on which group contains r:

    r in T_A:  d(B_V) ≤ (2/3)d(B_A);
    r in T_V:  d(B_A) ≤ 1/p+(2/3)d(B_V).

This argument also works when the other group has no Mersenne start.

3. The forced base

Set d₀=1−1/x=(p−2^k)/p. Formula (1) gives

    d(B_V)=d₀−1/(xY),       d₀<d(B_A)≤d₀+1/(xY).

In the first case of section 2 it follows that d₀≤5/(xY), so
p−2^k≤5·2^k/Y≤5. In the second, d₀≤1/p+2d₀/3, so p−2^k≤3.
Including the empty case gives 1≤p−2^k≤5 throughout.

As ord_p(2)=2h, one has p≡1 modulo 2h. In particular 2h≤p−1
and 2h divides 2^k since both are powers of two. Thus p−2^k≡1
modulo 2h≥32. Its size forces p=2^k+1. Primality forces k to be a
power of two: an odd prime divisor of k would give the proper factor
2^(k/r)+1 of 2^k+1. Since p>4096, one has 4|k. Hence p≡2 modulo 5
and 5|p²+1. This contradicts the sparse-core condition in section 1.
We conclude b=1 and a=h as asserted.

4. A test for cores with several repeated primes

Write C=oddpart(σ*(c)). First cancel C alone by descending prime
factorization: a factor in the core uses up one copy, and a factor q
outside the core forces the simple prime q and then oddpart(q+1).
Excess demand excludes the core. This finite procedure depends only on
c. If its remaining core is 1, a prime, or the square of a prime, then
every completion at this sparse core has b=1. In particular the odd
part has a bound independent of the sizes of its prime factors.
An immediate sufficient condition, requiring only modular arithmetic, is
that c/gcd(c,σ*(c)) is 1, a prime, or a prime square. Cancelling the
prime factors demanded by subsequent shifts can only decrease this
remaining integer by divisibility.

Indeed F=2^h+1 must use a prime p≡2 mod 3 directly from the core. It
cannot use a copy already assigned to C. If b>1, A must use a further
core factor. This deals with the first two cases. If the remaining core
is p², cancel one p from F. The groups of V=F/p and A then have
terminals 1 and p as before. The primes assigned to C may already
include 3 and 5. The group containing 5 includes 3 because no terminal
is divisible by 3, so the argument giving 3∉T_A,T_V still holds.
Sections 1–3 up through p=2^k+1 apply verbatim. Comparing the orders
of 2 modulo p gives k=h: primality makes k a power of two and
ord_p(2)=2k=2h. Hence p=Y+1, V=1 and

    B_A=(1+1/Y)²/(1+1/Z).

The following argument removes the dependence on the exponent of p
in the original core. A Mersenne start r≤Y/2−1 in T_A would give
1+1/r≥Y/(Y−2)>(1+1/Y)². Every possible starting exponent is
therefore at least h. The index h is even and greater than two, so
the indices are odd and start at h+1 or later. Their total loss from
the path bound is at most ∑_(j≥h+1, j odd)2^(1−j)=4/(3Y).
There can be at most one shift using p. Its prime q satisfies
q≥4p−1, since 2p−1=2Y+1 is a proper multiple of 3. Delete q and all
its successors in the direction of larger primes. These form one
path of product at most w(q) and thus cost at most 1/(2p) in reciprocal
loss. In the remainder all demands divide the product of the retained
primes, since any retained shift using a deleted prime would itself
have been deleted. Consequently

    d(B_A) ≤ 1/(2p)+4/(3Y) < 11/(6Y).

But Z≥Y³ gives Y·d(B_A)≥(2Y−1)/(Y+1)>11/6 for Y>17. This excludes
the last case. The same test applies if cancelling F and C separately
shows that F uses exactly one copy of p, that p divides F, and that the
entire unused core after both cancellations is exactly p. The neutral
group of F/p and the group of A again have the displayed ratios. The
earlier Ω(b) bound by itself allows an arbitrarily large prime in this
case. If b=1 the sparse-order condition leaves a finite list of h.

5. A restriction on the sizes of possible prime-square cores

Whenever the sum of the reciprocals of the repeated primes is at most
2^−14, the suppliers of 5 and 7 are forced to be 19 and 223. Delete
the shifts using repeated primes and their upward paths. Section 5 of
Erdős1052-core-625.txt bounds the loss by 3s/(1−s) at reciprocal sum s.
Including the factors from the core and from 2^a costs at most

    (1+2^−16)/((1−2^−28)(1−3/(2^14−1))).

Multiplying that expression by 2−1/2048 gives a number strictly below
2. Thus the retained abundancy lies in [2−1/2048,2). For each of the
other three supplier pairs, the core-1 interval test with 3,5,7 and the
two suppliers required excludes that interval. These tests use 24,15,35
nodes for (19,433),(79,13),(79,223), respectively. All mandatory primes
are below the least possible deleted prime and survive the deletion. See large_base_suppliers()
in erdos1052_group_bounds.py.

For a prime-square core let x=p/2^floor(log₂p). Some m∈{1,2} must
satisfy the following restriction. Write y for x^m divided by the unique
power of two putting y in [1,2), and put t=min(y,2/y). Then

    t < 4237/4096     or     256/223 < t < 19/16.                    (2)

This applies to all p≥2^14. For b=1 there are just two groups, for
F=Y+1 and (p²+1)/2. The F terminal is p^m for m=1 or 2. If
t≥4237/4096 then y and 2/y are both far enough above 1 that the
normalized group abundancies are y/(1+1/Y) and (2/y)/(1+p^−2).
Both corrections are strict. Neither seed supplies 5 or 7 directly:
the unique suppliers 19 and 223 supply their copies in shifts.
Consequently 3,5,19 lie in the same group and 7,223 lie in the same
group. These two closures contribute 32/19 and 256/223. If t≥19/16
neither group can reach 32/19. If 4237/4096≤t≤256/223 the smaller
uncorrected ratio is too small for either closure and the larger is at
most 8192/4237=(32/19)(256/223). The strict correction makes it too
small for both together. These contradictions prove (2). For instance
all bases with 19/16≤x≤sqrt(32/19) or sqrt(19/8)≤x≤32/19 are excluded
at e=2, with no upper size limit on p.

6. Complete exponent exclusion for 167772161^e, 2≤e≤8192

Let p=167772161. The earlier order and residue conditions require
h=2^23 and 32|e. Split the seeds into C=(p^e+1)/2, V=(Y+1)/p and
A=(Z+1)/(Y+1) as above. Write their terminal integers as p^(e−i−j),
p^(j−1),p^i respectively, with j≥1 and i+j≤e. If b>1 the first
layer requires i≥1. For b=1 the A-seed is 1 and descending cancellation
assigns it the empty group (i=0). Up to powers of two, the group
abundancies in the order A,V,C are

    p^i(1+1/Y)/(1+1/Z),    p^j/(1+1/Y),    p^(−i−j)/(1+p^(−e)).

Only the dyadic normalizations in [1,2) are possible. The verifier
encloses them with the first 96 binary digits of each p^s, using
1/Y,1/p^e<2^−96 and outward rounding. At a normalization boundary
it checks both possible sides. It deletes p-using shifts and their
upward paths separately in each group. For a terminal p^u the resulting
loss is at most min(3/(p−1),u/(2p)). Indeed there are at most u such
shifts and their primes are at least 4p−1 because 3|2p−1. The other
bound is the single-base estimate of Erdős1052-core-bounds.txt §4.
Larger primes lie on the deleted paths. The required small suppliers
and their full downward closures remain in their original groups.

For each i,j the verifier tests the resulting three intervals with the
core-1 tail lemma and disjoint assignments of the two mandatory closures.
It also distributes the optional Mersenne primes 31,127,8191,131071,
524287 among the groups, including the possibility of absence. A
required prime limits the least remaining prime to at most that prime;
this allows the interval test to enforce its presence. A cutoff limits
only finite branching and the tail bound covers every larger prime.
With the first cutoff 262144, every i>0 is already excluded. The only
remaining (i,j) have i=0 and

    j ∈ {602,1206,3482,5169,5249,7445,8049,8129}.

For these eight allocations the pair (19,223) is required by section 5.
All eight are excluded with cutoff 2^26. Thus there are no completions
for any exponent a and any core p^e in the stated range. Running
python3 -B erdos1052_group_bounds.py reproduces every interval exclusion.

7. An explicit odd-part bound for an arbitrary fixed core passing a comparison

Fix a permissible h. Cancel the two fixed seeds C and F=2^h+1,
retaining their simple primes and their terminal core factors. If
either seed creates a repeated demand for a simple prime, or if the
core is overdrawn, there is no completion. Otherwise let D be the
unused part of the core. This determines the terminal of the group
assigned to A=(2^(hb)+1)/F in any completion, independently of b.
For T a group assigned to either fixed seed (or any fixed factor split
off it), write B for its exact abundancy and E for its terminal. All
these quantities are finite computations in c and h.

Define W(d)=∏_(p^m||d)(p/(p−1))^m and λ(d)=1−1/W(d). Normalize the
odd integer DF by a power of two to its unique value R in (1,2).
Suppose 3 is absent from T and from the A-group. The latter follows,
for example, if 3 is already forced by one of the two fixed seeds.
It also follows in the range 2^(hb)≥1/(R−1) when R≤8/5: the A-group
then has abundancy strictly below 8/5, and 3 and 5 belong together.
If the fixed quantities satisfy

    d(B) ≥ λ(E)+(2/3)d(R),        Δ = d(R)−λ(D)−(2/3)d(B) > 0,

there is the explicit bound

    2^(hb) ≤ max(1/(R−1), 1/(RΔ)).

Indeed above the first threshold the normalized A-group abundancy is
exactly R/(1+2^(−hb)). Its loss is d(R)−2^(−hb)/R. For arbitrary
terminals E,D the least-start argument in section 2 gives at least one
of d(B)≤λ(E)+(2/3)d(B_A) and d(B_A)≤λ(D)+(2/3)d(B). For this, use
the multiplicative weight W on the terminal instead of the single
prime weight. The first inequality is strictly impossible under the
displayed assumption. The second is exactly RΔ≤2^(−hb). This proves
the bound, with no restriction on the prime factors of b. It also
gives a finite test for each c,h with such a witness. There is no
argument here that every sparse core has a witness to both inequalities.

8. Two uniform consequences

First, a=16 is impossible for every nonempty sparse core. The full
2-adic balance gives Σ_(q∈S)v₂(q+1)=a+1−v₂(σ*(c))≤16. Delete the
core-using paths from S as in section 5 of Erdős1052-core-625.txt.
The retained set satisfies the same bound, its abundancy is below
2, and it must reach

    2(1−4096^−2)(1−3/4095)/(1+2^−16).

The four possible pairs of suppliers all survive. In the interval
search, if u units of 2-adic valuation are still available then at
most u further primes can occur. Above y their total factor is at
most (1+1/(y+1))^u. At a selected q subtract v₂(q+1) from u.
Applying this along with the ordinary tail bound excludes all four
pairs, with (nodes,largest cutoff) equal to (16,310), (17,568),
(12,3368), (20,223), respectively, in the order of the supplier
list in section 6. The program checks the four certificates exactly.
Consequently every sparse solution has a≥48: h=16 would require
b≥3 and the remaining permitted h are at least 64. In particular
there is no completion at any sparse core whose compulsory C-group
leaves only 65537 or 65537². Section 4 would force b=1 and the
order of 2 modulo 65537 would force h=16. An empty remaining core
already excludes every exponent because F must consume a core factor.

Second, the exclusion for the single base in section 6 extends to an
unbounded range of bases. Let p≥2^30 be prime and let x=p/2^floor(log₂p).
For every 2≤e≤8192, no core p^e with

    |x/(5/4)−1| ≤ 2^−35

admits any exponent a. This includes every prime 5·2^t+1 for t≥33.
To certify the entire interval, enclose each power x^s (s≤8192) by
the corresponding power of 5/4 times [1−sε,1/(1−sε)] with ε=2^−35.
This follows from Bernoulli's inequality on both sides. The binary
mantissa of (5/4)^s equals that of 5^s. Use 2^30 as a lower bound
for p in the path loss, and bound 1/Y and 1/p^e by 2^−56, since
h≥64 and e≥2. The same allocation test over these outward intervals
excludes every i,j. Its two search cutoffs are 2^18 and 2^26.
The order argument imposes no upper bound on h or p here. The program
also verifies this whole band with its default command.

9. Removing every occurrence of a repeated prime from shifted factors

For fixed prime p write d'(q) for oddpart(q+1) with all factors of p
removed. If ∏oddpart(q+1) divides p^u D∏q, then ∏d'(q) divides D∏q.
This is immediate by taking the part coprime to p on both sides. Apply
the same weight proof as in section 2 directly to d'. Its proof uses
only that d'(q)>0 and 2d'(q)≤q+1. The starting primes are precisely
those of the form 2^j p^s−1 for j≥1, s≥0. Their contribution beyond
the cutoff tends to one as the cutoff increases. The exponent u
contributes no fixed error.

Here are the exact changes to the interval test. The possible next
primes satisfy q=2^j p^s d−1, where d divides the ordinary remaining
tokens. A selection consumes d and makes q available. If all remaining
primes exceed x, the tail bound is the old one multiplied by a bound
on ∏w(q) over additional starts q>x with s≥1. For p=65537 every such
prime up to 2^35 is found by trial division:

    262147, 1073758207, 4295032831, 17180131327.

For each retained start add 2/(q+1) to the loss. Put L=max(2^35+1,x+1).
For each p^s≤L, s≥1, let z be the first 2^j p^s>L, j≥1. The sum of
losses over these possible starts beyond L is at most 4/z, counting
all larger j. If p^k is the largest power at most L (allow k=0),
the contribution from all s>k is at most 2/(p^k(p−1)). One minus
the total loss bounds the reciprocal of the additional product below.
All quantities are rational. More generally every finite set P of
repeated bases can be stripped in the same way. Its possible starts
have q+1=2^j d with d supported on P. The sum of 2^(1−j)/d over all
j≥1 and all such d converges to 2∏_(p∈P)(1−1/p)^−1. Thus their
tail losses tend effectively to zero for an arbitrary fixed P.

Apply the single-base version at p=65537 and 2≤e≤8192. The order of
2 forces h=16, so F=p and the V-group is empty. Section 8 excludes
b=1. For b≥3 put Y=65536 and let i be the exponent of p consumed by
the A-group. Then 1≤i≤e−1. Because (1+1/Y)^8192<8/7, the normalized
ratios in [1,2) of the A-group and the C-group are exactly

    (1+1/Y)^(i+1)/(1+Y^−b),     2/((1+1/Y)^(i+1)(1+p^−e)).

The first is below 8/7, so both mandatory closures in section 5
belong to the C-group. Its abundancy is at least 8192/4237.
Compute intervals for the two displayed ratios with b≥3 and e≥2.
Their endpoints may be rounded outwards with 96 binary fractional
bits. Use the stripped demands and assign the optional small Mersenne
primes and the four additional starts to disjoint groups or to neither.
The cutoff 2^18 excludes every i except 8,2097,2105,2109. At these
four indices the cutoff 2^30 excludes both groups occurring together.
The script prints the four indices. The result is uniform in b and e
throughout the stated range.

Together with section 6 and the earlier complete Fermat-base sieve,
this proves that no core p^e with 4096<p≤10^12 and 2≤e≤8192 has a
unitary perfect completion. The same proof at 65537 also eliminates
the exact core exponent 8192 left open by the previous valuation
certificates. The check of the base sieve is erdos1052_fermat_bases.py;
the stripped-demand check is in erdos1052_group_bounds.py.

10. What an unbounded odd multiplier would force at a fixed core

Fix c and one of its permissible h, and cancel the fixed seeds as in
section 7. Let D be the remaining core and R the binary normalization of
D(2^h+1). If there are arbitrarily large odd multipliers b, there must
exist an infinite set T of simple odd primes, disjoint from all those
already assigned to the fixed seeds, such that

    ∏_(q∈T) (1+1/q) = R.

Every finite initial segment of T satisfies the stripped divisibility
in section 9: the product of its stripped demands divides its product
of primes. In particular this is a problem about attaining one explicit
nonintegral dyadic rational, for each of finitely many h at the core.

For a proof, the A-groups have abundancies R/(1+2^−(hb)) for large b.
Take a diagonal subsequence on which membership at every fixed prime
eventually stabilizes. Stripped demands of a prefix ending at X only
involve smaller primes. Their valuations on the full right side can
therefore be supplied entirely by the prefix. On passing to primes
above X, the available ordinary tokens are a divisor of the product
of prefix primes, so their number is at most π(X). The uniform tail
bound for the stripped groups is consequently

    (1+2/X)^π(X) ∏_(r>X, r+1=2^j d, d supported on core bases) w(r),

where the product runs over primes r. The first factor tends to one
because π(X)/X tends to zero. The second does so by the convergent
series in section 9. Hence the group products converge uniformly in
their tails; the limiting set T realizes the asserted product exactly.
A finite product of (1+1/q) has odd denominator in lowest terms, whereas
R∈(1,2) is dyadic. Thus T must be infinite.

This also gives a finite certificate whenever this obstruction is
absent. The class of sets whose finite prefixes obey the stated
divisibilities and exclusions is closed in the product of the prime
membership coordinates. The product map is continuous by the same
uniform tail estimate. If R is outside its compact image, for some
finite X the intervals obtained by multiplying each possible prefix
abundancy by [1,U(X)], with U(X) any effective tail bound tending
to one as above, all miss R. Their positive distance from R bounds a
since R−B_A=R/(2^a+1). This criterion does not determine whether
the requisite infinite set exists for every remaining sparse core.

The Lean theorem Erdos1052.PrimeSquare.prime_square_of_perfect verifies
the prime-square implication from the definition of unitary perfection
given the sparse-order and divisibility conclusions listed in section 1.
Its proof includes the assignment of groups, the exclusion of 3 from
both competing groups, the geometric tail, and the short gap at a power
of two. The sparse hypotheses themselves use the earlier paper estimates
and exact finite interval certificates. Sections 4–10 supply further
written arguments and, for the finite tests, exact rational verification.
Erdos1052.Stripped.strip_balance and strip_tail verify the general
single-base divisibility and tail lemma in section 9.
The general fixed-core bound and the uniform bound on c are still open.
