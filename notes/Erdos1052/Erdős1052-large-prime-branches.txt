Two large prime factors in the fixed-core exponent problem

Let n=2^a c ∏(q∈S)q be unitary perfect, with fixed odd repeated core c
and the other odd primes occurring exactly once. Fix B≥2 larger than
or equal to every prime factor of c. There is an effective W(c,B) such
that, whenever a≥W, one of the following holds.

  • Two distinct primes greater than B divide 2^a+1.
  • Two distinct primes greater than B divide q+1 for the same q∈S.

Consequently exponent finiteness holds unconditionally for completions
in which the second-largest prime divisor of each of these numbers is
bounded by a prescribed B. This allows arbitrarily large largest prime
divisors and imposes no sparsity condition on c. It does not bound the
second-largest divisor for all completions.

The conclusion also localizes to a single multiplier. Write a=hb with
b odd, and fix an odd prime ℓ dividing b. Let A=(2^(hℓ)+1)/(2^h+1).
Enlarge B to include the prime factors of 2^h+1. For all sufficiently
large ℓ (depending only on c,h,B), two primes above B must occur
together in A or in q+1 for a simple prime forced from A. The latter
means a simple prime reached by starting with the primes of A and
recursively factoring the odd parts of their shifts, stopping at c.
The previous bound for Ω(b) leaves precisely the possibility of large
prime multipliers. This result restricts their entire forced descent.

1. An interval missed by small multiples of prime powers

Fix a positive integer D. Choose J with 2^J>2D, for example the
smallest such integer. Put M=2^(2J)+D and P=(2D+2)(M²)!.
For each odd u≤D and each e from J to 2J inclusive, m=2^e+u is odd
and satisfies D<m≤M. Since φ(m²) divides P, Euler's theorem gives
2^P≡1 mod m². For E=P+e this gives a factorization
    2^E+u=m t,    t=1+2^e(2^P−1)/m,    gcd(m,t)=1.
The second factor exceeds D because 2^P>2D and m≤2·2^e. Thus both
coprime factors exceed D. It follows that 2^E+u cannot equal d p^j
for any 1≤d≤D, prime p and j≥0. Otherwise each coprime factor would
have to be divisible by p.

We have a simultaneous interval [P+J,P+2J] for every allowed offset
u and every denominator d. No primality testing of the numbers in the
interval is used. The Lean theorem prime_power_free_interval uses a
larger interval width 2D and the same argument.

2. Why a single chain must enter this interval

Use d(q)=oddpart(q+1). For any odd seed m dividing 2^a+1 the necessary
divisibility is
    m ∏(q∈S)d(q) | c ∏(q∈S)q.
More generally one may start with any subset T of S and any seed m
satisfying that divisibility on T. Take d₀ odd and fixed and suppose
d₀m=2^N+1. For the first conclusion take d₀=1 and N=a. For the local
version take d₀=2^h+1, N=hℓ; decreasing-prime cancellation gives a
subset T forced from A with A ∏(q∈T)d(q)=D' ∏(q∈T)q for some D'|c.

Assume m and each q+1 in the chosen support have at most one prime
above B counted with multiplicity. Take D=d₀ c ∏(p≤B, p prime)p.
Remove the small prime factors of m. If a single large factor r
remains, r must be simple and belongs to the chosen support. Replace
r by oddpart(r+1), extract its small odd prime factors, and repeat.
Each remaining large prime is smaller than the previous one. The
product of all extracted small odd factors divides c ∏(p≤B)p: this
follows from the displayed divisibility, since each shifted factor is
used at most once. A square factor above B would already contradict
the right side, hence whenever there are two large factors they are
distinct.

At each large prime the equation has the form
    d r = 2^E+u,    d≤D,    1≤u≤d,    d and u odd.
Initially d is d₀ times the extracted factors of m and u=1. If
r+1=2^j t r' where t is the extracted odd factor and r' is the next
large prime (or 1 at termination), then
    2^j d t r' = 2^E+u+d.
As long as E≥J, one has j<J. Indeed 0<u+d≤2D<2^J, so the right
side cannot be divisible by 2^J. Also j≥1. The next exponent is E−j,
the denominator is d t≤D, and the new offset (u+d)/2^j is positive,
at most d and odd as long as E−j≥1. Oddness follows by reducing the
next equation modulo 2.

A starting E above the forbidden interval cannot cross it in one step,
because the width is J and every decrement is less than J. The recursion
terminates because the large primes decrease. Termination at an exponent
at or above the interval would give 2^E+u=d for d≤D, which is impossible.
This proves both conclusions with W=P+J.

The first place where two large prime divisors occur is also close to
the top of this particular chain. The same forbidden interval works with
P replaced by tP for every t≥1. Given N≥P+J, choose t with
tP+J≤N<(t+1)P+J. Every preceding single-prime step lowers E by at least
one, and cannot cross the interval. Within at most P steps there must
therefore be a factor with two primes above B, while E≥tP+J>N−P.
The factor at that step has size (2^E+u)/d>2^(N−P)/D. This gives both
a bounded distance from the initial quotient and a fixed positive lower
bound on the factor's size relative to 2^N, for fixed c,h,B.

3. Where the argument stops

If a seed or shifted factor has two large primes, both can be unbounded
and neither can be absorbed into the bounded denominator d. Tracking
their product through the replacement of one of them adds a multiple of
the other prime to u. That term defeats the uniform bound u≤D used in
the construction of the forbidden interval. The unresolved fixed-core
case now requires such pairs for arbitrarily large B. The separate
unconditional bound on the size of all repeated cores is also open.

4. How many simple primes one large multiplier forces

There is also an unconditional size bound for the exponent in terms of the
number of simple primes forced from the one quotient. Fix h≥1 and put

    L=(2^h+1)c(2Ω(c)+5).

Write N=hℓ and A=(2^N+1)/(2^h+1) for an odd multiplier ℓ≥3. Choose
the assigned set T of simple primes and d|c as in section 2, so that
A ∏(q∈T)o(q+1)=d ∏(q∈T)q. If k=|T|, then

    N < 2^k log₂(2L).

There are also individually bounded primes. If (2L)^(2^i)≤2^N, then
T contains at least i+1 primes. Its (i+1)-st smallest prime is less
than (2L)^(2^i). The bounds apply however many large factors occur
together in the seed or its shifts.

To prove this put F=d(2^h+1) and E=Σ(q∈T)v₂(q+1). The group identity
and its abundancy strictly below two give

    (1+2⁻ᴺ) ∏(q∈T)(1+1/q)=F/2^j=R,       j=N−E>0.

Indeed R<2(1+2⁻ᴺ)≤3 and F≥3. Write the primes of T in increasing
order q₁,...,qₖ. Let Qᵢ be the product of the first i, and Pᵢ their
abundancy. Since Pᵢ<R, writing both sides over a common denominator
gives 1−Pᵢ/R≥1/(F Qᵢ). This applies even at i=k.

The unprocessed primes satisfy ∏o(q+1) | d Qᵢ ∏q by the original
group identity, and Ω(d Qᵢ)≤Ω(c)+i. For r=qᵢ₊₁, inequality (4) in
Erdős1052-core-bounds.txt bounds their abundancy by

    (1+2/(r−1))^(Ω(c)+i) ∏(q≥r, q a Mersenne prime in T) (q+1)/(q−1).

For each of the first Ω(c)+i factors, the loss of its reciprocal from
one is 2/(r+1). For the Mersenne factors these losses sum to at most
Σ(j≥ceil(log₂(r+1)))2^(1−j)≤4/(r+1). Since 1−∏(1−xᵢ)≤Σxᵢ,
including the extra factor 1+2⁻ᴺ yields

    1/(F Qᵢ) ≤ 1−Pᵢ/R ≤ 1/(2^N+1)+(2Ω(c)+2i+4)/(r+1).

At i=k the second term is zero. It follows that the lesser z of 2^N
and the next prime (or just 2^N at i=k) satisfies

    z+1 ≤ F(2Ω(c)+2i+5)Qᵢ ≤ L·2^i Qᵢ.

Starting at Q₀=1, continue until z=2^N. Before then Qᵢ₊₁≤L·2^i Qᵢ²,
giving Qᵢ≤L^(2^i−1)·2^(2^i−i−1) by induction. Hence
z+1≤L^(2^i)·2^(2^i−1)<(2L)^(2^i) at each step. This proves both
claims, since the stopping index is at most k.

For h a power of two and an odd prime ℓ not dividing 2^h+1, every prime
dividing A has multiplicative order 2hℓ for the base 2. Indeed a common
prime of A and 2^h+1 must divide ℓ, by evaluating the alternating quotient
at −1. The order is a divisor of 2hℓ that does not divide hℓ, and order
2h would give the excluded common divisor. Thus every seed prime is at
least 2hℓ+1. For every i with (2L)^(2^i)≤2hℓ, at least i+1 simple
primes in T are proper descendants below every prime dividing the seed.
Their number tends to infinity with ℓ, even allowing unrestricted
branching. A uniform upper bound on this count would bound ℓ. The
present argument provides the lower bound.

5. Which primes supply 5 and 7 at a sparse core

Suppose the nonempty core satisfies the reciprocal sum condition. The
previous sparse-core argument forces 3,5,7 as simple primes, a≥16, and
every simple prime except 3 and 5 to be 1 mod 3. Delete all simple primes whose shift uses a core
prime, together with all their successors in the direction of larger
primes. The surviving set G contains 3,5,7. It satisfies ∏o(q+1)|∏q.
Its abundancy lies in [L₀,2), where

    L₀=2/((1+2⁻¹⁶)E₀),    E₀=1/((1−4096⁻²)(1−3/4095)).

For s=5 or 7 there must be a prime q in G with s|q+1. In fact if no
such q existed, the spare s on the right would give s∏o(r+1)|∏r.
The exact interval test with seed s excludes [L₀,2). Because s is
simple, this q is unique even in the original support.

In the direction of increasing primes, each r in G has at most one
successor, since r can divide only one shifted factor. Deleting q and
all its successors loses abundancy at most w(q)=(q+1)/(q−1). It frees
s on the right while retaining s. The interval test at seed s excludes
[L₀/w(X),2) with X=554 for s=5 and X=1040 for s=7. Thus q<X.
Deleting only the proper successors of q instead frees q with loss at
most w(2q−1). Test each prime s<q<X equal to −1 mod s. Exclude it if
its downwards closure is inconsistent, contains a disallowed residue,
or the seed q excludes [L₀/w(2q−1),2). This leaves

    s=5: q=19 or 79.           s=7: q=13, 223 or 433.

For a pair p,q from the two lists, their forced closures, listed below,
are disjoint. Neither belongs to the other's proper successor chain.
Removing both proper successor chains frees the seed pq with loss at
most w(2p−1)w(2q−1), including when the chains coalesce. The pair
(19,13) already has abundancy 512/247>2. The test for (79,433)
excludes the requisite interval at its first node with cutoff 31.
This leaves exactly four necessary alternatives for the two suppliers,

    (19,223), (19,433), (79,13), (79,223).

The closures are {19,5,3}, {79,5,3}, {13,7}, {223,7}, {433,7,31}.
The interval tests use the proof in Erdős1052-core-bounds.txt and exact
rational arithmetic. They can be rerun with erdos1052_sparse_parents.py.

7. A finiteness theorem in which the repeated core may vary

Here the initial seed is 2^a+1 and there is one further source x+1 for
each odd prime power x exactly dividing n. Fix any B≥2 and suppose that
the product of the odd prime powers of n with base ≤B is bounded by D.
Impose two conditions on the sources:

  (i) Each source contains at most one distinct prime greater than B.
 (ii) Each prime greater than B divides at most one source.

Under these conditions there are only finitely many unitary perfect n.
No assumption is made on the sizes or multiplicities of individual odd
prime powers above B. In particular at B=4096 every sparse core in the
question satisfies the small-prime bound D=∏(odd primes p≤4096)p.

Indeed the odd-part balance is (2^a+1)∏o(x+1)=∏x for a>0. By (ii) a
large prime encountered in a source occurs there with its entire
valuation in n. By (i) the odd part of this source is t·p^e with t
supported on small primes, or simply t. Starting at the seed and moving
to p^e+1 gives a path of whole prime powers. These powers strictly
decrease, because the next divides o(p^e+1)≤(p^e+1)/2<p^e. Thus each
source on the path is used at most once. The product of all extracted
t divides the small part of ∏x and is at most D. Repeat the proof in
section 2 with p^e in place of the single prime. The interval in
section 1 excludes every prime power with the same W(D), and the
positive odd offset and exponent-decrement bounds are identical.
This bounds a uniformly. The Lean theorem bounded_prime_power_paths
checks this last step directly for every finite prime-power path.

For completeness, bounded a implies finitely many n. For fixed a>0 let
x₁<...<xₖ be the odd prime-power components. Each xᵢ+1 is even, so the
2-adic valuation of the full balance gives k≤a+1. Their abundancy product
equals R=2^(a+1)/(2^a+1). After any proper prefix of product Q and
abundancy V, the positive rational gap 1−V/R is at least 1/(2^(a+1)Q).
If x is the next component, the same gap is at most k/(x+1), by taking
the product of the remaining reciprocal abundancies. Thus each next
component has a finite bound determined by a and the prefix. For a=0,
k≤1 by the same valuation argument, and no positive odd n is unitary
perfect. This proves the asserted finiteness. In particular, outside a
finite set, every hypothetical unitary perfect number with a sparse core
must have either two different large prime divisors in one source or a
single large prime divisor shared by two sources. The unrestricted
uniform bound on repeated cores still requires treating those cases.

8. Two bounded simple primes must occur on different branches

Fix c and an odd initial denominator d₀. Suppose d₀A=2^N+1 and the
assigned support satisfies A∏o(q+1)=d∏q, d|c, with all q simple
and outside c. There exist effective L(c,d₀) and N₀(c,d₀) such that for
N>N₀ two primes in this support, both at most L, are incomparable in
the following sense. No descending path from one to the other exists,
where an edge from r to q means q divides r+1. In particular a single
path through all small simple primes cannot support arbitrarily large
N, irrespective of the complexity of the other branches. Taking
d₀=2^h+1 gives the assertion for any odd multiplier, including primes.

Each simple q is supplied exactly once, either by A or by the shift of
a unique larger prime. The latter inequality follows from q|(r+1)/2.
Thus the predecessors form rooted trees. Suppose all simple primes
at most L are on one path. Pick a path starting at a prime of A that
contains all of them, using an empty path if necessary. Include a
final endpoint 1. At the seed write A=z₀r₀ for the remaining path
prime r₀, or 1 if the path is empty. Each subsequent step has

    r+1=2^j z r',      j≥1.

Here z collects all odd factors other than the next path prime r'.
The off-path primes descending from each such z form disjoint sets Uᵢ.
Cancelling factors in each subtree gives

    zᵢ βᵢ = 2^(kᵢ) Dᵢ,    βᵢ=∏(q∈Uᵢ)(1+1/q),
    kᵢ=Σ(q∈Uᵢ)v₂(q+1),    ∏Dᵢ=d.

This includes a factor of zᵢ belonging directly to the core, even if
there are no simple primes in its subtree. All off-path primes exceed
L. Their union U satisfies ∏(q∈U)o(q+1) | c∏(q∈U)q. The tail estimate
in section 4 gives 1−1/∏βᵢ≤(2Ω(c)+4)/(L+1). Thus by taking L large
we can make the entire product of the βᵢ at most 1+η, for any prescribed
η>0, independently of the number and sizes of the branches.

Put D=d₀c. Choose J with 2^J>8D and construct the forbidden interval
[W,Z] of section 1 with Z=W+J. Choose positive η≤1/8 so small that
η(2^Z+D)<1/8, then L for this η. These choices depend only on c,d₀.

After stripping off each z, the current prime r (or final 1) satisfies

    d' r = K(2^E+w),    1≤K≤1+η,    0<w≤d'≤D.

The integer d' is d₀ times the product of the Dᵢ used so far, and K
is the product of their βᵢ. The exponent E is an integer, allowed to
be negative, and w is positive rational. Initially, after stripping
z₀, take E=N−k₀, w=2^(−k₀), K=β₀, d'=d₀D₀. At a shift with the next
off-path data (β,k,Dᵢ), put Δ=j+k. The exact updates are

    E' = E−Δ,   K' = Kβ,   d'' = d'Dᵢ,   w'=(w+d'/K)/2^Δ.

In particular w'≤d' because Δ≥1. At the initial stripping Δ=k₀ may
equal zero. At every step, including that initial stripping, the
post-step residual satisfies 0<K'w'≤2(1+η)D/2^Δ.

No step of size Δ≥J can land at an E'≤Z. Indeed K'w'<(1+η)/4. If
E'≥0, d''r'−2^E' is a strictly positive integer, while its upper
bound η2^Z+(1+η)/4 is less than one. If E'<0, d''r' is a positive
integer at most (1+η)/2+(1+η)/4<1. Both possibilities are impossible.

No post-step state can lie in [W,Z] either. There u=d'r−2^E is a
positive odd integer with u≤η2^Z+(1+η)d'<d'+1. Hence 1≤u≤d'≤D.
For r prime this contradicts the forbidden interval. For final r=1,
d'≤D<2^W contradicts the same positive difference. The path cannot
terminate at E>Z since its terminal value d' would exceed D there
as well. Starting at N>Z, any first step landing at or below Z has
size less than J, so it must land inside [W,Z]. This proves the claim.

This argument yields an alternative sufficient condition for exponent
finiteness at fixed c,h: if in every completion the small primes of the
quotient branch all lie on a single path, using the effective L above,
then its odd multiplier is bounded. Finiteness without the restriction
on the arrangement of the small primes remains open. The Lean theorem
Erdos1052.SinglePath.bounded_paths checks the normalized path bound. The
translation from the subtree balances to its hypotheses is proved above.

The conclusion persists in each prescribed range of large primes.
Fix B≥2 covering the primes of c and retain only T'={q∈T:q>B}. In the
balance for T, the product of o(q+1) for q≤B has no prime divisor above
B. It therefore divides d times the product of the primes q≤B in T.
After cancellation, A∏(q∈T')o(q+1)=d'∏(q∈T')q with
d'|c∏(p≤B, p prime)p. Apply section 8 with this enlarged fixed budget.
There are effective L(B)>B and N₀(B) such that every N>N₀(B) forces two
incomparable simple primes in (B,L(B)]. For a prime multiplier ℓ these
are strictly below every prime divisor of A once 2hℓ≥L(B). In particular
the obstruction cannot reside entirely in a fixed finite collection of
small branching primes.

9. Counting simultaneous cyclotomic factors of the exponent

Fix a nonempty sparse core, an allowed h=2^v≥16, and write a=hb with
b odd. Put Y=2^h, C=oddpart(σ*(c)), g=gcd(c,C(Y+1)), K=Ω(c/g).
Here Ω counts primes with multiplicity, ω counts distinct primes, and
ν_r denotes the exponent of a prime r. Then

    ν₃(b) + ω(b/3^ν₃(b)) ≤ K.                                  (9.1)

In particular every distinct prime dividing b uses a separate copy of a
core prime, and every copy of 3 does too. If K=1, then b is 1, is 3,
or is a power of one prime different from 3.

For i|b set A_i=|Φ_i(−Y)|, with Φ_i the i-th cyclotomic polynomial.
The factorization of X^b−1 at −Y gives 2^(hb)+1=∏(i|b) A_i. The
index 1 gives Y+1. For an odd prime r and any j≥0,

    A_(r^(j+1)) (Y^(r^j)+1) = Y^(r^(j+1))+1.

Consequently any collection of these layers can be split off at once,
with the leftover factor still divisible by Y+1. This uses their distinct
polynomial indices and allows common primes in the evaluated integers.
The decreasing-prime partition of the odd-part balance in section 1 of
Erdős1052-odd-part.txt then counts at most K indices whose group demands
a nontrivial core divisor. Its first-step lemma charges A_r for every r,
and its cubic lemma charges all the layers A_(3^(j+1)). This proves (9.1).

There is a sharper bound with multiplicities as well. Choose primes
p|h+1 and q|hp+1, and let m be the multiplicative order of p modulo q.
Then

    Ω(b) ≤ max(p−1,m+1) K.                                       (9.2)

Indeed for r≠p charge every j divisible by p−1. The first index j=0
uses the first-step lemma. If j>0, then p properly divides h r^j+1 by
Fermat's theorem and h r^j+1>h+1. The composite-start lemma charges
that prime-power layer. Each charged index covers at most p−1 successive
indices in the range 0≤j<ν_r(b). For r=p charge j=0 and j=1+tm with
t≥1. In the latter case q properly divides hp^j+1; the gap from j=0
to the next charge is m+1, and subsequent gaps are m. Summing these
separate charges over all prime powers proves (9.2). If hp+1 is itself
composite one can also charge at j=1; the coefficient m+1 can then be
replaced by m. The coefficient for r=p can always be taken as 1 if p=3.

Several v give simpler coefficients. If v is odd, then h≡−1 mod 3.
For r≡1 mod 3 every j>0 charges, and for r≡2 mod 3 every even j>0
charges, since 3 properly divides h r^j+1. Include the first index
for each r and all the cubic indices. This gives

    ν₃(b) + Σ_(r≡1 mod 3) ν_r(b)
           + Σ_(r≡2 mod 3) ceil(ν_r(b)/2) ≤ K,    Ω(b) ≤ 2K.

If an odd prime t divides v, then h r^j is a t-th power whenever t|j,
so h r^j+1 is composite. These indices give Ω(b)≤tK. If v≡2 mod 4,
then for 4|j one has h r^j=4x⁴ and 4x⁴+1=(2x²−2x+1)(2x²+2x+1).
Here x>1 because h≥16. The same count gives Ω(b)≤4K.

Thus bounding the prime factors of b by L gives b≤L^H with H from
(9.2) or the smaller applicable coefficient. When K=0 the only
candidate is a=h. When K>0, (9.1) and (9.2) permit a single arbitrarily
large prime r on their own. Sections 2, 4 and 8 restrict the support
that r would force. A uniform bound on r is still needed.

10. A uniform factor of two, and a bound on distinct prime divisors

In fact for every allowed h, independently of its size,

    Ω(b) ≤ 2K,     2^ω(b) ≤ K+2.                                 (10.1)

The additional congruence needed for the first bound is this. If s≥16
is even and s≡1 mod 5, then s≡6 mod 10. Put X=2^s. In a group with
core divisor 1 for a prime-power layer starting at s, its abundancy B
satisfies X²/(X²−X+1)≤B<1+1/X. Thus every member exceeds X.
All Mersenne starts other than r=2X−1 are 2^j−1 with odd j≥s+3.
On the path from r, the next prime cannot be 2r−1 because
2r−1=2^(s+2)−3 is divisible by 11. It cannot be 4r−1 because this
integer is divisible by 3, and 6r−1 would put a factor 3 into the
group through its shift. The next prime is therefore at least 8r−1.
Using the path estimate and summing 2^(1−j) over odd j≥s+3 gives

    B ≤ [8X/(8X−5)] [3X/(3X−1)] < 1+63/(64X).

This also bounds B if the candidate start r is absent. The lower bound
is greater than 1+63/(64X) for X≥65536, a contradiction. So s≡1 mod 5
charges the layer. If s≡−1 mod 5, or s≡−1 mod 3, then s+1 is composite
and the earlier Mersenne bound charges it as well.

For v even, h≡±1 mod 5. At each prime r≠5, every even j≥0 gives
h r^j≡±1 mod 5, so at least ceil(ν_r(b)/2) layers charge. For r=5,
j=0 charges, and at every odd j one has h5^j≡−1 mod 3. These give at
least the same count. For v odd the argument modulo 3 in section 9
applies. Hence Σ_r ceil(ν_r(b)/2)≤K, which proves Ω(b)≤2K. For K=1
this restricts b to 1, one prime, or one prime squared. The cubic bound
excludes the square of 3 in this case.

The distinct-prime bound uses all squarefree divisor indices of b.
For squarefree d>1 and y=2^(−h), Möbius inversion gives

    V_d = A_d / (2^h)^φ(d) = ∏_(e|d)(1+y^e)^μ(d/e).

When ω(d) is odd, the factor at e=1 is (1+y)⁻¹. In the correction
H=(1+y)V_d, the smallest index e>1 is the least prime r|d and has
sign +1. All denominator factors have indices at least r+1. Using
∏(1+x_i)⁻¹≥1−Σx_i for x_i≥0, we obtain

    H ≥ (1+y^r)(1−y^(r+1)/(1−y)) > 1,
    H ≤ 1/(1−y³/(1−y)).

The upper bound uses that every divisor e>1 is at least 3. For
0<y≤1/64 these bounds give 1/(1+y)<V_d<1. If the core divisor for
this group were 1, its abundancy B would be 1/V_d, since 1≤B<2 and
A_d B is a power of two. Consequently

    1+(63/64)y < (1+y)(1−y³/(1−y)) ≤ B < 1+y.

Thus all its simple primes exceed 2^h. If h≡−1 mod 3 or h≡−1 mod 5,
the composite-start upper bound gives B≤2/(2−y). In the remaining
case h≡1 mod 5, the sharpened path bound above gives B<1+(63/64)y.
These alternatives cover every h=2^v≥16. Hence every squarefree
nontrivial index with an odd number of prime divisors charges.

When ω(d) is even, the e=1 factor is 1+y. Bounding all the other
factors by the same geometric sums gives

    1 < (1+y)(1−y³/(1−y)) ≤ V_d ≤ 1/(1−y/(1−y)) < 4/3.

If this group had core divisor 1, the next power of two above A_d
would give B≥2/V_d>3/2. Two disjoint such groups would have product
abundancy greater than 9/4, while the full simple support has abundancy
less than 2. Therefore at most one of the nontrivial squarefree index
groups can have core divisor 1. There are 2^ω(b)−1 nontrivial indices
and at most K groups with core divisor greater than 1. This proves the
second inequality in (10.1), including b=1.

The Lean files Erdos1052DistinctFactors.lean and Erdos1052OddResidue.lean
verify the cyclotomic allocation, the additional congruence for even v,
the bound Ω(b)≤2K for unitary perfect numbers, and b≤L^(2K) when all
prime divisors of b are ≤L. The estimate 2^ω(b)≤K+2 uses the written
Möbius product argument above.

11. Fixed sparse cores that are powers of a prime up to 10¹²

For 4096<p≤10¹² prime, e≥2, all exponents a are ruled out except
possibly at these two families of cores:

    p=65537,     8192|e,       v₂(a)=4;
    p=167772161,   32|e,       v₂(a)=23.

In particular no sparse core p² in this entire interval has a
unitary perfect completion. Here e itself may be arbitrarily large
in the exclusion of every other base. The first family also has the
additional exclusions in section 6. The second requires ν₂(e) outside
{6,8,10,15} in addition to 32|e. Neither remaining family is known
from this argument to contain a solution.

By the sparse-core theorem, the sole repeated base p is 2 mod 3 and
divides F_v=2^(2^v)+1, where v=v₂(a). Every prime other than 5 that is
2 mod 3 and divides F_v must be in the core. The simple support contains
5, and every simple prime different from 3,5 is 1 mod 3. For v=4 this
requires p=65537; all other possibilities have v≥6.

For each v from 6 through 19 at least two distinct repeated bases would
be needed in any sparse core. There are two types of certificate. For v=9,11,12,13,15,18,19
there are two coprime divisors of F_v which are both 2 mod 3. Each needs
a prime divisor 2 mod 3, and 5 does not divide F_v. The exact integers
are recorded in SPLITS in erdos1052_fermat_bases.py. Their primality is
unnecessary; gcd and modular exponentiation suffice. For example, the
two divisors for v=12 are 114689 and 26017793.

For v=6,7,8,10,14,16,17 the certificate starts with a prime r|F_v that
is 1 mod 3. Since p is 2 mod 3, r is simple. At each successive step a
prime equal to 1 mod 3 divides the previous shift, so it too is simple.
The final shift contains an odd factor d≡2 mod 3 with gcd(d,5F_v)=1.
This forces some prime that is 2 mod 3 and is neither 5 nor p, which
contradicts the sparse-core conditions. The full chains are in PATHS.
For a core with several repeated bases, at least one intermediate prime
must be repeated or a repeated prime that is 2 mod 3 must divide d.
Either way, this prime is distinct from the required core divisor of
F_v that is 2 mod 3. These same tests filter individual multi-prime cores.
For example the row for v=6 is

    274177 → 137089 → 13709.

Here 274177|2^64+1, 137089|274178, and 13709|137090. An independent
way to see the contradiction in that row is that, if all three primes
are simple, both 137090 and 13710 use the single available factor 5.
The only possible v for c=67280421310721^e would be 6 since this base
divides F₆ and its order for the base 2 is 128. This excludes every e≥2.

For larger v, ord_p(2)=2^(v+1). Hence p≡1 mod 2^(v+1). For 20≤v<40
enumerate all integers 1+k·2^(v+1)≤10¹² that are 2 mod 3 and test
2^(2^v)≡−1 modulo the integer. Only 167772161 at v=23 survives, even
without a primality filter. For v≥40 the order alone exceeds the bound.

For p=167772161, put j=ν₂(e). If j=0, 3|p^e+1, while 5+1 already uses
the single available copy of 3. For j=1 the prime r=14073749003304961
equals (p²+1)/2. It is simple, (r+1)/2=7036874501652481 is also prime
and simple, and its shift is divisible by 911≡2 mod 3. If j=2,3,4 the
respective forced primes 41,17,1601 are themselves 2 mod 3. This proves
32|e. The remaining exclusions use the factors 257,26113,59393,65537
at j=6,8,10,15; for j=8, 11 divides 26114. In all rows the initial
divisor of p^(2^j)+1 divides p^e+1 because e/2^j is odd.

12. A bounded gap between Mersenne prime exponents is necessary for a free higher layer

Fix h=2^v≥16. Let s=hu with u≥3 odd and take a multiplier f≥3 odd.
If the group for A=(2^(sf)+1)/(2^s+1) uses no core prime, then there
are two Mersenne primes 2^(s+1)−1 and 2^(s+j)−1 with 3≤j≤2h+1.
Both belong to the group. In particular, when K=1, a putative b=ℓ²
requires these two Mersenne primes with s=hℓ. This restriction applies
to an individual large prime ℓ in the square case.

Put Y=2^s and Q_i=1+2^i(Y−1), so Q₁=2Y−1. The neutral-size lemma
requires Y²/(Y²−Y+1)≤B<1+1/Y for the assigned abundancy B, and all
the group's primes exceed Y. The Mersenne-start estimate forces Q₁
to be prime and present: if absent, every start exponent is at least
s+3 and the entire abundancy is at most 2Y/(2Y−1). Let k=2h−1.
Then 2^h+1 properly divides Q_k since Y≡−1 mod 2^h+1 and
2^(2h)≡1 mod 2^h+1. Thus the path from Q₁ cannot follow the minimal
chain Q₂,Q₃,...,Q_k all the way through.

At its first departure, say at index i≤k, the path ends or its next
prime is at least 4Q_(i−1)−1=2Q_i+1. Write w(q)=(q+1)/(q−1).
The minimal steps telescope because (1+1/q)w(2q−1)=w(q), and
w(2Q_i+1)/w(Q_i)=1−1/Q_i. Hence this entire path contributes at most

    w(Q₁)(1−1/Q_k) < [Y/(Y−1)](1−δ),       δ=1/(2^k Y).

Suppose all other starting exponents were ≥s+k+3. Their reciprocal
losses sum to at most Σ_(j≥s+k+3)2^(1−j)=δ/2, so their path product
is at most 1/(1−δ/2). This would give

    B < [Y/(Y−1)] (1−δ)/(1−δ/2)
      ≤ [Y/(Y−1)] (1−δ/2) < Y²/(Y²−Y+1).

The last inequality is equivalent to δ/2>1/(Y²−Y+1) and follows from
s≥3h>k+1. This is the contradiction. At K=1 the first index ℓ has
already used the only available core prime, so the second ℓ-power
layer must meet this obstruction. Even the first necessary exponent
hℓ+1 must be prime. Thus the set of possible prime ℓ in the square
case has relative density zero amongst primes, by the prime number
theorem in arithmetic progressions. Indeed sieve hℓ+1 by any finite
set of primes r>2 not dividing h: it rules out one of the r−1 nonzero
residue classes of ℓ for each r, apart from finitely many exceptions.
The resulting density bound ∏(1−1/(r−1)) tends to zero. In the
single-prime case b=ℓ the first layer can use the core, so this last
restriction leaves it open.

13. Every prime-power layer uses part of the core

With h=2^v≥16, a=hb, b odd, and K=Ω(c/gcd(c,oddpart(σ*(c))(2^h+1))),
the stronger bound is

    Ω(b) ≤ K.                                                      (13.1)

Thus b≤L^K if every prime divisor of b is at most L. At K=1 the
only possibilities are 1 and an odd prime. In particular every square
of a prime is excluded in that case, for primes of arbitrary size.
This argument still permits a single large prime when K≥1.

Here is a proof. In the terminology of sections 9 and 10, take an odd
multiplier f≥3 and an exponent s divisible by 16, with 3∤s. Suppose a
group for (2^(sf)+1)/(2^s+1) has core divisor 1. Put Y=2^s. Its simple
primes all exceed Y. Their abundancy B satisfies

    Y²/(Y²−Y+1) ≤ B < 1+1/Y.

The sum of their shift valuations v₂(q+1) is (f−1)s. Write w(q)=(q+1)/(q−1).
Partition the group into paths as in section 2 of Erdős1052-odd-part.txt.
Each path starts at a Mersenne prime, each edge r→q satisfies r|q+1,
and the full path contributes at most w(r). A vertex with oddpart(q+1)=r
has exactly that predecessor. These facts also cover paths with one vertex.

In fact all three Mersenne primes 2^(s+i)−1 for i=1,3,7 have to occur.
For an odd lower endpoint i, the total reciprocal loss 2/(q+1) from
all possible Mersenne starts at exponents at least s+i is at most
(4/3)·2^(1−i)/Y, by summing over the odd exponents. If a family of
paths has starts with total loss at most t<1, its abundancy is at most
1/(1−t).

The first start r=2Y−1 is forced by section 2 of the odd-part note.
Since s+1 must consequently be prime, 3∤s implies s≡1 mod 3. The
assumption 16|s gives s≡4 mod 12. Write Q_i=1+2^i(Y−1).
Since 2^s≡3 mod 13, Q₅ is divisible by 13 and exceeds 13.

If the start t=8Y−1 is absent, s+5 cannot supply a start either since
3|s+5. Thus the other starts have total loss at most 1/(48Y). On the
path from r=Q₁, at the first missing minimal step Q_i with i≤5, the
next prime is at least 2Q_i+1 or the path ends. Minimal steps telescope
and w(2Q_i+1)/w(Q_i)=1−1/Q_i. This bounds the path by
w(r)(1−1/Q₅). It follows that

    B ≤ [Y/(Y−1)] (1−1/(32Y))/(1−1/(48Y))
      < [Y/(Y−1)] (1−1/(96Y)) < Y²/(Y²−Y+1).

The last inequality uses Y²−Y+1>96Y. This contradiction forces t.

After a prime x≡1 mod 3 on these paths, if 2x−1 is absent, the next
prime is at least 8x−1. Indeed 4x−1 is composite and 6x−1 would
put 3 into the group. If 2x−1 is absent and 8x−1 is absent as well,
the next prime is at least 16x−1: the remaining even multipliers
10,12,14 bring in one of 3,5,7, all smaller than Y. Along either
path one may also stop, which only lowers the product.

The prime u=4Y−3 must follow r. Otherwise the path contributes at
most (1+1/r)w(8r−1)=8Y/(8Y−5); all other starts have loss ≤1/(3Y).
The combined reciprocal loss is at most (5/8+1/3)/Y=23/(24Y),
giving B≤1/(1−23/(24Y))<Y²/(Y²−Y+1). The two gains from r,u
and the gain from t also show 8Y−7 cannot be in the group. These
four distinct vertices would have reciprocal sum greater than
(1/2+1/4+1/8+1/8)/Y=1/Y, contrary to B<1+1/Y.

The prime z=16Y−3 must likewise follow t. Otherwise the first path
is bounded by (1+1/r)(1+1/u)w(8u−1)=16Y/(16Y−13), since 2u−1
is absent. The second path is bounded by (1+1/t)w(8t−1)=32Y/(32Y−5).
All other starts have loss at most 1/(48Y). Adding the losses gives
(13/16+5/32+1/48)/Y=95/(96Y), again too little because Y>96.

Now suppose 2^(s+7)−1 is absent. Other starts then have loss ≤1/(192Y).
On the second path, 2z−1=32Y−7 is divisible by 5 because 4|s.
The path from t therefore contributes at most

    (1+1/t)(1+1/z)w(8z−1)=64Y/(64Y−13).

On the first path put x=8u−1=32Y−25. If x occurs, its own immediate
successor 2x−1=64Y−51 is divisible by 7, since s≡1 mod 3 gives
Y≡2 mod 7. The next prime is at least 8x−1. If x is absent, the next
prime after u is at least 16u−1=2x+1, and w(2x+1)=1+1/x. Thus the
same upper bound works in both cases:

    (1+1/r)(1+1/u)(1+1/x)w(8x−1)=128Y/(128Y−101).

The first two paths and the other starts together give

    B ≤ 1/(1−(101/128+13/64+1/192)/Y)
      = 1/(1−383/(384Y)) < Y²/(Y²−Y+1),

since Y>384. This forces the third Mersenne prime.

If 5∤s these requirements are impossible. For s≡4,2,3 modulo 5
respectively, one of s+1,s+3,s+7 is a multiple of 5 exceeding 5.
For s≡1 modulo 5 one has s≡6 modulo 10 and 11 divides the forced
prime u=2^(s+2)−3. This deals with every multiplier when gcd(s,15)=1.

The multiplier f=5 is impossible even when 5|s. Its total shift
valuation is 4s. The three forced Mersenne primes have shift
valuations s+1,s+3,s+7. Any fourth one would take the sum past 4s.
Thus these three are all the starts, and the shift valuations of
all the other vertices sum to s−11.

To see the contradiction, treat Y as the value of an indeterminate X.
Start the three vertices with polynomials 2X−1,8X−1,128X−1. For
each other vertex q, its odd shift part is the product of smaller
vertices r, each available only once by the squarefree balance.
Replace all these polynomials by

    P_q(X)=2^j ∏_r P_r(X)−1,        j=v₂(q+1)≥1.

Processing the vertices by increasing size, after putting in the three
starts, is legitimate since every dependency is smaller than the vertex.
At each step P_q(Y)=q. The product F of the final polynomials therefore
satisfies F(Y)=(Y^5+1)/(Y+1)=Y⁴−Y³+Y²−Y+1, by the group balance.
In particular F(0)≡1 mod Y.

All current constant terms are nonzero odd integers. Track the product
of |z|+1 over these terms. It starts at 8. At a replacement it grows
by a factor of at most 2^j because

    |2^j∏z−1|+1 ≤ 2^j∏|z|+2 ≤ 2^j∏(|z|+1).

The last inequality uses at least one predecessor and j≥1. Hence
|F(0)|≤8·2^(s−11)=Y/256. On the other hand the product of |z| can
never decrease, since |2^j m−1|≥|m| for every nonzero integer m.
The forced vertex u has only r as predecessor, with j=1, so its
constant term is −3. This gives |F(0)|≥3. The congruence F(0)≡1 mod Y
and the absolute upper bound force F(0)=1, a contradiction.

Finally, for each odd prime p|b and each 0≤j<ν_p(b), take s=h p^j
and f=p. For p outside {3,5} one has gcd(s,15)=1. For p=5 use the
quintic argument, and for p=3 use the cubic obstruction in section 3
of the odd-part note. Thus every one of the Ω(b) distinct cyclotomic
indices p^(j+1) needs a nontrivial core divisor. The simultaneous
allocation of section 9 proves (13.1).

LayerConstants.lean checks the rational loss comparison and the
integer-product argument for the quintic case. The formation of its
frontier from the group and the three forced Mersenne primes use the
written path argument above. The earlier formal proof of Ω(b)≤2K is
in SparseOddExponent.lean.

There is also a divisor-count refinement. Put i=max(ν₃(b)−1,0),
j=max(ν₅(b)−1,0), and b₀=b/(3^i 5^j). With τ the number of positive
divisors,

    τ(b₀)+i+j ≤ K+2.

For d|b₀, d>1, write d=u m with m the product of its distinct prime
divisors. Then gcd(u,15)=1. The cyclotomic identity Φ_d(X)=Φ_m(X^u)
follows from the orders of the roots, since all primes of u divide m.
Apply the estimates of section 10 with s=hu and y=2^(−s). When ω(m)
is odd, a group using no core would have

    1+(511/512)y < (1+y)(1−y³/(1−y)) ≤ B < 1+y.

Here y≤1/65536. This lower bound suffices throughout the three-start
proof: each 1/(1−k y) with k≤383/384 is below 1+(511/512)y, and the
other upper bound (1−y/96)/(1−y) is also below it. Since gcd(s,15)=1,
the modulo 5 contradictions still apply. Thus every such index charges.
When ω(m) is even, the section 10 argument gives B>3/2 for a group
using no core, so at most one such index can be free. Finally the i+j
prime powers of 3 and 5 excluded from the divisors of b₀ all charge by
(13.1)'s proof. Counting these disjoint indices gives the refinement.

14. A size bound on each shifted prime in the single-prime case

Take an odd prime ℓ|b, N=hℓ, d₀=2^h+1 and A=(2^N+1)/d₀. Every simple
prime q in the group assigned to A satisfies

    (q−1)(2^v₂(q+1)−1) < 2(2^N+1).

In particular, for a Mersenne prime q=2^t−1 in this group one has
t≤N/2. This holds for all its descendants, including when a shifted
factor has several large prime divisors.

Indeed, follow q upwards to a prime p|A. Every simple prime used in a
shift has only one such successor. The primes strictly increase on the
path, so it ends at a prime of A by the group balance. The endpoint
satisfies p=Lq−u for positive L and 0≤u<L. Start with L=1 and u=0.
At a successor of shape kx−1, with integer k≥2, the new pair is
(kL,ku+1) and retains the same inequalities. Put A=Hp and t=v₂(q+1).
Since q≤p≤A<2^N, one has t≤N. Reducing
2^N+1=d₀H(Lq−u) modulo 2^t shows

    2^t | d₀H(L+u)+1.

The right side is positive. Thus 2^t−1≤d₀H(L+u)<2d₀HL, while
L(q−1)<p. This gives the claimed bound. For a Mersenne prime it becomes
q(q−1)<2(2^N+1). If t≥N/2+1, the left side is at least
(2·2^(N/2)−1)(2·2^(N/2)−2), greater than the right side since
2^(N/2)>3. This proves t≤N/2. The estimate bounds each such prime
in terms of ℓ; a bound independent of ℓ is still needed for finiteness.
