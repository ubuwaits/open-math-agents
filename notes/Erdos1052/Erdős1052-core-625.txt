The unitary perfect numbers with repeated odd core 625

Theorem. Suppose n is unitary perfect and its only repeated odd prime
power is 5^4. Then n = 146361946186458562560000. In particular there
is no such number with v₂(n)=2ℓ for any prime ℓ.

The proof below excludes every odd ℓ≥25 without assuming ℓ is prime.
Section 4 covers the other exponents. Section 5 gives a restriction
on general cores that can have any number of repeated prime factors.

The Lean theorem Erdos1052.Core625.no_prime_double proves the prime
case from the definition of a unitary perfect number. The theorem
no_large_odd_double covers every odd ℓ≥25, and no_prime_double_test
applies directly to the arithmetic test in R2.H2. The four finite
inequalities are checked by the kernel. The full classification also
uses the exact arithmetic checks in section 4. Section 5 uses the
paper argument and the exact arithmetic in erdos1052_625.py.
The single-file Lean proof, checked against the accepted R2 revision, is at
https://github.com/ubuwaits/open-math-agents/blob/97cd73bdcb6daca2c349223dcbc96c22963be2ea/notes/Erdos1052/PrimeDouble625.lean
Its exported exclusion theorems use only propext, Classical.choice and
Quot.sound. The H2 problem desk records the result in note 29756.

1. Divide the simple primes into two groups

Write n=2^a·625·∏(q∈S)q with distinct odd primes q≠5. The unitary
divisor sum of any prime power p^e is 1+p^e. Hence 625+1=2·313,
313+1=2·157, 157+1=2·79 and 79+1=16·5 force 313,157,79 into S.
Let R be S with these three primes removed and o(t) the odd part of t.
After cancelling the forced factors, take odd parts and abundancies.
For positive a the two resulting identities are

  (2^a+1) ∏(q∈R)o(q+1) = 125 ∏(q∈R)q,
  ∏(q∈R)(1+1/q) = (125/64)/(1+2^(−a)) < 125/64.                  (1)

Now take a=2ℓ, ℓ=2k+1, k≥12, and put X=2^k and L=2^ℓ=2X².
The integer factors

  A₊ = L+2X+1,       A₋ = L−2X+1

have product L²+1. Both are odd, and their difference is 4X, so they
are coprime. Also 5 divides L²+1 since ℓ is odd.

Here is a constructive way to split R. Start with the two odd integers
A₊ and A₋ and process R in decreasing prime order. The current largest
prime q divides one of the two running integers. Indeed their product
times the unprocessed o(r+1)'s equals 125 times the unprocessed primes.
No remaining o(r+1) can supply q because o(r+1)<q whenever r≤q and
r is an odd prime. Assign q to the side it divides and replace that
running integer H by (H/q)o(q+1). This preserves the identity. At the
end the two integers have product 125, so they are 5^j₊ and 5^j₋ with
j₊+j₋=3. Denote the two disjoint prime sets by R₊,R₋. We obtain

  Aᵢ ∏(q∈Rᵢ)o(q+1) = 5^jᵢ ∏(q∈Rᵢ)q.                               (2)

Put Eᵢ=Σ(q∈Rᵢ)v₂(q+1) and Bᵢ=∏(q∈Rᵢ)(1+1/q). Then

  Bᵢ = 2^Eᵢ 5^jᵢ/Aᵢ = (5/4)^jᵢ · 2^(Eᵢ+2jᵢ−ℓ) · L/Aᵢ.              (3)

Each Bᵢ is at least 1 and strictly below 125/64 by (1).
Since X≥4096, both L/Aᵢ lie strictly between 1−1/2048 and 1+1/2048.
For instance L/A₋−1=(2X−1)/(2X²−2X+1)<1/(X−1), and
1−L/A₊=(2X+1)/(2X²+2X+1)<1/X. An exponent of at least one on the
middle power of two in (3) would put Bᵢ above 125/64. An exponent of
at most minus one would put it below 1 even at jᵢ=3. Consequently

  Eᵢ+2jᵢ=ℓ,                Bᵢ=(5/4)^jᵢ L/Aᵢ.                         (4)

In particular j₊≠0, since L/A₊<1.

2. Four finite inequalities, valid for unbounded primes

For j=1,2 let Iⱼ be the half-open interval with endpoints
(5/4)^j(1−1/2048) and (5/4)^j(1+1/2048). Consider any finite set T
of distinct odd primes disjoint from {5,79,157,313} satisfying

  ∏(q∈T)o(q+1) | 5^h ∏(q∈T)q.

The following four rows exclude ∏(q∈T)(1+1/q) from Iⱼ provided the
indicated further primes are absent from T.

   h    j    further primes absent    nodes checked    largest cutoff
   0    1            3                      10                 598
   1    1            3,7                     1                  14
   1    1            3,31                   17                2119
   1    2            7,31                   23                1229

The arithmetic proof of the table is reproducible with erdos1052_625.py.
For clarity, here is the bound and the full enumeration rule. With
current integer D and all remaining primes greater than x≥1, set

  U(x,D)=(1+2/x)^Ω(D) (2048/2047)
         · ∏(p∈{3,7,31,127}, p>x) (p+1)/(p−1).

This bounds every remaining abundancy whenever ∏o(q+1) divides D∏q.
To see this, extend prime weights g(p)=1+2/x for p≤x and
g(p)=p/(p−1) for p>x completely multiplicatively. If d=o(q+1)>1,
choose p|d. From q≥x+1 and q≥2p−1 one obtains
g(d)≥g(p)≥(q+1)/(q−1)=(1+1/q)g(q). When d=1, include the factor
(q+1)/(q−1) separately. Multiply over q, use the divisibility and
cancel ∏g(q). Each g(p)≤1+2/x. The separate factors have q=2^t−1.
For t<13 the only such odd primes are 3,7,31,127 by trial division.
For t≥13 the sum of 2^(1−t) is 1/2048, so the product of any finite
subset of 1/(1−2^(1−t)) is at most 2048/2047. This proves the bound.

Start at x=1,D=5^h,B=1. A node with B at least the upper endpoint of
Iⱼ is finished. Otherwise find the smallest y from x through 4096
such that BU(y,D) is below the lower endpoint. This deals with every
continuation whose primes all exceed y, including the empty one.
For the remaining possibilities test every allowed prime q∈(x,y].
If q is the next prime, all factors of o(q+1) are smaller than q,
so o(q+1) must divide D. In exactly these cases recurse with
x=q, D replaced by Dq/o(q+1) and B replaced by B(1+1/q).
The four searches terminate with the counts above. All comparisons
are exact. The largest cutoff in a row only bounds explicit trial
division in the proof. U also covers primes beyond that cutoff.

We now use the table. If one group has j=1, (4) puts its abundancy
in I₁, which is below 4/3, so it cannot contain 3. If 5 divides its
Aᵢ, cancel 5 in (2) and use the first row with h=0. Otherwise the
group satisfies the condition with h=1, and the next two rows force
both 7 and 31 into it. The other group then has j=2. Its Aᵢ is
divisible by 5, so it has allowance h=1 after cancellation in (2).
It is disjoint from 7 and 31, and the fourth row excludes I₂.
Thus the two j's cannot be 1 and 2. Since j₊≠0, only (3,0) remains.

3. The group with j₋=0 is impossible

Write T=R₋ and A=A₋=2X²−2X+1. Equations (2) and (4) give

  A ∏(q∈T)o(q+1) = ∏(q∈T)q,        ∏(q∈T)(1+1/q)=2X²/A.            (5)

T is nonempty. Each q∈T satisfies 1+1/q≤2X²/A, hence
q≥A/(2X−1)>X−1. Its least prime r has o(r+1)=1, since (5) would
otherwise supply a smaller prime in T. Thus r+1 is a power of two
strictly greater than X, giving r≥2X−1.

Every prime divisor of A belongs to T. Since 1<A<(2X−1)², A itself
must be prime and must belong to T. More generally, every divisor of
∏(q∈T)q strictly between 1 and (2X−1)² is a prime in T.

Set Qᵢ=1+2^i(X−1). We have Qₖ₊₁=A. For i≥2, o(Qᵢ+1)=Qᵢ₋₁.
Whenever Qᵢ∈T, (5) implies this odd part divides ∏(q∈T)q. It is
strictly between 1 and (2X−1)². Descending induction forces every
Q₁,...,Qₖ₊₁ to be prime.

To contradict this, write k=u d with u the largest power of two
dividing k and d odd. If d≥3, reduce modulo N=2^u+1. Then X=2^k≡−1
and 2^(2u)≡1 mod N. Therefore Q₂ᵤ₋₁≡1−2^(2u)≡0 mod N. The index
2u−1 is between 1 and k+1, and 1<N<Q₂ᵤ₋₁.

If d=1, write k=2^m. For m odd we have k≡2 mod 3, and 7 divides
Q₁=2^(k+1)−1. For m even, k≥12 implies m≥2, so k≡4 mod 12.
Then 2^k≡3 mod 13 and Qₖ₊₁=1+2·2^k(2^k−1)≡0 mod 13. In each case
the displayed divisor is proper. This completes the unbounded exclusion.

4. The full classification

The core-625 argument in section 3 of Erdős1052-core-bounds.txt rules
out all odd exponents and all multiples of four. Its exact checks are
also run by erdos1052_625.py. Every remaining exponent is twice an odd
integer. Sections 1–3 exclude these as soon as ℓ≥25. For 0≤a≤49,
the exact descending prime-factor test from (1), or equivalently
the original unitary balance, returns only a=18. It gives

  n=2^18 · 5^4 · 3·7·11·13·19·37·79·109·157·313.

Direct multiplication of its unitary divisor factors gives 2n.
Charles R. Wall's paper on the fifth unitary perfect number verifies
this known example. The classification here fixes its repeated odd core.
https://doi.org/10.4153/CMB-1975-021-9

5. Progress on the bound for general cores

Let P be the nonempty set of repeated odd prime bases in c. Suppose
s=Σ(p∈P)1/p < 1/4096. This hypothesis allows any number of bases
and unbounded exponents. Any unitary perfect completion satisfies
either 64|a, or v₂(a)=4 and 65537∈P. In both cases every prime equal
to 2 mod 3 dividing 2^a+1 belongs to P. Every member of P equal to
2 mod 3 occurs to an even exponent in c. In particular, if 65537∉P
then 64|a. This holds, for example, whenever s<1/65537.

Here are the estimates extending the single-base argument. Every p in P
is greater than 4096. Suppose S is a set of simple primes outside P with
simple abundancy below 2, and that m ∏o(q+1) divides c ∏q for a seed m
chosen from 3,5,17,257,641,65537, with m∉P. Draw an edge from r∈S to q∈S whenever
r|q+1. Each vertex can supply at most one edge. Remove all q for which
some p∈P divides q+1, together with all their successors. No prime of
m is removed. For m≤641 every deleted vertex exceeds m. For m=65537
the only odd primes dividing m+1=65538 are 3,11,331. Thus m cannot
start a deleted path and none of its predecessors can be deleted.
In the remaining set G, prime-by-prime cancellation gives
m ∏(q∈G)o(q+1) | ∏(q∈G)q.

Along an edge r→q, q≥2r−1. Since (1+1/r)w(2r−1)=w(r) for
w(r)=(r+1)/(r−1), each path starting at r contributes at most w(r).
It suffices to bound Σ 2/(q+1) over the starting vertices, even when
paths overlap. For the starts whose odd part uses only primes in P,
sum over 2^j ∏p^i with j≥1 and at least one i positive. The result
is at most 2(∏(1−1/p)⁻¹−1)≤2s/(1−s). At any other start, choose
a simple prime r and a core prime p dividing q+1. Different starts
use different r, and 2/(q+1)≤1/(pr). Since Σ(r∈S)1/r<1, the mixed
starts contribute at most s. Consequently the deleted abundancy is
at most 1/(1−3/4095). The core contributes at most 1/(1−s²), since
its local factors are 1+p^(−e) with e≥2 and Σ p^(−e)≤s².

Put E=1/((1−1/4096²)(1−3/4095)). The retained G obeys the core-1
tests in section 4 of Erdős1052-core-bounds.txt, with total correction
at most E. The script rechecks each rational interval at this new E.
For 0<a<20 it uses the exact required abundancy 2/(1+2^(−a)) with
seed Fᵥ=2^(2^v)+1, where v=v₂(a).
For a≥20 and v₂(a)≤5 use m=3,5,17,257,65537,641 respectively.
These cases are excluded unless the seed is 65537 and belongs to P.
The original reduction handles a=0, leaving only the two stated cases.

For 64|a, any prime r≡2 mod 3 dividing 2^a+1 exceeds 1024. For
v₂(a)=4 the sole possibility at or below 1024 is 449. Both statements
follow by taking the exponent modulo r−1 for each prime r≤1024.
The prime 449 would be simple, and 9|450 would require 9|c ∏q,
which is impossible as 3∉P. If r>1024 and r∉P, it too must be
simple. Remove r and use r|2^a+1 to cancel r on the right of the
odd-part balance. This leaves the seed 3 from r+1. The extra factor
1+1/1024 still gives (1024/513)E(1+1/1024)(1+2^(−16))<2.
Thus all such r, including those dividing Fᵥ, belong to P. If a member
p≡2 mod 3 of P had odd exponent e, then 3|p^e+1 would give the same
contradiction at seed 3 directly.

Finally Fᵥ≡2 mod 3, so at least one of its prime factors p is 2 mod 3.
For such a p the multiplicative order of 2 modulo p is exactly 2^(v+1).
Hence a fixed P satisfying the reciprocal condition gives at most |P|
possible values of v₂(a), determined by the orders of 2 modulo its
members. If none of the repeated primes is 2 mod 3 there is no
completion at all. A uniform bound on c remains open.
